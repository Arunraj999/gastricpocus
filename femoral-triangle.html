Femoral triangle block

Local anaesthetic placed beneath sartorius, beside the femoral artery, in the lower part of the femoral triangle, before the vessels enter the adductor canal. It reaches the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis together, and the medial femoral cutaneous nerve when given high enough. Many trials of the so-called adductor canal block were in fact done at this level: the midpoint of the thigh lay a mean 4.6 cm above the start of the canal in every one of 22 volunteers.1 The 2024 ASRA–ESRA nomenclature names a block by its nerve and its anatomical site, which is why this page treats it separately from the adductor canal block.2

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The needle work is simple: a superficial target in a plane beneath sartorius, beside an artery that is easy to see. The difficulty lies in knowing where you are. The block is defined by one landmark on the scan, the point where the medial border of sartorius meets the medial border of adductor longus, the apex of the femoral triangle; above it you are in the triangle, below it in the adductor canal.1 Surface measurements do not find that point reliably, and the trial literature shows the cost of getting it wrong. In a review of 130 trials of the adductor canal block, only 29 used a nomenclature that matched the anatomy, and an injection halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella, used in 27 of them, was reclassified as a femoral triangle block.6

Anatomy

The space. The femoral triangle is bounded by the inguinal ligament above, the medial border of sartorius laterally and the medial border of adductor longus medially. Its floor is iliopsoas laterally and pectineus and adductor longus medially. The femoral vessels and nerve run through its upper part. Its apex, where the medial borders of sartorius and adductor longus meet, is the entrance to the adductor canal, a tunnel roofed by the vastoadductor membrane that runs on to the adductor hiatus.1

The three nerves for the knee. The saphenous nerve, the nerve to vastus medialis and the medial femoral cutaneous nerve leave the femoral nerve high in the triangle and run through almost its whole length to the apex.1 Beyond the apex their paths separate. The saphenous nerve is consistently found in the canal. The medial femoral cutaneous nerve joins the subsartorial plexus outside the canal, and the nerve to vastus medialis enters its own fascial sheath just above the canal.1 This is the anatomical case for injecting in the lower triangle, where all three still lie together.

What each nerve brings to the knee. In 20 cadaveric limbs the nerve to vastus medialis contributed substantially to the knee capsule, through intramuscular, extramuscular and deep genicular branches; the saphenous nerve made a more modest contribution through its infrapatellar and posterior branches.12 Mapping the anterior capsule in 15 knees, the superomedial quadrant was supplied by the nerve to vastus medialis, the nerve to vastus intermedius and the superior medial genicular nerve; the inferomedial quadrant by the inferior medial genicular nerve, with the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve in 3 knees. The lateral quadrants were supplied mainly by the nerve to vastus lateralis, the lateral genicular nerves and branches of the common fibular nerve, which lie outside this block.13

Where the injectate goes. In four paired cadavers, 20 mL of dye in the distal femoral triangle stained both the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis in every specimen; the same volume in the distal adductor canal stained only the saphenous nerve. The authors cautioned that the higher injection may also reach the nerve to vastus intermedius, a motor nerve.11 Spread in the other direction is limited: 10 mL of dye injected in the distal femoral triangle did not reach the popliteal fossa in any of three cadaver sides, whereas the same volume in the distal canal stained the popliteal plexus in all ten.14 On CT after 36 cadaveric injections of 2 or 30 mL in the distal triangle or the distal canal, contrast always reached the saphenous nerve and never the femoral nerve trunk.15

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg slightly abducted and externally rotated and the knee a little flexed.610 External rotation brings the saphenous nerve lateral to the artery and shortens the needle path.6 The probe sits across the anteromedial thigh, over the sartorius, in the middle third of the thigh. Find the level on the scan, not with a tape measure: the apex of the triangle was a mean 27.4 cm (range 24.0 to 31.4) below the anterior superior iliac spine in 22 volunteers, and the thigh midpoint 22.9 cm.1

Indications

  • Total knee arthroplasty, as part of multimodal analgesia. A femoral triangle block alone was followed by a median 20 mg of intravenous morphine in the first 24 hours, against 2 mg when an obturator nerve block was added.3 Procedure-specific guidance recommends a single-shot adductor canal block with local infiltration analgesia;16 the femoral triangle block is the more proximal alternative described on this page, and international consensus supports peripheral nerve block for knee arthroplasty unless contraindicated.17
  • Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. An injection with the femoral artery medial to sartorius, which a later review classifies as a femoral triangle block, reduced 24-hour opioid use against injections further down the thigh.46 Combined with a posterior capsule block it reduced morphine use against surgical infiltration.5 The evidence is not uniform: a meta-analysis of trials labelled adductor canal block, done at mixed levels, found no analgesic benefit over control after ligament reconstruction.18
  • Other knee surgery where quadriceps power matters, as a more motor-sparing alternative to the femoral nerve block.10
  • Anteromedial leg, ankle and foot surgery in the saphenous territory, with a sciatic block. A block lower down the thigh does the same job with less risk to the quadriceps.19

Nerves blocked

The block is aimed at the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis, and in a proximal injection the medial femoral cutaneous nerve as well.622 The saphenous nerve is sensory to the medial leg and, through its infrapatellar branch, to part of the anteromedial knee.13 The nerve to vastus medialis is a motor nerve that also supplies the superomedial capsule.1213 The medial and intermediate femoral cutaneous nerves supply the skin over the front of the thigh and knee, where the midline incision lies. A distal femoral triangle injection left a gap at the anteromedial knee in 17 of 20 volunteers, against 2 of 20 after a proximal one.22 The intermediate femoral cutaneous nerve needs its own injection, superficial to sartorius.229

The obturator nerve, the posterior capsule and the lateral side of the knee are outside this block.1314

What the block covers

The anteromedial knee and the medial leg, without the posterior or lateral knee.

Landmark technique

There is no reliable landmark technique. The block is defined by a sonographic point, and the surface proxy most often used, the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella, lay above the adductor canal in all 22 volunteers, by a mean 4.6 cm (range 2.3 to 7.0).1 Any injection at the thigh midpoint is therefore likely to be a femoral triangle block, whatever it is called. Do this block with ultrasound.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Place a high-frequency linear probe across the anteromedial thigh and find the femoral artery beneath sartorius. Confirm it with colour Doppler or by compression.6
  2. Slide distally to the apex. Follow the artery until the medial border of sartorius meets the medial border of adductor longus. This is the start of the adductor canal.1
  3. Come back up 1 to 2 cm. The artery is again seen in short axis, beneath the medial part of sartorius, still in the triangle.79
  4. Name the nerves. The saphenous nerve is the round bright structure lateral to the artery; the nerve to vastus medialis lies further lateral, between sartorius and vastus medialis.7
  5. Advance in plane from lateral to medial, through or beneath sartorius, to a point beneath sartorius and anterolateral to the artery.69
  6. Inject in small aliquots, aspirating and watching the spread surround the saphenous nerve and reach towards the nerve to vastus medialis. Use hydrodissection to open the plane.710
  7. For a midline knee incision, consider a separate intermediate femoral cutaneous injection superficial to sartorius.229

Cadaver

Triangle or canal

Twenty millilitres in the distal triangle stained the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis in all four specimens; in the distal canal only the saphenous nerve.11 A 10 mL injection at the proximal end of the canal stained the saphenous nerve and the posteromedial branch of the nerve to vastus medialis in all seven specimens, with little or no spread back into the triangle.24

RCT

Level changes analgesia

In 108 patients having anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, the injection with the artery medial to sartorius cut 24-hour oral morphine equivalents to 34.3 mg, against 64.0 and 65.7 mg for the mid and distal injections further down the thigh. Quadriceps strength fell by a similar amount in all three groups: 16.7%, 13.4% and 15.3%.4 A later review classifies that injection site as a femoral triangle block.6

Volunteer RCT

Proximal or distal in the triangle

Forty volunteers had an active proximal triangle injection on one leg and an active distal one on the other, and an active intermediate femoral cutaneous block on one randomly chosen side. With the cutaneous block and the proximal injection, 15 of 20 had the whole incision line anaesthetised. With the cutaneous block in place, a gap at the anteromedial knee remained in 2 of 20 after the proximal injection against 17 of 20 after the distal one.22

RCT

Adding the cutaneous nerves

In 80 patients having knee arthroplasty, all of whom also had an IPACK block and an adductor canal catheter, adding an anterior femoral cutaneous nerve block to a femoral triangle block lowered pain on movement at 12 hours (mean difference −2.02 on a 0 to 10 scale), reduced incision site pain and 48-hour morphine use, and was associated with better quadriceps strength immediately after surgery.9

Needle path and nerve stimulator

The needle passes through skin, subcutaneous tissue and fascia lata, then through or beneath sartorius into the plane that holds the artery and the nerves. The femoral vein lies close to the artery, and the target is anterolateral to the artery, so keep the tip on that side and in view. A nerve stimulator is not required; if it is used, a twitch of vastus medialis means the tip is at the nerve to vastus medialis, a motor nerve, and the needle should be withdrawn slightly before injecting. These are standard descriptions rather than findings of a specific study.

Systematic review

The vessels

Because the femoral vessels are so close, intravascular injection is the main procedural concern in both femoral triangle and adductor canal blocks; ultrasound guidance and aspiration reduce the risk.6

Cadaver CT

The femoral nerve trunk

After 36 injections of 2 or 30 mL in the distal triangle or distal canal, contrast reached the saphenous nerve every time, never the femoral nerve itself and never the sciatic nerve; it reached the popliteal fossa in three.15

Block performance

Volumes and drugs studied in the trials cited here: 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.5%;7 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.2%;8 20 mL of a levobupivacaine mixture with adrenaline and ketorolac;9 and 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% through the needle followed by 10 mL through a catheter.10 These are the regimens of individual trials, not a recommendation. Whatever volume you choose, calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic from lean body weight, as the ASRA guidance advises,25 and add any infiltration the surgeon gives, which in knee arthroplasty trials has been 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or 200 mg of ropivacaine.2610 Dose per weight, total dose and patient weight all predicted toxicity in a registry of 25,336 blocks.27

Continuous techniques

Many catheter trials called “proximal adductor canal” were placed at a level that a later review reclassifies as the femoral triangle, including those placed halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella or where the artery lay under the medial third of sartorius.6 Read them with that in mind.

  • Against a catheter lower down the thigh. In 73 patients after knee arthroplasty, the proximal catheter was non-inferior for 24-hour intravenous morphine equivalents (difference −7.2 mg).28 In 50 patients with surgical infiltration, a catheter at the thigh midpoint gave lower pain the morning after surgery (median 0.5 against 3.0), with no difference in opioid use or walking distance.26 In 95 patients, continuous femoral triangle, proximal canal and distal canal catheters gave similar pain on the first morning, and quadriceps strength and opioid use did not differ.29 A meta-analysis of six trials and 352 patients found no difference between proximal and distal catheters, but the required information size was not reached.30
  • Against a femoral nerve catheter. In 94 patients after knee arthroplasty, continuous femoral triangle and femoral catheters gave comparable pain relief, and quadriceps strength and the timed up and go test were better with the femoral triangle and adductor canal catheters than with the femoral catheter.10
  • Against placebo. In an early trial of catheters placed at the thigh midpoint, ropivacaine reduced 0 to 24 hour morphine (40 against 56 mg) and the timed up and go test at 24 hours (36 against 50 seconds).316
  • Motor weakness after a bolus through catheters at two thigh levels, both later classified as femoral triangle, affected 19 of 25 and 16 of 24 patients.326
  • Dislodgement happened in one femoral triangle catheter between 24 and 48 hours in a trial of 94 patients.10

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the injection site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Patients in whom any quadriceps weakness would be unsafe, for example walking unaided on the day of surgery, unless the risk is explained and falls precautions are in place. Profound weakness has followed a midthigh injection.33
  • Coagulopathy and anticoagulation: the femoral vessels are close to the target, so apply your local guidance on peripheral nerve blocks in patients taking anticoagulants.
  • Pre-existing neuropathy in the limb: document it before the block.

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study, except where referenced.

Side effects and complications

Quadriceps weakness is the side effect that matters for this block. In volunteers, the effect on vastus medialis rose with volume. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

Electromyographic effect is not the same as clinical weakness: in the same volunteers, dynamometer strength did not differ between volumes.23 In another volunteer study, 10 and 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.1% each caused more than 25% loss of strength in 2 of 26 subjects.34

  • Falls. In a pooled analysis of placebo-controlled trials of continuous femoral nerve blocks after knee and hip arthroplasty, 6 of 85 patients receiving ropivacaine fell, against none of 86 receiving saline.35 These were femoral catheters, not femoral triangle blocks, but the mechanism is the same. A patient given 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% at midthigh had no quadriceps strength on discharge, lasting 20 hours.33 Explain the risk and follow your falls precautions.
  • Mobilisation compared with the adductor canal block. In a review that reclassified trials by injection site, pain at 24 hours did not differ between femoral triangle and true adductor canal blocks in five trials, but the timed up and go test at 24 hours favoured the adductor canal block in three.6
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks the incidence was 0.87 per 1000; ultrasound guidance was associated with a lower risk.27 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation and a higher risk in small or sarcopenic patients.36 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, manage the airway and give 100% oxygen, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion.25 Follow the Association of Anaesthetists guideline on the management of severe local anaesthetic toxicity.
  • Nerve injury. In the first Australasian audit, block-related nerve injury occurred in 0.4 per 1000 blocks, and most new neurological symptoms after surgery were unrelated to the block.37 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not this block alone.
  • Vascular puncture and haematoma are possible because the target lies beside the femoral vessels.6

Pearls of wisdom

  • Find the apex first, then come back up. The name of the block depends on where the needle is relative to the apex, not on a tape measure.17
  • If a trial says “adductor canal block at midthigh”, read it as a femoral triangle block.16
  • Higher in the triangle covers more skin over the knee: a gap at the anteromedial knee in 2 of 20 volunteers after a proximal injection, 17 of 20 after a distal one.22
  • The midline incision needs the intermediate femoral cutaneous nerve as well; block it above sartorius.229
  • Volume reaches vastus medialis. More volume, more effect on the muscle.23
  • Do not expect posterior knee cover. Dye from the distal triangle did not reach the popliteal fossa; add a posterior capsule or obturator block if needed.14320
  • Count every milligram: the block, the posterior block and the surgeon's infiltration all come out of the same lean-body-weight maximum.2527
  • Warn the patient and the ward about leg weakness before the first walk.3335

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Triangle against canal, head to head

The first meta-analysis restricted to this comparison included six trials and 452 patients. Pain did not differ at 6, 12 and 24 hours, and early quadriceps strength favoured the adductor canal block. The paper reports the 48-hour activity pain result in opposite directions in its results and its conclusion, so that finding should not be relied on.38 Proximal and distal adductor canal blocks have also been compared, with a small benefit for proximal injections at 2 and 24 hours at rest and low certainty of evidence.40

Two injections in one thigh

Combining a distal femoral triangle injection with a distal canal injection reduced anterior and posterior knee pain and morphine use against an adductor canal block with IPACK in 70 patients, and the timed up and go test was faster at 24 hours (59.2 against 85.6 seconds).7 A dual subsartorial block gave better pain control than either injection alone in 120 patients,41 and adding an injection above sartorius improved it further in 88.42 These are single-centre trials.

Where is the nerve to vastus medialis?

Cadaver studies disagree. A distal canal injection missed it in all four specimens in one study,11 but other studies found it stained in every specimen: its posteromedial branch after an injection at the proximal end of the canal,24 and the nerve itself (heavily in 4 of 12 specimens, moderately in 8) after an injection in the middle of the canal.43 If the canal reaches it reliably, the case for the more proximal injection narrows.

The posterior knee

With a femoral triangle block in place, IPACK and a selective tibial block gave similar morphine use after knee arthroplasty (6 against 5 mg on day 1), but 4 of 45 patients with the tibial block had common peroneal motor block.21 A popliteal plexus block was non-inferior to IPACK and quicker to perform (3 against 5 minutes).20 After ACL reconstruction with surgical infiltration, adding IPACK did not help.8
