Ankle block

Five nerves blocked where they cross the ankle: four branches of the sciatic nerve (tibial, deep fibular, superficial fibular and sural) and the saphenous nerve.1 The foot below the injections goes numb while the muscles that move the ankle keep working. In 655 patients having a unilateral block, ultrasound guidance gave surgical anaesthesia in 84% against 66% with landmarks.2 The price, compared with a popliteal sciatic block, is shorter analgesia.34

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The ankle block was one of the lower limb techniques placed in a residency core curriculum with strong consensus by an Italian Delphi panel.10 The nerves are shallow, from about 2 to 9 mm below the skin in cadavers,11 and none lies near a body cavity. The skill lies in finding five small nerves, four of them beside a vessel, and in accepting several needle entries. In a six-year cohort in which trainees performed about 80% of blocks, ultrasound guidance improved surgical anaesthesia over landmarks, and the authors concluded that the gain was greatest for less experienced practitioners.2 Names on this page follow the ASRA–ESRA template of nerve plus location, for example tibial nerve block at the ankle.12 Older papers call the fibular nerves peroneal.

Anatomy

Four of the five nerves come from the sciatic nerve: the tibial nerve, the deep and superficial fibular nerves from the common fibular nerve, and the sural nerve. The fifth, the saphenous nerve, is the end of the femoral nerve.1 Two run deep beside an artery (tibial and deep fibular), one beside a vein (sural), one beside a vein in the subcutaneous fat (saphenous), and one is purely subcutaneous by the time it reaches the ankle (superficial fibular).

Depth and distance. In 24 cadaveric ankles sectioned at the level of the malleoli, the sural, tibial, deep fibular, saphenous and medial dorsal cutaneous nerves lay 5.2, 9.2, 7.4, 2.8 and 2.1 mm deep to the skin. The tibial, deep fibular and saphenous nerves were 32.5, 62.8 and 24.4 mm from the medial malleolus along the skin; the sural nerve and the medial dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial fibular nerve were 27.9 and 52.7 mm from the lateral malleolus. The sural and tibial nerves each lay about halfway between their malleolus and the back of the Achilles tendon. The deep fibular nerve was usually between the tendons of extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus.11 In 60 dissections the tibial nerve lay a mean 21.1 mm from the medial edge of the Achilles tendon and 11.6 mm deep, and the sural nerve 18.3 mm from the back of the lateral malleolus.13

The tibial nerve divides early. It splits into the medial and lateral plantar nerves above the malleolar–calcaneal axis, within the tarsal tunnel, in 81.7% of 60 limbs, at the axis in 15% and below it in 3.3%; 8.3% had a trifurcation or an accessory branch.14 Above the malleolus it is more predictable: in 100 volunteers scanned at the supramalleolar level it had already divided in only 1%.15 The medial calcaneal nerve to the heel left the tibial nerve a mean 7 mm above the tip of the medial malleolus, but anywhere from 7 mm below to 60 mm above it.16

The superficial fibular nerve pierces the deep fascia of the leg a mean 89.6 mm above the lateral malleolus, with a wide range (26.5 to 153.8 mm), and then divides into medial and intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerves a mean 40.8 mm above the lateral malleolus.17 On ultrasound in 26 volunteers it pierced the fascia about 0.31 of the way along a line from the lateral malleolus to the head of the fibula, and in cadavers it had already divided before emerging in 46.88% of limbs.18

The sural nerve is most often formed by the union of the medial and lateral sural cutaneous nerves, usually in the lower or middle third of the leg (42.40% and 40.00%). A meta-analysis of 36 studies gave a pooled length of 144.54 mm from its formation to the lateral malleolus.19 It runs with the small saphenous vein, which is the landmark for the ultrasound-guided block.20

The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve reached the proximal or distal leg in 45.7% and 44.6% of 83 limbs, and lay close to the Achilles tendon in 13.2%. It is one possible explanation for patchy skin sensation around the back of the ankle after an otherwise good block.21

Surface anatomy

Five points around the ankle. Medially, the tibial nerve lies about halfway between the medial malleolus and the back of the Achilles tendon, and laterally the sural nerve lies halfway between the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon.11 In front, the deep fibular nerve lies between the tendons of extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus, which stand out when the patient lifts the big toe and the other toes.11 The saphenous nerve runs with the great saphenous vein in front of the medial malleolus, and the superficial fibular branches cross the front of the ankle under the skin.

The patient usually lies supine with the foot on a support so that the back of the ankle is reachable. Turning the leg outwards opens the medial side, and inwards the lateral side; some prefer the lateral or prone position for the posterior nerves. The volunteer trials of ultrasound-guided tibial and sural blocks placed subjects prone.2220

Indications

  • Forefoot surgery as the sole anaesthetic, with an ankle tourniquet and sedation if wanted. In 81 consecutive procedures in a Scottish unit all patients went home walking the same day.6 In an audit of 1000 midfoot and forefoot operations under ankle block, 97.2% said they would have surgery again with an ankle tourniquet.7
  • Analgesia after hallux valgus surgery under general anaesthesia. PROSPECT recommends the ankle block as the first-choice regional technique, with wound infiltration as the alternative.235
  • Bilateral foot surgery. In 66 patients, 89% had a successful bilateral ankle block, with no adverse local anaesthetic events.24
  • Diabetic foot surgery, debridement and toe amputation, often in patients with significant comorbidity.2526
  • Selected hindfoot surgery, as tibial and sural nerve blocks added to general anaesthesia for calcaneal fracture fixation.27

Nerves blocked

The tibial, deep fibular, superficial fibular and sural nerves, all from the sciatic nerve, and the saphenous nerve from the femoral nerve.1 Not every operation needs all five. A selective block of the tibial and both fibular nerves was used for great toe surgery in a bilateral trial,29 and in 100 bunion operations the saphenous territory reached the planned incision in only 3 patients.1 The medial calcaneal nerve may leave the tibial nerve well above the malleolus, so a low tibial injection can miss the heel.16

Because the block is below the muscles of the leg, ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion are preserved. Only the small muscles of the foot are weakened.

What the block covers

The foot below the level of injection. The skin of the ankle itself and the joint are outside the classic block.32

Landmark technique

The traditional ankle block uses two deep injections and a subcutaneous ring.

  1. Tibial nerve. Behind the medial malleolus, about halfway to the Achilles tendon, beside the pulse of the posterior tibial artery.11 A retromalleolar and a supramalleolar approach, each with 3 mL of mepivacaine 2%, succeeded in 81.8% and 78.2% of 110 blocks.8
  2. Sural nerve. Halfway between the lateral malleolus and the Achilles tendon.1113
  3. Deep fibular nerve. Between the tendons of extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus, beside the anterior tibial artery.11
  4. Superficial fibular and saphenous nerves. A subcutaneous ring across the front of the ankle from one malleolus to the other.932

A two-puncture variant, one entry for the tibial and sural nerves and one for the three anterior nerves, gave a complete block in 93% of 84 operations.13

RCT, volunteers

Where landmarks fall short

In 18 volunteers, each with one ankle blocked by each technique, the tibial nerve block was complete at 30 minutes in 72% with ultrasound against 22% with landmarks.22 For the sural nerve, loss of cold sensation at 10 minutes was 94% against 56%.20 For the deep fibular nerve ultrasound sped onset but made no difference after 20 minutes.34

Cohort study

In everyday practice

In 655 unilateral blocks, landmark technique led to more supplementary local anaesthetic (10% against 5%), more unplanned general anaesthesia (17% against 7%) and more intravenous opioid (21% against 12%) than ultrasound guidance.2

Ultrasound technique

A high-frequency linear probe, one nerve at a time. Three of the nerves are found by their companion vessel; colour Doppler helps.

Interactive scan and needle path to follow.

  1. Tibial nerve. Probe across the leg just above and behind the medial malleolus. Find the posterior tibial artery and its veins; the nerve lies just posterior to them. Slide up the leg: above the malleolus the nerve is almost always still single,15 while within the tarsal tunnel it often divides.14 Inject high enough to include the calcaneal branches if the heel matters.16
  2. Sural nerve. Probe behind the lateral malleolus over the small saphenous vein. Use light pressure so the vein stays open, and inject around the vein.20
  3. Deep fibular nerve. Probe across the front of the ankle. Find the anterior tibial artery between the extensor tendons; the nerve lies beside it.1135
  4. Superficial fibular nerve. Scan up the anterolateral leg to where the nerve comes through the fascia, about a third of the way from the lateral malleolus to the head of the fibula,18 and inject there or above its division. Otherwise infiltrate under the skin across the front of the ankle.9
  5. Saphenous nerve, if needed, beside the great saphenous vein in front of the medial malleolus. For bunion surgery it is rarely needed.1

RCT, volunteers

Tibial nerve

Ultrasound took longer (159 against 79 seconds) and needed more redirections, but 78% of volunteers preferred it, and the block was complete in more of them at every time point.22

RCT, volunteers

Sural nerve

Injecting around the small saphenous vein gave complete loss of cold sensation in 78% at 10 minutes against 28% with landmarks, and the block lasted: 33% against 6% were still completely numb at 60 minutes. It took a mean 102 seconds longer.20

RCT, volunteers

Deep fibular nerve

With 5 mL of chloroprocaine 3%, ultrasound gave better cold sensation and motor block at 10 minutes, with no difference from 20 to 60 minutes.34

Observational

Saphenous nerve

After blocks of the other four nerves in 100 bunion operations, the unblocked saphenous territory ended a mean 22 mm short of the first tarsometatarsal joint. Only 3% of incisions reached it.1

Needle path and nerve stimulator

Interactive scan and needle path to follow.

  1. Tibial nerve: in plane or out of plane, on a path that avoids the posterior tibial artery and veins; a stimulator response is flexion of the toes.
  2. Deep fibular nerve: out of plane or in plane beside the artery. A twitch of the toe extensors or of the muscle on the lateral dorsum of the foot confirms the nerve.35
  3. Sural and saphenous nerves: shallow injections around the companion vein, avoiding it.20
  4. Superficial fibular nerve: a subcutaneous injection over the nerve where it pierces the fascia, or a subcutaneous ring.189
Observational, volunteers

The stimulator at the front of the ankle

In 16 volunteers blocked on both feet, any evoked motor response or paraesthesia from the deep fibular nerve, after the nerve and artery were seen on ultrasound, was followed by complete numbness of the first web space.35

RCT

Two approaches to the tibial nerve

Retromalleolar and supramalleolar landmark approaches did not differ in onset, success, duration or pain on needling. The authors suggested the retromalleolar point for those new to the block because the nerve is easier to locate there.8

Continuous techniques

No randomised trial of catheters placed at the ankle was found for this page. When analgesia beyond a single injection is wanted after foot surgery, a catheter is usually placed at the popliteal level; see the popliteal sciatic block page. For hallux valgus repair the 2026 PROSPECT review found no evidence for continuous popliteal sciatic block.5

Dexamethasone 8 mg added to 30 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% gave no benefit at any time point over the same dose given intramuscularly, in a randomised double-blind trial.40

Contraindications

  • Infection, ulcers or broken skin at the injection sites.
  • Surgery at the ankle joint or above, or surgery needing a tourniquet above the ankle in an awake patient. Even a calf tourniquet caused more discomfort than one at the ankle.3233
  • Operations at high risk of compartment syndrome without a plan for monitoring. Compartment syndrome of the forefoot after revision forefoot surgery has been reported, and the authors warned that local anaesthetic blocks may obscure the clinical picture.43

Apart from the reference given, these are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Complications after ankle and popliteal blocks in the two largest comparisons. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies used different definitions and follow-up, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Nerve injury. In 2516 patients having foot and ankle surgery with an ankle or popliteal block, neurological or block-site complications occurred in 7.2% and serious ones in 0.7%, but both reviewers judged only 5 of the 195 complications likely to have been caused by the block.44 Across 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks of all types, neurological symptoms lasted more than 6 months after 0.9 per 1000.45
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks in an Australasian registry, with a lower risk when ultrasound was used,36 and seizures in 0.08 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks in another.45 These figures cover all peripheral blocks. Venous concentrations after ankle block were low in a small pharmacokinetic study.42 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation.37
  • Failure and conversion. Conversion to general anaesthesia was 13.6% after ankle block and 12.7% after popliteal block in a randomised trial.3 Unplanned general anaesthesia was 17% with landmarks and 7% with ultrasound in a cohort.2
  • Tourniquet pain. 3.1% of 1000 patients under ankle block had pain from an ankle tourniquet, and 0.4% needed general anaesthesia for it; the risk rose with age and beyond 30 minutes.7
  • Nausea and vomiting with perineural dexamethasone: 24% against 8% with intravenous dexamethasone in one trial.30
  • Masking of compartment syndrome after extensive forefoot surgery.43

Pearls of wisdom

  • Spend your ultrasound time on the tibial and sural nerves. That is where it made the biggest difference over landmarks.2220
  • Catch the tibial nerve above the malleolus, before it divides and before the heel branches leave it.151416
  • For bunion surgery you can usually leave out the saphenous nerve.1
  • Find the superficial fibular nerve where it comes through the fascia, about a third of the way up from the lateral malleolus, and expect it to have divided already in some patients.1817
  • Do not trim the volume too far. Cutting it to a mean 16 mL with ultrasound, against 30 mL by landmarks, kept the block working but more patients needed rescue analgesia (50% against 20%).9
  • Give the block time to work before the incision. Short soak time was the most plausible contributor to failures in a 2026 series of 92 feet.46
  • Put the tourniquet at the ankle, not the calf. It was better tolerated, though patients of 70 or over and tourniquet times beyond 30 minutes need more care.337
  • Patchy numbness behind the ankle after a good block may be the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, which reaches the Achilles region in some people.21

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Fewer needle entries

The five-in-two block reaches all five nerves through two punctures, with median sensory block of 554 minutes and no complications in 80 patients.48 A small artery beside the superficial fibular nerve in the mid-calf has been proposed as a landmark for that nerve, which until now had no companion vessel.49 Neither has yet been compared with the classic block in a trial.

Ankle or popliteal

The newest trial, with dexamethasone and full multimodal analgesia in both groups, found no significant difference in duration (15.4 against 20.0 hours), and 58% and 80% of patients never asked for morphine.41 The 2024 meta-analysis still favours the sciatic block for duration.4 PROSPECT 2026 confirms the ankle block as the first-choice regional technique for hallux valgus repair.5

Dexamethasone: perineural or intravenous

In a 100-patient ankle block trial, 4 mg perineurally and 10 mg intravenously gave the same duration (19 against 23.2 hours), with more nausea in the perineural group.30 A 2024 meta-analysis of nine lower limb trials found perineural dexamethasone 2.73 hours longer, judged unlikely to be clinically relevant, and noted that the perineural route is off label.50

The diabetic foot

In 70 patients having debridement, an ultrasound-guided high ankle block with 35 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% gave better early ulcer healing than general anaesthesia, with no difference in reoperation or amputation.26 In 42 patients with neuropathy, onset was faster when HbA1c was 8.0% or more (4.9 against 7.4 minutes).25 Both are single studies.
