Lumbar plexus block

Local anaesthetic placed within the posterior part of psoas major, where the femoral, obturator and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves run together before they separate. One injection reaches all three; the obturator nerve in particular is blocked more consistently than with a three-in-one femoral block.12 The price is depth and neighbours: the plexus lies 7 to 9 cm under the skin,23 close to the epidural space, the kidney and large retroperitoneal vessels, and European and American guidance treats it like a neuraxial technique when the patient is anticoagulated.45

Level of difficulty

Advanced block

This is a deep block, performed close to the neuraxis with a curvilinear probe, and the plexus itself is often hard to see. Through the paramedian transverse window in 30 young volunteers, the plexus was seen in 57% and the psoas compartment in 87%.11 In 15 patients scanned the same way the plexus was identified in two thirds, and the needle position was confirmed by a quadriceps twitch in every case.12 Most published series pair ultrasound with nerve stimulation.121314 Serious complications are rare but they are the kind that matter: total spinal anaesthesia, seizures, cardiac arrest and retroperitoneal bleeding have all been reported.15161017 It should be learnt under supervision once the operator is fluent with superficial blocks and neuraxial techniques. The names on this page follow the ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus template of nerve plus anatomical location.18

Anatomy

The lumbar plexus forms from the anterior rami of L1 to L4, often with a contribution from T12, inside psoas major. Its three main branches for the lower limb are the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2, L3), the femoral nerve (L2 to L4) and the obturator nerve (L2 to L4). The iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerves leave higher up.

The plexus lies inside the muscle. In four cadavers and 22 CT studies, the plexus at the level of L5 lay within the substance of psoas major, not in a plane between psoas and quadratus lumborum. The femoral nerve lay between the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and the obturator nerve. The obturator nerve was sometimes in the same plane as the other two and sometimes in its own fold of muscle, which helps to explain why it is missed.3 On CT the femoral nerve was 9.01 cm (SD 2.43) deep, and psoas extended from 2.73 cm to 6.41 cm from the midline.3

Depth depends on the patient. In 35 CT scans the plexus was a median 85 mm deep in men and 70 mm in women, and depth rose with body mass index. The distance from the L4 transverse process to the plexus did not differ between men and women and did not track body mass index, which is why the transverse process is the reliable depth landmark.2

Neighbours. Medially lie the vertebral body, the intervertebral foramen with the dural cuffs of the nerve roots, and the epidural space. Anterior and lateral lie the kidney, which reaches the L3 level in some patients, and the retroperitoneum; the lumbar arteries and veins run around the vertebral bodies. Posteriorly lie the transverse processes, quadratus lumborum and the erector spinae muscles. Injection into a root cuff can lead to subdural or intrathecal spread.1915 Renal subcapsular haematoma has been reported after the block.20

Where the injectate goes. On MRI in volunteers, injectate from both the Capdevila and the modified Winnie approach spread mainly within psoas around the L2 to L4 branches and tracked upwards to the nerve roots. In one of seven studies it spread between psoas and quadratus lumborum instead.21

Surface anatomy

The patient lies lateral with the side to be blocked uppermost, hips and knees flexed, as for a neuraxial block. Mark the spinous processes, the iliac crests and the intercristal line, which usually crosses the L4 spinous process or the L4 to L5 interspace, and the posterior superior iliac spine. For the landmark techniques the entry point lies a few centimetres lateral to the midline near L4. For the paramedian ultrasound scan the probe sits close to the midline or about 5 cm from it; for the shamrock scan it sits on the flank, just above the iliac crest.22

Distances from the midline follow the techniques as usually described; check them against the original papers and local practice.

Indications

  • Analgesia after hip arthroplasty and hip fracture surgery. Against general anaesthesia alone, a lumbar plexus block reduced intraoperative fentanyl use and early postoperative pain and morphine use after total hip arthroplasty.6 The 2021 PROSPECT review, however, did not recommend it for routine use after total hip arthroplasty because the adverse effects outweighed the benefits.23
  • Anaesthesia for the leg with a sciatic block, when neuraxial anaesthesia is unsuitable. A meta-analysis found this combination effective for minor knee surgery but found too few data to recommend it as the sole technique for hip and major knee surgery.124
  • Analgesia after knee arthroplasty by catheter, with more reliable obturator block than a three-in-one femoral block but no better analgesia.25
  • Surgery on the thigh and femur where the femoral, obturator and lateral femoral cutaneous territories are all involved.26

Nerves blocked

The femoral, obturator and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves, blocked together inside psoas. With a continuous block after hip arthroplasty, sensory block one hour after the loading dose was found in the femoral territory in 95% of patients, the obturator in 90% and the lateral femoral cutaneous in 85%.2 Against the three-in-one femoral block, the posterior approach blocks the obturator nerve more consistently.125 The genitofemoral nerve and, with higher spread, the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves may also be reached; this is not consistent.

The sacral plexus is not blocked. The sciatic nerve, the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve and the posterior hip joint need a separate injection.24

What the block covers

The front, inner and outer thigh, the anterior and medial hip, the knee from the front and inside, and the saphenous strip of the medial leg.

Landmark technique

Winnie and colleagues, who described the anterior inguinal “three-in-one” block in 1973,34 went on to describe a posterior approach, and Chayen and colleagues described the psoas compartment block in 1976.35 In 2002 Capdevila and colleagues used CT in 35 patients to define new landmarks, measured from the L4 transverse process, and showed that the depth of the plexus varies with sex and body mass index.2 The steps below describe the landmark approach in general terms.

  1. Mark the entry point. Winnie: where the intercristal line crosses a line parallel to the spine through the posterior superior iliac spine. Capdevila: on the intercristal line, at the junction of the lateral third and medial two thirds of the distance from the L4 spinous process to that parallel line, which brings the entry point more medial. Chayen: lower and more lateral.28
  2. Advance a long insulated needle perpendicular to the skin until it contacts the L4 transverse process, then walk it off the process.2
  3. Advance no more than about 2 cm beyond the transverse process, seeking a quadriceps twitch. A hamstring twitch means the needle is too caudal and medial, on the sacral roots.
  4. Aspirate, inject a test dose, then inject slowly in small fractions while watching for signs of epidural, intrathecal or intravascular injection.2619

The landmark geometry and the twitch responses follow standard descriptions of these approaches; the 2 cm margin beyond the transverse process is a widely taught limit, not a trial result.

Retrospective cohort

A lower, more lateral entry

In 700 patients who had hip arthroplasty or hemiarthroplasty with Chayen's approach, 25 mL of local anaesthetic and a parasacral sciatic block, epidural spread was recorded in one patient (0.14%).28 This is a single-centre retrospective series, and the comparison with other approaches was made against published figures rather than within the study.

Ultrasound technique

Three scanning windows are used. The paramedian sagittal scan shows the transverse processes as a “trident” with psoas visible through the gaps.24 The paramedian transverse scan looks through the intertransverse space at the articular process, psoas and, in many patients, the plexus.1112 The shamrock scan places the probe on the flank above the iliac crest: the transverse process forms the stem, with psoas anterior, erector spinae posterior and quadratus lumborum lateral as the three leaves, and it shows the plexus, the needle and the spread in real time in most patients.8

Interactive scan and needle path to follow.

  1. Choose a curvilinear probe and a deep setting. Position the patient lateral with the side to be blocked uppermost.
  2. Find the level. Count the transverse processes up from the sacrum on a paramedian sagittal scan, or identify L4 from the iliac crest.
  3. Shamrock view: place the probe transversely in the flank above the iliac crest and slide it until the transverse process and the three muscles appear. The plexus usually appears as a bright, oval structure in the posterior part of psoas. Insert the needle in plane from the back of the probe.87
  4. Paramedian transverse view: place the probe transversely a few centimetres from the midline and direct the beam medially through the intertransverse space towards the intervertebral foramen, to see the articular process and psoas.1112
  5. Confirm with a nerve stimulator. A quadriceps twitch confirms the plexus; injection into the posteromedial quadrant of psoas without stimulation has also been studied.1312
  6. Inject slowly, in fractions, at low pressure, watching the spread within psoas and checking for spread towards the intervertebral foramen.9

Anatomical relationships and stimulation responses follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

RCT, volunteers

Shamrock or paravertebral

In a crossover trial in 20 healthy men given 20 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline, the shamrock block was quicker than the lumbar ultrasound trident (238 against 334 seconds), less uncomfortable (median 3 against 4 out of 10) and needed fewer needle insertions (median 2 against 6). Spread on MRI was similar, and epidural spread was seen in 0 of 20 and 1 of 19.8

Volunteer imaging study

How often can you see it?

Through the paramedian transverse window in 30 volunteers, the nerve root was seen in 57%, the paravertebral space in 27%, the plexus in 57% and the psoas compartment in 87%.11 In 30 patients scanned at three probe positions, the plexus stood out best against psoas at the paravertebral position; at the shamrock position both looked brighter.22

Cadaver study

Accuracy

In 10 embalmed cadavers, 47 of 48 ultrasound-guided needle placements into the psoas compartment at L2 to L5 were confirmed on CT; one tip lay behind psoas.36

RCT

In plane or out of plane

In 42 patients with femoral neck fracture, an out-of-plane shamrock approach needed fewer insertions and redirections, and a shallower needle path (5.1 against 6.5 cm), than the in-plane approach, with no difference in procedure time, block onset or epidural spread.29

Needle path and nerve stimulator

In the shamrock approach the needle passes through erector spinae and quadratus lumborum, or along the transverse process, into the posterior part of psoas. In the paravertebral approaches it passes through erector spinae and the intertransverse space. The motor response sought is a quadriceps twitch; one trial accepted it at a current of 0.2 to 0.8 mA.13

Interactive scan and needle path to follow.

RCT

Ultrasound with or without stimulation

In 110 patients having hip or knee arthroplasty, adding stimulation to ultrasound shortened total anaesthesia time (15.3 against 20.1 minutes) and onset (10.2 against 15.5 minutes). The difference fell inside the equivalence margin, and success, needle passes, block pain and adverse events did not differ.13

RCT

Stimulation with or without ultrasound

In 44 patients for knee arthroscopy, ultrasound plus stimulation gave a faster complete sensory block than stimulation alone (median 10 against 30 minutes to pinprick) and fewer patients needed five or more needle passes (0% against 27.3%), at the cost of a longer performance time (658 against 528 seconds). Failure was 9.1% against 31.8%, a difference that was not statistically significant.14

RCT

Injection pressure

During stimulator-guided blocks with 35 mL of mepivacaine 1.5%, a high opening pressure (above 20 psi) led to neuraxial block to T10 or higher in 5 of 10 patients and bilateral femoral sensory block in 6 of 10, against none of 10 with low pressure (below 15 psi). The trial was stopped early.9

Case report

A test dose earned its keep

After a quadriceps twitch over the transverse process, a 3 mL test dose of lidocaine 1.5% produced a block that spread to both legs and up to C7, with hypotension, consistent with subdural injection. The patient recovered fully.19

These are the regimens used in the cited studies, not recommendations: 0.4 mL/kg of bupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline;6 20 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline in volunteers;8 30 mL of lidocaine 1% with levobupivacaine 0.25%, adrenaline and dexamethasone;13 35 mL of mepivacaine 1.5%;9 and, for sensory block alone with the shamrock technique, an estimated 17.1 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% for 50% and 25.8 mL for 95% of patients.7

Maximum safe dose. This is a high-dose block into a well-perfused muscle and is often combined with a sciatic block. In 11 patients given 150 mg of ropivacaine without adrenaline, ropivacaine appeared in plasma within 30 seconds, peaks came within 10 minutes in nine, and peak total concentrations ranged from 422 to 3905 ng/mL; one patient had a partial intravascular injection.37 Calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient from lean body weight, add together the doses for every block and infiltration, and stay below it.38 Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk.39 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, manage the airway and give oxygen, treat seizures (benzodiazepines first), and give 20% lipid emulsion following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline.38

Continuous techniques

In 80 patients after hip arthroplasty, catheters threaded 5 to 8 cm lay within psoas in 74% and between psoas and quadratus lumborum in 22% on contrast radiography; three were malpositioned. With a 0.4 mL/kg loading dose and a 48-hour infusion of ropivacaine 0.2%, median pain was about 10 mm at rest and 18 to 25 mm during physiotherapy.2 In 169 consecutive patients, radiography found epidural spread of contrast in 3 (1.8%), only one of them clinically relevant; the authors concluded that routine radiography was unnecessary.26

Against intravenous patient-controlled analgesia alone, a continuous block reduced morphine use by 12 mg and pain by 2.1 points out of 10 over 36 hours in 34 patients; one patient with a catheter developed a delayed paresis.40 In 225 patients, a continuous lumbar plexus block gave less pain during physiotherapy on days 1 and 2 than a continuous femoral block or analgesia alone, with greater walking distances.27 In 47 patients, femoral and lumbar plexus catheters gave the same pain scores (3.6 against 3.5), but patients with a femoral catheter walked less the morning after surgery (2 against 11 m).5

Ambulatory infusion for 4 days, against overnight only, shortened the time to meet discharge criteria (29 against 51 hours), but three patients receiving ropivacaine fell (13%) against none receiving saline.33 With a 30 mL bolus of ropivacaine 0.5% and an infusion of 0.2% at 12 mL/h, plasma concentrations were higher early with the posterior approach than with a three-in-one block, and concentrations rose throughout the 48-hour infusion with both.41

Catheter removal follows the same anticoagulant timing rules as insertion; see Contraindications.4

Contraindications

  • Anticoagulation or abnormal coagulation. The ESAIC/ESRA guideline applies neuraxial timing intervals to deep, non-compressible blocks, and ultrasound does not change those intervals.4 ASRA guidance likewise recommends against continuous posterior lumbar plexus blocks during several perioperative anticoagulant regimens.542 Treat this block as you would an epidural, for both catheter insertion and removal.
  • Patient refusal, infection at the site, and allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Relative: spinal deformity, previous lumbar spine surgery at the level, a known renal or retroperitoneal mass, and patients in whom a neuraxial block would be dangerous, because epidural spread occurs.643

Infection, refusal, allergy and spinal deformity are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Epidural or bilateral spread is the commonest adverse effect in published studies. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies used different techniques, volumes and definitions, and the small trials give wide uncertainty, so compare within a study and not across them. Injection pressure matters: high-pressure injection caused neuraxial block in half the patients in one trial.9

  • Total spinal and subdural block. Total spinal anaesthesia has followed a continuous posterior lumbar plexus block,15 and a 3 mL test dose revealed a subdural injection that spread to C7.19 Fractionate the dose, keep talking to the patient, and be ready to support the airway and circulation.
  • Cardiac arrest. In a French survey of 158,083 regional anaesthetics, one cardiac arrest followed a posterior lumbar plexus block, which the authors reported as a rate of 80 per 10,000 for that block against 2.7 per 10,000 for spinal anaesthesia; a single event in a small number of plexus blocks gives a very imprecise estimate. In the same survey, systemic local anaesthetic toxicity consisted of seizures only, without cardiac toxicity, so this arrest was not reported as systemic toxicity.10
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Two patients had grand mal seizures immediately after levobupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline for a posterior lumbar plexus block despite negative aspiration.16 In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks the overall incidence was 0.87 per 1000, and the site of injection was one of the predictors.44
  • Retroperitoneal haematoma. Extensive retroperitoneal haematoma has been reported in two patients anticoagulated after the block,45 and a delayed haematoma presenting two weeks later, with motor deficit and the need for transfusion, followed three unsuccessful attempts and postoperative enoxaparin.17 Renal subcapsular haematoma has also been reported.20
  • Weakness and falls. Quadriceps and adductor weakness are expected.32 Three falls occurred in 24 patients given 4-day ropivacaine infusions.33
  • Nerve injury. One patient with a lumbar plexus catheter developed a delayed paresis in a 34-patient trial.40 Across all peripheral nerve blocks in the French survey, 9 of 12 neurological complications occurred when a nerve stimulator had been used.10
  • Hypotension follows epidural or bilateral spread; it was more frequent when a T12 paravertebral block was added to a two-level lumbar plexus block than with a lumbar plexus and quadratus lumborum combination.46

Pearls of wisdom

  • Plan the anticoagulant timing before you plan the block. If you would not site an epidural, do not site this.4
  • Use the transverse process as your depth gauge. Skin depth varies with sex and body mass index; the distance from the process to the plexus does not.2
  • Inject slowly and at low pressure. High-pressure injection produced a neuraxial block in half the patients in one trial.9
  • A test dose and fractionated injection buy you time to spot subdural, intrathecal or intravascular placement.1916
  • Expect the peak plasma level within 10 minutes. Stay with the patient and monitor closely through that window.37
  • Count the total dose when you add a sciatic block or surgical infiltration, and work from lean body weight.38
  • Warn the ward about quadriceps weakness and falls, especially with an infusion.33
  • Ask whether you need this block at all. For many hip operations a more superficial block or infiltration gives similar analgesia with less risk.2330

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Network meta-analyses for the hip

Three network meta-analyses published in 2025 and 2026 reached different rankings. One of 87 trials favoured PENG with infiltration and noted motor block with the lumbar plexus block;32 one of 18 trials ranked the lumbar plexus block first for 24-hour opioid use but rated the certainty low or very low;47 one of 18 trials found no single block best across outcomes.48 None makes a case for routine use when superficial blocks are available.

Combining with a quadratus lumborum block

In 84 patients having hip arthroplasty, a single-level shamrock lumbar plexus block alone covered the target skin area in 7.1%, against 67.6% when a transmuscular quadratus lumborum block was added at L3 and 89.3% with a T12 paravertebral and two-level lumbar plexus block. The quadratus lumborum combination caused less hypotension than the paravertebral combination. No toxicity, nerve injury, haematoma or epidural block was observed.46

Out-of-plane shamrock

An out-of-plane needle path needed fewer redirections and a shallower route than the in-plane approach in 42 patients with femoral neck fracture.29 One small trial; the safety implications of a shorter path have not been studied.

Pharmacokinetics and adjuncts

In a 2026 randomised pharmacokinetic study in 46 patients, perineural dexmedetomidine 0.5 µg/kg lowered the peak ropivacaine concentration and prolonged sensory block by 175 minutes. Modelled toxic doses of ropivacaine, expressed per kilogram, fell as body weight rose.49 These are modelling results from one population and should not be used as dosing rules.
