Saphenous nerve block

The saphenous nerve is the only branch of the femoral nerve that reaches below the knee. It carries sensation from the inner side of the leg, the medial malleolus and part of the medial foot, and deep branches to the periosteum of the distal tibia and the medial ankle capsule.1 It can be blocked anywhere from the mid-thigh to the ankle. Its commonest job is to complete a popliteal sciatic block: after major ankle surgery with a sciatic block alone, 8 of 9 patients given a saline saphenous block had significant pain, against 1 of 9 given local anaesthetic.2

Level of difficulty

Basic block

In the thigh the femoral artery is an easy landmark and the nerve lies beside it, so the ultrasound-guided block is a basic one. The difficulty is the nerve itself: it is thin, purely sensory and often hard to see. In a trial of 91 analysed patients it was easily visible in only 49% at the adductor canal and 48% just above the knee, and neither technique was reliable enough to give surgical anaesthesia on its own.11 The approaches without ultrasound are simple to perform but unreliable: a field block below the knee succeeded in 30% of volunteers, against 100% for an ultrasound-guided block beside the femoral artery.9

Anatomy

The saphenous nerve is the terminal sensory branch of the femoral nerve (L3 and L4). It runs down through the femoral triangle on the lateral side of the femoral artery, crosses in front of the artery and enters the adductor canal with it, then leaves the canal near the knee and follows the great saphenous vein down the medial leg to the ankle and foot. It has no motor fibres.

Femoral triangle and adductor canal. The adductor canal begins where the medial border of sartorius crosses the medial border of adductor longus, and ends at the adductor hiatus. In 22 volunteers this proximal end lay a mean 4.6 cm (range 2.3 to 7.0) distal to the midpoint of the thigh, so an injection at the midpoint of the thigh is in fact a femoral triangle block.12 The roof of the canal is a continuous fascia that thickens distally into the vastoadductor membrane.13 In one dissection study of 15 limbs the saphenous nerve was the only nerve seen inside the canal;13 in another of 20 limbs the nerve to vastus medialis was also consistently found along it.14 The ASRA–ESRA consensus names limb blocks by the nerve and the place where it is blocked, which is why "saphenous nerve block" needs a level attached to it.15

Leaving the canal. In 9 knees the nerve left the canal a median 10.25 cm (range 7.0 to 11.5) above the upper border of the patella and then ran with the saphenous branch of the descending genicular artery; it divided into its infrapatellar and sartorial branches a median 2.7 cm above the patella.16 In 11 cadaver knees it left through the lower opening of the canal in 9 and higher up in 2, and in every dissection it then passed between sartorius and the femoral artery.17 In 12 of 17 cadavers the infrapatellar branch left the canal separately from the main nerve; in the rest it arose below the canal.18

Below the knee the nerve lies close to the great saphenous vein, which is the landmark for the paravenous techniques.5 Just below the medial tibial condyle it can be seen between the aponeuroses of sartorius and gracilis.19

At the ankle and foot. In 29 cadaver feet the nerve was present at the ankle in 24 and continued into the foot in 19. It lay a mean 4 mm from the great saphenous vein and 15 mm from the tibialis anterior tendon, and reached the first metatarsal in 28%.20 On ultrasound in 120 limbs the vein sat a mean 15.4 mm from the medial malleolus and the nerve a mean 3.9 mm from the vein.21 In 103 cadaver limbs deep branches left the nerve 3.9 to 8.2 cm above the medial malleolus to supply the periosteum of the distal tibia and the medial ankle capsule.1 A systematic review of ten anatomical studies places its joint supply in front of and below the medial malleolus and at the medial talocalcaneonavicular joint.22

Variations. The cutaneous branch of the obturator nerve has been traced as far as the medial ankle and foot in two cadavers, running with the great saphenous vein; in both, the saphenous nerve supplied only the skin in front of the vein.23

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg slightly abducted and externally rotated, so that the medial thigh and leg face the operator.

  • Thigh. The probe lies across the anteromedial thigh. The midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella is usually still in the femoral triangle; the canal begins a few centimetres further down.12 In 20 patients the nerve was found a mean 12.7 cm above the knee joint, at a depth of 2.7 cm.4
  • Above the knee. The nerve leaves the canal about 10 cm above the upper border of the patella.16
  • Below the knee. The great saphenous vein on the medial side of the leg, at about the level of the tibial tuberosity or just below the medial tibial condyle.519
  • Ankle. The great saphenous vein in front of the medial malleolus, about 1.5 cm from it.21

Indications

  • Foot and ankle surgery, with a popliteal sciatic block. In a placebo-controlled trial after major ankle surgery, significant pain occurred in 8 of 9 patients given a saline saphenous block, and in every case breakthrough pain from the saphenous territory began within 30 minutes of surgery.2 In the AnAnkle trial, popliteal sciatic and saphenous block anaesthesia was compared with spinal anaesthesia for ankle fracture fixation, and the blocks succeeded in more than 94%.3
  • Surgery under a calf tourniquet. With a cuff 10 cm below the knee, tolerance ranged from 60% to 100% depending on how the saphenous nerve was blocked.24
  • Surgery or wounds on the medial leg and ankle, within the skin territory of the nerve.
  • Knee surgery. A meta-analysis of nine trials found lower pain scores and 24-hour morphine use with a saphenous block than with placebo.25 For knee arthroplasty and cruciate ligament surgery the block is described on the adductor canal and femoral triangle pages.

Nerves blocked

The saphenous nerve, and with it the infrapatellar branch if the injection is above the point where that branch leaves; in most cadavers the infrapatellar branch leaves inside the canal.18 In a placebo-controlled trial, numbness in the infrapatellar territory at the knee correlated with relief of pain at the front and inner side of the ankle.2

What else is blocked depends on the level. Injections in the thigh can reach motor nerves to the quadriceps: in volunteers an adductor canal block reduced quadriceps strength by a mean 8% from baseline, against 49% with a femoral nerve block.33 Inside the adductor canal, 5 mL caused vastus medialis weakness in 36% of patients, against none when the same volume was given just below the canal.10 Injectate in the canal can also spread down into the popliteal fossa.13

What the block covers

The skin of the medial leg to the medial malleolus and part of the medial foot. The rest of the leg and foot belongs to the sciatic nerve and needs its own block.

Landmark technique

Before ultrasound the saphenous nerve was blocked by infiltration below the knee, by a paravenous injection, by a transsartorial injection above the knee or through the femoral nerve at the groin. Success varied widely.

  • Transsartorial. In 20 volunteers the transsartorial block succeeded in 80%, against 65% for a field block below the knee and 40% for a field block beside the femoral condyle. A successful transsartorial block numbed the medial malleolus in 94%; the other two did so in fewer than 40% of their successful blocks.34
  • Five approaches in the same volunteers. In a crossover study of 10 volunteers the transsartorial approach was best for the medial foot, and unlike a perifemoral block at the groin it did not weaken the hip flexors or knee extensors. When the medial foot was only partly numb, a block of the medial dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial peroneal nerve completed it.35
  • Paravenous below the knee. With 5 mL of mepivacaine in 20 volunteers, a paravenous injection beside the great saphenous vein worked in all 20, against 6 (reported as 33%) with blind infiltration on the other leg.5
  • Through the femoral nerve. In 80 patients having bunion surgery, a femoral nerve stimulator approach at the groin succeeded in 95%, against 60% paravenous, 50% transsartorial and 45% for infiltration below the knee.24 It blocks the quadriceps as well.

In a direct comparison in volunteers, ultrasound-guided approaches above the knee were far more successful than a below-knee field block.9 This page does not describe a landmark technique in detail.

Ultrasound technique

Choose the level first. The thigh approaches are the most reliable; the distal ones avoid any effect on the quadriceps but give up some reliability.

  1. Thigh: find the femoral artery in short axis under sartorius on the medial thigh, using a linear probe.4
  2. Slide distally past the point where the medial border of sartorius crosses the medial border of adductor longus. Below it you are in the adductor canal rather than the femoral triangle.12
  3. Look for the nerve on the anterior or anteromedial side of the artery, deep to sartorius and the vastoadductor membrane.46 If it cannot be seen, aim for the same space beside the artery.
  4. For a distal subsartorial block, follow the artery down until it leaves for the adductor hiatus, then find the saphenous branch of the descending genicular artery beneath sartorius; the nerve runs with it.1611
  5. Below the knee, find the great saphenous vein; with light probe pressure it collapses. Inject around the vein, or at the nerve between the sartorius and gracilis aponeuroses just below the medial tibial condyle.519
  6. At the ankle, find the vein in front of the medial malleolus and place the local anaesthetic around it; the nerve is a few millimetres away.2120

RCT

Which level in the thigh

In 120 patients the adductor canal approach succeeded in every case, against 85% for the distal transsartorial approach, with a shorter median time to success (3 against 9 minutes) and better nerve images.8 In a second trial of 91 analysed patients, the adductor canal and the peri-genicular-artery approach gave 84% and 81%, and the arterial landmark was easily seen in 84% against 60%.11

RCT

Above or below the knee

In 68 patients analysed, success was 91% with a medial infracondylar approach below the knee and 88% with a subsartorial approach. The subsartorial block lasted longer, a mean 415 against 370 minutes, a difference the authors judged clinically unimportant.19

Dose-finding

How little is enough

In 18 volunteers, the ED50 for a selective saphenous block beside the femoral artery was 1.5 mL of mepivacaine 2%, and the calculated ED95 1.9 mL. No femoral motor block occurred.6 With 5 mL of lidocaine 1.5% with adrenaline, complete block was reached in only 55 to 59% of patients within 15 minutes.10

Cadaver

Where the injectate goes

Fifteen millilitres injected into the canal in 15 cadaver limbs spread along the whole canal, up into the femoral triangle and 1 to 2 cm into the popliteal fossa. Injections superficial to the canal spread only half its length and looked different on ultrasound.13

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. In the thigh, pass the needle in plane,4 usually from the lateral end of the probe, through or beneath sartorius, towards the anterior side of the femoral artery.
  2. Aspirate and inject a small test volume. The local anaesthetic should spread beside the artery beneath sartorius, or beneath the vastoadductor membrane if the injection is inside the canal.13
  3. Below the knee and at the ankle, the needle is short and subcutaneous; place the local anaesthetic on both sides of the vein without entering it.5

RCT

A sensory nerve can still be stimulated

The stimulator gives no twitch, but it can produce a tapping sensation down the inner leg towards the medial malleolus. In 80 patients, adding stimulation to ultrasound did not raise complete block significantly (55% against 45%). No tapping sensation could be found within 5 minutes in 20% of the stimulated group, and all three failed blocks in that group were among them.7

RCT, volunteers

Quadriceps strength

In 11 volunteers analysed, a block in the adductor canal region reduced quadriceps strength by a mean 8% from baseline, against 49% with a femoral nerve block, and walking tests were better preserved.33

Cadaver and case series

Further down, fewer motor nerves

With 5 mL of dye in the subsartorial compartment below the point where the nerve crosses the adductor magnus tendon, the saphenous nerve was stained in 15 of 15 cadaver sides and a terminal branch of the nerve to vastus medialis in 2. All five patients given this block with a popliteal sciatic block walked without support.36

RCT

Veins in the way

The paravenous approach caused a painless minor haematoma in 7 of 20 volunteers, against 2 of 20 with blind infiltration.5 Ultrasound lets you see the vein before the needle reaches it.

Volumes studied. Trials used 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline,2 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%,7 8 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline,11 10 mL of lidocaine 1.5%,17 5 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline plus 5 mL of bupivacaine 0.5%,4 and 5 mL in two trials.105 A dose-finding study in volunteers calculated an ED95 of 1.9 mL of mepivacaine 2% for a selective block.6 These are the volumes used in the cited studies, not a dosing recommendation.

Maximum safe dose. This block is nearly always given with a popliteal sciatic block, so add the two doses together. Work out the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient, based on lean body weight as ASRA guidance recommends, and stay below it.37 In a registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, dose per weight, dose and patient weight were among the predictors of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.38 Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk.39 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, secure the airway and give oxygen, treat seizures, support the circulation and give 20% lipid emulsion, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline.3739

Continuous techniques

A saphenous catheter adds little when a popliteal sciatic catheter is running. In 44 patients analysed after major ankle surgery, all with a sciatic catheter and an initial saphenous block, a saphenous catheter in the proximal thigh running ropivacaine gave a mean 48-hour morphine use of 24.7 mg, against 27.8 mg with saline.40 An older trial in 50 patients found less pain on movement and less morphine when a femoral catheter was added to a popliteal catheter, and less pain on movement at 6 months.27 A prospective study of 60 outpatients found less opioid use on the day of surgery and the first day after it with two catheters than with a popliteal catheter and a single saphenous injection.41

Alternatives to a catheter are being tried. Repeating single sciatic and saphenous injections at about 24 hours kept 9 of 11 patients opioid-free for 48 hours after major ankle and hindfoot surgery.42 Perineural dexamethasone 4 mg prolonged a single saphenous block from a mean 23.2 to 29.4 hours.26

Contraindications

  • Infection at the injection site, patient refusal, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • As the sole anaesthetic for foot or ankle surgery. Neither of two ultrasound techniques in a trial of 91 patients was reliable enough for surgical anaesthesia on its own,11 and the sciatic territory is outside the block.
  • A patient who must walk soon after surgery, where an injection in the thigh could weaken the quadriceps;33 a more distal level spares it.1736
  • Existing neuropathy in the saphenous territory: record it before the block.

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study unless a reference is given.

Side effects and complications

Haematoma and muscle weakness are the complications measured in saphenous trials. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies used different volumes and definitions, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Quadriceps weakness with injections at or above the adductor canal. The mean loss of strength after an adductor canal block was 8% in volunteers,33 but individuals vary; take care with early walking.
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks in an Australasian registry, lower with ultrasound;38 seizures in 0.08 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks.43 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation.39 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not the saphenous block alone.
  • Nerve injury. Block-related nerve injury in 0.4 per 1000 blocks in the Australasian audit, where most neurological symptoms after surgery were judged unrelated to the block.44 Neurological symptoms lasting more than 6 months followed 0.9 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks.43 In a prospective series of 92 feet given ankle blocks or popliteal and saphenous blocks there was no neurological injury.45
  • Rebound pain. In the AnAnkle trial, sciatic and saphenous block anaesthesia gave a better pain profile than spinal anaesthesia overall, despite substantial rebound pain when the blocks wore off.3 Plan oral analgesia before the block ends.

Pearls of wisdom

  • If the saphenous block is missed after ankle surgery, the pain arrives early: breakthrough pain from the saphenous territory began within 30 minutes of surgery in every case.2
  • If you need the medial malleolus, do not rely on a field block. A successful transsartorial block numbed it in 94%; field blocks above and below the knee did so in fewer than 40% of their successful blocks.34
  • The midpoint of the thigh is usually the femoral triangle, not the canal.12 Even a canal block cost volunteers a mean 8% of quadriceps strength;33 go below the canal if walking matters.36
  • If you use a stimulator and cannot find the tapping sensation down the leg, be ready for the block to fail: all three failures in the stimulator group were in patients with no response.7
  • For bunion surgery the saphenous territory reached the incision in only 3% of patients,32 but in cadavers the nerve reached the first metatarsal in 28%. Look at where the surgeon will cut.20
  • Patchy numbness of the medial foot after a good saphenous block? Add the medial dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial peroneal nerve.35
  • At the ankle the nerve is about 4 mm from the vein. Spread around the vein rather than hunting for the nerve.2120
  • Without an adjunct, popliteal sciatic and saphenous blocks gave a median 870 minutes and a mean 17.6 hours of analgesia in two recent trials; intravenous dexamethasone extended this, and as much as perineural dexamethasone.2931

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Making it last: dexamethasone

With popliteal and saphenous blocks in 120 patients, median analgesia lasted 1,400 minutes with intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg and 870 minutes with placebo; adding intravenous dexmedetomidine 1 µg/kg gave 1,572 minutes, no different from dexamethasone alone.29 In 75 patients, intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg (26.2 hours) was non-inferior to perineural dexamethasone 7.5 mg (27.9 hours), and both beat placebo (17.6 hours).31

Lower concentration, less weakness

In 31 patients having both feet operated on, ropivacaine 0.25% on one side and 0.375% on the other gave the same analgesia (31.7 against 31.9 hours), with less motor block on the 0.25% side.30

Liposomal bupivacaine

In 142 patients having foot or ankle surgery, liposomal bupivacaine 133 mg for popliteal sciatic and saphenous blocks lowered sufentanil use at 12, 24, 48 and 72 hours compared with ropivacaine 50 mg.49 This was a single-centre trial.

New levels and combinations

A distal subsartorial compartment block spared the quadriceps in a small series.36 A two-injection ankle block reaches the saphenous nerve with the tibial and sural nerves above the malleolus.48 Under processed-nociception-guided general anaesthesia, sciatic and saphenous blocks lowered pain scores immediately after ankle fracture surgery (2.6 against 6.2) and reduced remifentanil use.28 In a prospective series, a short interval between block and incision was the most likely contributor to failure.45
