LL-09 · Lower limb

Genicular nerve block

Small volumes of local anaesthetic placed on the bone of the distal femur and proximal tibia, usually beside a genicular artery, to block the articular branches that supply the front of the knee capsule. These branches run on the periosteum, which is why a bone-contact injection can reach them.1 The block is sensory, so the quadriceps and the foot are spared. Added to an adductor canal catheter and a posterior capsule block after knee arthroplasty, it reduced opioid use in the first 24 hours from 58 to 23 morphine milligram equivalents against saline.2 In a large 2026 cohort with a full multimodal pathway, however, adding it brought no clinically meaningful gain.3

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

Each target is shallow and lies on bone, and no large nerve trunk is in the path of the three standard injections. The difficulty is that the nerves themselves are usually too small to see. The operator aims instead for the genicular artery that travels with each nerve, or, if no artery can be found, for a bony point on the femur or tibia.6 That makes this a block of three to five separate injections guided by surrogate landmarks. In cadavers the approach works: ultrasound-guided dye placed a mean 1.3 to 3.4 mm from the three standard nerves.11 Choosing the right bony point matters. The classical fluoroscopic target for the superomedial nerve captured it in none of the cadaver knees tested, against all of them with revised targets.12

Anatomy

A shared and overlapping supply. The knee receives articular branches from the femoral, sciatic and obturator nerves, with much overlap and variation between people. A 2026 review of 32 studies found the anterior capsule to have the highest density of nociceptors in the joint.13 The “genicular nerves” are the named articular branches that reach the capsule around the distal femur and the proximal tibia.

Four quadrants at the front. In 15 dissected knees, each articular branch ended in one quadrant of the anterior capsule, with little overlap. The superolateral quadrant was supplied by the nerves to vastus lateralis and vastus intermedius, the superior lateral genicular nerve and the common fibular nerve; the inferolateral by the inferior lateral genicular and recurrent fibular nerves; the superomedial by the nerves to vastus medialis and vastus intermedius and the superior medial genicular nerve; and the inferomedial by the inferior medial genicular nerve, joined in 3 specimens by the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve. All these branches, except the nerves to vastus lateralis and vastus medialis, ran at the level of the periosteum.1 The nerve to vastus medialis is a substantial contributor to the anteromedial capsule; the saphenous nerve contributes more modestly, through its infrapatellar and posterior branches.14

Five constant targets. In 21 limbs, the nerve to vastus medialis, the saphenous nerve, the anterior branch of the obturator nerve and a sciatic branch supplied the medial capsule; the sciatic nerve and the nerve to vastus lateralis supplied the superolateral aspect, and the fibular nerve the inferolateral quadrant. The authors identified five constant genicular nerves with reliable landmarks.15 Another cadaver study found the paths of these nerves to be closely tied to the muscles around them, but variable enough between individuals to be hard to predict.16

The back of the knee is different. In 15 specimens, branches of the tibial nerve and the posterior division of the obturator nerve supplied the posterior capsule in every case; branches from the common fibular nerve were present in 53% and from the sciatic nerve in 20%.10 Standard genicular injections at the front of the knee are not aimed at these branches.

The medial nerves. The superior medial genicular nerve curves around the femoral shaft, passes between the adductor magnus tendon and the medial epicondyle, and descends about 1 cm anterior to the adductor tubercle. The inferior medial genicular nerve runs horizontally around the medial tibial condyle and passes beneath the medial collateral ligament.17

The arteries as guides. The descending genicular artery was present in all 27 specimens in one study, arising a mean 130.5 mm above the joint line. Three of its branches ran consistently with nerves on the medial side of the knee: with the superior medial genicular nerve, with the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve, and as the saphenous branch.18 The superior lateral, superior medial and inferior medial genicular arteries accompany their nerves at the junction of shaft and epicondyle.5

The inferolateral nerve. In ten cadaver knees, an inferior lateral genicular nerve arising from the common fibular nerve was found in 8. It has often been left out of genicular blocks to avoid the common fibular nerve and foot drop.19

Names. The 2024 ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus recommends naming limb blocks after the nerve and the place where it is blocked.20 On that template, a genicular nerve block is not one injection but a set of named nerve blocks around the knee; trials should state which nerves were targeted.

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the knee extended or slightly flexed on a pillow. A linear high-frequency probe is used.21 For the two upper targets, the probe is placed over the medial and then the lateral side of the distal femur and moved up and down the shaft until the bone flares into the epicondyle; for the lower target, the same is done over the medial side of the proximal tibia.5 Palpable guides are the adductor tubercle (about 1 cm behind the superomedial target) and the medial collateral ligament over the tibia (for the inferomedial target).17 On the lateral side, the head of the fibula marks the course of the common fibular nerve, which must be avoided.

Indications

  • Analgesia after total knee arthroplasty, as part of a multimodal plan, usually with an adductor canal block and often a posterior capsule (IPACK) block.2219 A 2025 meta-analysis of six trials found lower pain scores up to 24 hours, but no difference in morphine use or knee flexion.22
  • As an alternative to periarticular infiltration when the surgeon does not infiltrate. Results against infiltration are mixed.2356
  • Arthroscopic knee surgery, including ligament reconstruction.42425
  • Chronic knee pain (a different use). In pain medicine, genicular blocks with local anaesthetic, sometimes with a steroid, are used for osteoarthritis pain and to select patients for radiofrequency ablation.2627 That use, and ablation itself, are summarised separately under Evolving evidence; they are not perioperative techniques.

Nerves blocked

The three standard targets are the superior lateral, superior medial and inferior medial genicular nerves; most perioperative trials have used these three.22184

Expanded protocols add other branches. A cadaver-validated ultrasound protocol targeted five: the superior medial, superior lateral and inferior medial genicular nerves, the recurrent fibular nerve and the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve.29 One arthroplasty trial blocked five nerves including the inferior lateral genicular nerve and a recurrent branch beside the anterior recurrent tibial artery.6 Another added a fourth injection for the inferolateral quadrant.30

The nerves to the vasti carry articular branches as well as supplying the quadriceps, which is why genicular injections are kept small and placed on the bone rather than higher in the muscle. The posterior capsule, supplied by tibial and posterior obturator branches, is not part of this block.10

What the block covers

The front of the knee capsule, quadrant by quadrant, depending on which targets are injected. Not the skin incision, not the back of the knee, and not the muscles.

Landmark technique

Genicular nerve blocks began in chronic pain practice under fluoroscopy, using bony landmarks: in a 2011 sham-controlled trial of ablation, a positive diagnostic block was the entry test.27 Those classical landmarks are inaccurate. In 14 cadaver knees, the distance from the needle tip to the superior medial nerve was a mean 17.8 mm with the classical target against 0.7 mm with a revised one, and accuracy was 0% against 100%. For the superior lateral nerve, the distance to its descending branch was 24.4 against 3.7 mm, and accuracy 35% against 64%.12

A surgical variant exists: in 140 patients, an intraoperative landmark-based genicular block was compared with periarticular infiltration.23 For chronic knee pain, ultrasound and fluoroscopy gave similar pain relief, function and complication rates in 80 patients randomised.31 This page describes the ultrasound technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Position and probe. Supine, with the knee slightly flexed. Use a linear high-frequency probe.21
  2. Superomedial target. Place the probe over the medial distal femur and slide up and down until the shaft meets the medial epicondyle. Find the superior medial genicular artery with colour Doppler; the target is the bone beside it, about 1 cm anterior to the adductor tubercle.5176
  3. Superolateral target. Repeat on the lateral femur. The superior lateral genicular artery lies deep within vastus lateralis, close to the bone, where the shaft meets the lateral condyle.65
  4. Inferomedial target. Move to the medial proximal tibia. The inferior medial genicular artery lies at the junction of shaft and medial condyle, deep to the medial collateral ligament.617
  5. Optional targets. For the inferolateral nerve, inject on the tibia deep to or just below the lateral collateral ligament, at the level of the joint line, keeping well away from the common fibular nerve.196 For the recurrent branch, place the probe between the tibial tuberosity and Gerdy’s tubercle and look for the anterior recurrent tibial artery.6
  6. No artery? Inject on the bone surface at the bony landmark.6

Cadaver

How close does ultrasound get?

In 10 knees, dye placed by ultrasound lay a mean 2.33 mm from the superior lateral, 3.44 mm from the superior medial and 1.32 mm from the inferior medial genicular nerve.11 With revised targets in 10 knees, the paint mark touched the nerve in 7 of 10 (superior medial), 8 of 10 (superior lateral), 10 of 10 (inferior medial) and 9 of 10 (recurrent fibular).29

Cadaver

Even half a millilitre

Injecting 0.5 mL of ink at the medial targets under ultrasound stained both the superior and inferior medial genicular nerves in all six knees tested.17

Cadaver

Where does the fluid go?

With 4 mL at each of four nerves, contrast spread widely around the capsule on CT, following it without entering the joint.30 A quantitative CT study found the spread variable, especially around the recurrent nerve, but the region covered by 80% of the spread closely matched the pericapsular distribution of the genicular nerves.32

Cadaver

The inferolateral nerve

Two millilitres of dye below the lateral collateral ligament stained the inferior lateral genicular nerve in 5 of the 8 knees in which it was found (62.5%). The common fibular nerve was not stained in any specimen.19

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. In plane, usually from anterior to posterior along the probe, through skin and subcutaneous tissue towards the bone.215
  2. Through or beside the muscle at the upper targets (vastus medialis or vastus lateralis) to the periosteum, where the articular branches run.1
  3. Place the tip on bone beside the artery, not in it. Aspirate, then inject slowly, watching fluid lift the tissue off the bone.5
  4. Repeat at each target. Each injection is separate; check the cumulative dose.

Practice note

Nerve stimulation

The genicular nerves are sensory articular branches, so there is no motor twitch to seek and the trials cited here did not use a stimulator. A muscle twitch in the leg or foot suggests the needle is near a mixed nerve and should be withdrawn.

Cadaver

The arteries are the signposts

Three branches of the descending genicular artery ran with the medial nerves in 27 specimens.18 In one 2026 cadaver series, however, 9 of 10 native knees lacked an arterial branch near the superior lateral target, which may make that injection less exact.33

Registry study

Ultrasound and toxicity

In an Australian and New Zealand registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, ultrasound guidance was associated with a lower incidence of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.34

Review and cadaver study

Vessels near the targets

The superior lateral, superior medial and inferior medial genicular arteries lie close to the standard targets. Injuries to them are documented in the surgical literature as pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, haemarthrosis and osteonecrosis of the patella.35

Volumes studied in the cited trials

  • 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.25% with 2 mg dexamethasone at each of the three standard targets, with an adductor canal block.21
  • 5 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% at each of the three standard targets, before knee arthroplasty.8
  • 4 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% at each of five nerves, against 80 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% for local infiltration.6
  • 2.5 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% at each target point.36
  • 2 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% at each of three nerves for knee arthroscopy;4 3 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% in each quadrant except the inferolateral, with an adductor canal block.24
  • 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% for the block, as reported in the abstract of a three-arm trial in mixed knee surgery.28
  • 4 mL at each of four nerves in a case series of 12 arthroplasty patients.30
  • 1 mL per site for a prognostic block before radiofrequency ablation in chronic pain.7

These are the volumes used in the studies, not a dosing recommendation. Whatever is chosen, the total dose of local anaesthetic must stay within the maximum safe dose for the drug, calculated on lean body weight. That matters here because genicular blocks are usually given alongside an adductor canal block, an IPACK block, spinal anaesthesia or surgical infiltration, and the doses add up. In a large registry, dose per weight and the site of injection were among the predictors of toxicity.34 Perineural dexamethasone, used in several of these trials, is an unlicensed use.

Continuous techniques

No trial of a genicular nerve catheter was found for this page; the block is a set of single injections. To extend analgesia, trials added dexamethasone to the local anaesthetic,221 or relied on a continuous adductor canal block running alongside.923

Radiofrequency ablation before surgery does not substitute. In 70 patients, cooled ablation of the three standard nerves 2 to 6 weeks before knee arthroplasty had no effect on opioid use, pain or function at any time up to 6 months, compared with sham.37 A second sham-controlled pilot of conventional ablation in 60 patients, 2 to 4 weeks before surgery, also found no benefit at 2 or 6 weeks.38

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the injection sites, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Caution with anticoagulation: the targets lie beside named arteries.35 The sites are superficial and compressible, so follow the local guideline for superficial blocks.
  • Posterior or extra-articular pain will not be relieved by this block alone.10
  • Caution at the inferolateral target because of the common fibular nerve.19

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Vascular injury is the specific concern, because each target lies beside a genicular artery. No vascular injury after genicular radiofrequency ablation had been reported by 2016, but a review of 27 surgical cases of injury to the arteries near these targets showed how they were distributed. These are cases after surgery, not after genicular nerve block. They show which arteries are at risk, not how often a block injures them.

  • Motor block. None of 132 patients had complete tibial or common fibular sensorimotor block;9 quadriceps strength was similar to periarticular infiltration;5 straight leg raise did not differ from control after arthroscopy;4 and no foot drop was seen with a five-nerve protocol.6
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In a registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks the incidence was 0.87 per 1000.34 Contemporary reports suggest a trend towards delayed presentation.39 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, manage the airway with 100% oxygen, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline; the ASRA advisory sets out the same principles.39 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not genicular blocks alone.
  • Nerve injury. Block-related nerve injury occurred in 0.4 per 1000 blocks across all block types in the Australasian audit, and most postoperative neurological symptoms were unrelated to the block.40
  • After radiofrequency ablation (chronic pain). Septic arthritis, injury to the pes anserinus tendons, a third-degree skin burn and significant haematoma or haemarthrosis have been reported.41 In a national database, swelling at 30 days was more common after ablation than after intra-articular steroid (9.4% against 6.4%), while infection and septic arthritis were rare and no more frequent than after intra-articular injections.42
  • Steroid in chronic pain blocks. Adding triamcinolone to lidocaine lowered pain scores at 2 and 4 weeks, but the authors judged its benefit unclear against lidocaine alone and questioned its routine use.26

Pearls of wisdom

  • Find the artery first with colour Doppler; the nerve runs with it. If you cannot see one, inject on the bone at the junction of shaft and condyle.65
  • The superomedial target sits about 1 cm anterior to the adductor tubercle, not at the old fluoroscopic point.1712
  • Stay on the periosteum. The articular branches run there; only the nerves to vastus lateralis and vastus medialis do not.1
  • Think twice about the inferolateral target. The common fibular nerve is close; in cadavers the injection reached the nerve in only 62.5%.19
  • Pair it with something for the back of the knee. A genicular block plus IPACK beat IPACK alone for pain on movement at 4 and 8 hours.910
  • Add up every source of local anaesthetic: canal block, IPACK, spinal and surgical infiltration.34
  • Know what it adds in your pathway. With an adductor canal block, IPACK and full multimodal analgesia, a 2026 cohort of several thousand patients found no clinically meaningful gain.3
  • For chronic pain, a 1 mL prognostic block did not improve the success of later cooled ablation.7

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Does it add anything to a full pathway?

Placebo-controlled trials say yes: a 2026 trial of 64 patients found less pain, less morphine (26.5 against 50 mg over 48 hours) and more flexion at 24 hours (115° against 97.5°).8 A meta-analysis of six trials found lower pain but no difference in morphine or flexion.22 And in a propensity-matched cohort of 2803 inpatient and 1102 outpatient pairs already given a canal block and IPACK, differences in opioid use and in time to physiotherapy clearance (21 against 22 hours for inpatients) were statistically significant but not clinically important.3

Three nerves or five?

Revised and expanded targets have been validated in cadavers.29 In a trial of 55 patients with chronic osteoarthritis pain, revised targets gave more responders at 1 hour (100% against 82.1% with classical targets); after that the difference was no longer significant, and both techniques relieved pain up to 12 weeks. The authors suggested the larger therapeutic volume made up for less precise targeting.48 Perioperative trials comparing three with five targets are still awaited.

Radiofrequency ablation for chronic knee pain

Distinct from the perioperative block. Against sham, eight trials (627 patients) showed lower pain at 12 weeks (mean difference −1.65) and better function.47 Cooled ablation gave at least 50% pain relief at 6 months in 71% against 38% after a hyaluronic acid injection.49 In a pilot comparing cooled with conventional ablation, about 22% in each group had at least 50% relief at 12 months.50

Prosthetic knees

In a 2026 cadaver comparison of 10 native and 10 prosthetic knees, the targets were broadly the same, but the nerves were thinner, the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve was caught in scar, and the superior medial target was nearer the joint line (4.00 against 4.53 cm).33 This matters for treating persistent pain after arthroplasty.
