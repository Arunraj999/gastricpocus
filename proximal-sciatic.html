Proximal sciatic nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed around the sciatic nerve between the greater sciatic foramen and the top of the thigh, by a parasacral, transgluteal, subgluteal or anterior approach. Higher up, the nerve is reached before the hamstring branches leave it, smaller volumes have been effective than at the knee, and the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve can be included, depending on the approach.12 The price is depth: the nerve lies under thick muscle, a curvilinear probe is needed, and the needle path is long.34

Level of difficulty

Advanced block

The nerve is large but deep, under gluteus maximus or, from the front, under the quadriceps and adductors. A low-frequency curvilinear probe is the rule. In 15 volunteers scanned with a 2 to 5 MHz curved probe, the nerve was seen at the gluteal, infragluteal and proximal thigh levels, and easily seen in 87%.3 Even with ultrasound, the authors of a 2013 trial noted that sciatic nerve block remains among the least performed peripheral blocks.12 The anterior and parasacral approaches add depth and nearby vessels; the subgluteal approach is the usual starting point. The ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus recommends a template that names each limb block by the nerve and the place where it is blocked, and the approaches on this page are named in that way.13

Anatomy

The sciatic nerve is formed from the L4 to S3 roots on the front of the sacrum and leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, usually below piriformis. It passes down between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity, deep to gluteus maximus and on the short external rotators and quadratus femoris, then runs into the back of the thigh on adductor magnus, under the long head of biceps femoris. It carries two divisions in one sheath, tibial (medial) and common peroneal (lateral), which separate above the popliteal fossa. The branches to the hamstrings leave it in the thigh.

How it leaves the pelvis. In a meta-analysis of 45 studies and 7068 limbs, the nerve left the pelvis as a single trunk below piriformis in 85.2%. In 9.8% it had already divided, the common peroneal part piercing piriformis and the tibial part passing below it. The pooled width of the nerve at the lower border of piriformis was 15.55 mm.14 Across 6062 cadavers, a piriformis and sciatic nerve anomaly was present in 16.9%.15 A high division explains why a proximal injection can sometimes block one division and miss the other.

More nerve, less padding. In serial sections from five cadavers, neural tissue made up 67% of the nerve's cross-section in the mid-gluteal region, 57% subgluteally, and 46% at mid-thigh and in the popliteal fossa: a ratio of about 2:1 proximally and 1:1 distally. The authors suggested that this may make the proximal nerve more vulnerable to injury and may explain the smaller volumes and faster onset seen with proximal blocks.16

The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve leaves the pelvis with the sciatic nerve and supplies the skin of the back of the thigh. At the infragluteal crease, in eight cadaver thighs, it lay deep to gluteus maximus, slightly superficial or lateral to the sciatic nerve, and the two were separated by the deep investing fascia of the thigh. Coloured latex injected around each nerve mixed substantially with the other in only two thighs.17 This is why the more distal and anterior approaches miss it more often.218

The subgluteal space. Between gluteus maximus and quadratus femoris lies a tissue plane that contains the sciatic nerve. An injection into this plane, first described with ultrasound in a series of five patients, spreads around the nerve.9

The parasacral level. At the greater sciatic foramen the sciatic nerve lies with the sacral plexus, deep to gluteus maximus and piriformis, close to the inferior gluteal vessels and next to the posterior border of the ischium.8 In 17 patients the gluteal arteries were identified in 10, and piriformis in none.19 In a cadaver, dye injected into the deep gluteal compartment, between piriformis and quadratus femoris, reached the superior gluteal nerve and the nerve to quadratus femoris as well as the sciatic nerve.20

Surface anatomy

Posterior approaches are done with the patient lateral, block side up, hip and knee flexed, or prone. The bony points are the posterior superior iliac spine, the greater trochanter, the ischial tuberosity and the gluteal crease. For the parasacral scan in one series the probe was placed on an axial plane 8 cm lateral to the uppermost point of the gluteal cleft, and the curved line of the posterior border of the ischium was the guide.8 For the transgluteal and subgluteal scans the probe lies across the line between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity, or just below it at the gluteal crease.119

The anterior approach is done supine, which avoids turning a patient in pain. The probe sits on the anteromedial upper thigh, a few centimetres below the inguinal crease, at about the level of the lesser trochanter. In 45 patients the nerve was best seen, and was shallower, with the hip externally rotated to 45 degrees and flexed.21

Indications

  • Foot, ankle and lower leg surgery, with a saphenous or femoral block, when a proximal approach is preferred to the popliteal one. Onset was faster than at the knee in a trial of 75 patients.6
  • Analgesia after knee arthroplasty, for the back of the knee, added to a femoral block. A meta-analysis of 8 trials found less morphine use in the first 24 hours.5
  • Lower limb amputation, often with a catheter, in high-risk patients.7
  • Hip surgery in high-risk patients, as one part of a combination with lumbar plexus, femoral, obturator or fascia iliaca blocks.102223
  • When the back of the thigh matters, for example a thigh tourniquet or posterior thigh incision; choose a parasacral, transgluteal or lateral approach for the best chance of covering the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve.24218

Nerves blocked

The sciatic nerve, with both its tibial and common peroneal divisions, and the branches to the hamstrings that arise from it in the thigh. The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve is included in a proportion of patients that depends on the approach: 91% with Labat's transgluteal approach,24 68.1% subgluteally against 14.9% anteriorly,2 and 60% with a lateral approach against 15% anteriorly.18 Injectate in the deep gluteal compartment can also reach the superior gluteal nerve and the nerve to quadratus femoris.20

The obturator nerve is not reliably blocked by a parasacral injection. In a randomised trial of 40 patients for knee surgery, adductor strength was lower when an obturator block was added to the parasacral block than with the parasacral block alone, and those patients had less pain and needed less sedation and analgesia.29 Plan a separate obturator block when it matters.

The saphenous nerve and the rest of the femoral territory always need their own block.

What the block covers

The leg below the knee and the foot except the saphenous strip, the back of the knee, and the back of the thigh in a proportion of patients that depends on the approach.

Landmark technique

The proximal sciatic blocks were landmark and nerve stimulator techniques long before ultrasound. The classic transgluteal approach of Labat draws a line from the posterior superior iliac spine to the greater trochanter and enters perpendicular to its midpoint, a few centimetres caudad; Winnie added a third line from the greater trochanter to the sacral hiatus. A narrative review of 46 trials concluded that the classic transgluteal approach was reliable, and that the subgluteal approach deserved consideration because nerve location and catheter placement could be quicker.31 The parasacral approach was described by Mansour in 1993.32

The anterior landmark approach is the hardest. On 100 pelvic radiographs, the classic anterior landmarks pointed at the lesser trochanter in 51% and directly at the femur in 42%. With modified landmarks in 200 patients having knee replacement, the nerve was blocked in 196 (98%), at a median depth of 9.5 cm, but with bone contact in 17.5% and vascular puncture in 5%.4 Adjusting the entry point to the patient's height gave complete sciatic and posterior femoral cutaneous blocks in 92% of 53 patients, at a median depth of 12 cm.33

Ultrasound has largely replaced these techniques. A meta-analysis of 10 trials of sciatic block found a higher success rate with ultrasound than with nerve stimulation (risk ratio 1.22) and less vascular puncture (risk ratio 0.13), with no difference in procedure time.34 This page does not teach a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

Use a curvilinear probe (about 2 to 5 MHz) and a long insulated needle, typically 100 mm and 150 mm for the anterior approach in larger patients. The nerve is a flattened oval or triangular bright structure. Combine ultrasound with a nerve stimulator: the needle is long and the image of the tip is often poor at this depth.

Interactive scan and needle path to follow.

  1. Choose the approach. Posterior thigh skin or a tourniquet: parasacral or transgluteal. Patient cannot turn: anterior. Most other cases: subgluteal.21821
  2. Subgluteal and transgluteal. Place the probe across the line from the greater trochanter to the ischial tuberosity. Find the two bony points, gluteus maximus above, and quadratus femoris below. The nerve lies in the plane between them, nearer the ischial tuberosity.119
  3. Parasacral. Start about 8 cm lateral to the top of the gluteal cleft in an axial plane. Find the curved posterior border of the ischium; the nerve is the bright structure just medial to it, deep to piriformis and lateral to the inferior gluteal vessels. Follow it down onto the back of the ischium if unsure.8 Run colour Doppler for the gluteal arteries.19
  4. Anterior. Externally rotate and flex the hip. Find the femur and lesser trochanter laterally; the nerve lies posteromedial to the femur, deep to adductor magnus. It is deeper than from behind.212
  5. Confirm the nerve. Tilt the probe, scan up and down, and stimulate: a tibial or common peroneal twitch confirms it.3
  6. Inject and watch the spread. Aim for spread around the nerve; reposition if it goes only to one side.28

RCT

One injection or several

In 90 patients (86 analysed) given 20 mL of mepivacaine 1.5% with adrenaline subgluteally, complete sensory block of all sciatic territories at 30 minutes was more frequent when the needle was moved to surround the nerve than with one injection on its back surface (41.9% against 16.3%). Complete motor block of foot extension was 67.4% against 34.9%. Multiple injections took longer.28

RCT

Plane or nerve

In 27 overweight and obese patients for knee arthroplasty, injecting 30 mL into the plane between gluteus maximus and quadratus femoris took a mean 4.4 minutes, against 9.0 minutes for an injection around the nerve at the infragluteal level. Needle passes and procedural pain were lower, with no detectable difference in success or analgesia.12

RCT

Front or back

In 100 patients for knee surgery, the anterior approach found the nerve deeper but was as quick for the sciatic injection and quicker for the whole set of blocks. Success, onset and duration in the tibial and peroneal territories did not differ; the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve was blocked in 14.9% against 68.1%.2

RCT

A parasacral shortcut

The parasacral parallel shift starts with the probe over the ilium, between the posterior superior iliac spine and the greater trochanter, and slides it without rotation until the bone line breaks at the greater sciatic notch, where the sacral plexus lies under piriformis.3536 Against the classical sacral plexus approach in 144 patients given 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, it shortened scanning, needling and block performance time, gave faster sensory and motor onset, and more often a complete sciatic block.36

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. Posterior approaches: in plane from lateral to medial, through gluteus maximus, towards the lateral edge of the nerve, or out of plane at the gluteal crease.
  2. Anterior approach: in plane from the anteromedial thigh, or out of plane, passing medial to the femur and away from the femoral vessels.
  3. Set the stimulator to stop at about 0.5 mA; a twitch below about 0.5 mA with the tip next to the nerve fits with the trial data.3711
  4. Inject slowly, aspirate and watch. High resistance, pain or swelling of the nerve on the screen means stop and withdraw.3839

Volunteer study

Ultrasound to the nerve, stimulator to confirm

In 15 volunteers the needle reached the nerve within two attempts in all, and stimulation then succeeded at a mean threshold of 0.42 mA, producing plantar flexion or dorsiflexion of the foot.3

Retrospective

Which twitch, which current

In 269 ultrasound-guided subgluteal blocks, a tibial or a common peroneal response made no difference to onset or duration. A minimal current below 0.5 mA was associated with faster onset and, with mepivacaine, a longer block.37

Observational

Unintended intraneural injection

Video review of 325 subgluteal blocks found intraneural injection in 46 patients (16.3%). Onset was faster, duration the same, and no patient had a nerve injury.39 Faster onset is not a reason to aim inside the nerve.

Cadaver

Injection pressure

In ten fresh cadavers, across five nerves including the subgluteal sciatic nerve, opening injection pressure for intraneural injections was 21.5 to 25.8 psi, against 3.8 to 6.1 psi for injections outside the nerve.38

Meta-analysis

Diabetes and the stimulator

Across six studies and 486 participants, the stimulation threshold for sciatic block was a mean 0.57 mA higher in people with diabetes, particularly with neuropathy or a diabetic foot. The evidence was rated low.40 Rely on the image as well as the twitch.

Volumes studied. Trials on this page used 10 mL of lidocaine 1.33% with adrenaline at the parasacral level as part of a combined block,10 20 mL of mepivacaine 1.5% subgluteally or anteriorly,22839 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% at the sacral plexus,36 20 mL of ropivacaine 4.75 mg/mL subgluteally,41 25 mL of mepivacaine 1%,24 and 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or 0.75%, or of a lidocaine and bupivacaine mixture.42612 Dose-finding studies show that smaller volumes can work proximally.111 These are study protocols, not recommendations.

Duration. In 72 patients given 20 mL of ropivacaine 4.75 mg/mL subgluteally, the median sensory block lasted 21 hours in patients with type 2 diabetes and early neuropathy, against 17 hours without diabetes; motor block 16 against 12 hours.41 Warming 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% to 30 °C shortened median sensory onset from 22 to 16 minutes, without changing duration, in 54 patients.42

Maximum safe dose. A proximal sciatic block is almost always given with a femoral, saphenous, lumbar plexus or other block, so add the doses together. Calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient, based on lean body weight as recommended by ASRA guidance, and stay below it.43 In a registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, the site of injection, dose per weight and patient weight were among the predictors of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.44 Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk.45 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, maintain the airway and give oxygen, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline.4345

Continuous techniques

Subgluteal against popliteal catheters. After foot surgery in 60 patients, subgluteal and popliteal catheters gave similar analgesia; displacement or occlusion occurred in 2 and 4 patients respectively.26 After hallux valgus repair in 56 patients, analgesia was again similar, but local anaesthetic use was lower subgluteally (3.8 against 4.9 mL per hour of levobupivacaine 0.0625%).25

Knee arthroplasty. In the meta-analysis of femoral block with or without sciatic block, a continuous sciatic block reduced intravenous morphine in the first 24 hours by a mean 20.5 mg, reduced pain at rest up to 36 hours and on movement up to 48 hours, and cut the odds of nausea and vomiting. The trials were very heterogeneous.5 With modified anterior landmarks, sciatic catheters were inserted in 200 patients having knee replacement and the nerve was blocked in 196.4

Parasacral catheters. A continuous parasacral ischial plane block after knee arthroplasty was placed successfully in 90% of patients, and 96.3% of catheters were still in place on day 3.46 In a later trial of 100 patients, catheter problems were failure 4.1%, leakage 2.2%, dislodgement 4.3% and clogging 6.7%.47

Amputation. In 65 high-risk patients having lower limb amputation under transgluteal sciatic and femoral catheters, 58 (89%) needed no general anaesthetic.7

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the site, and allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Anticoagulation. The parasacral and anterior approaches are deep and the vessels are not compressible; apply local anticoagulation guidance for deep blocks.
  • Surgery where early detection of a common peroneal or tibial nerve injury, or of compartment syndrome, matters, such as some knee and tibial procedures; agree a plan with the surgeon first.
  • Pre-existing sciatic neuropathy, which should be documented before the block. In diabetes the block may last longer and stimulation thresholds are higher.4140
  • Patients who must walk early, because the leg will be weak, including the hamstrings.

Apart from the referenced points, these are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

These studies used different techniques, guidance and definitions, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Nerve injury. The proximal sciatic nerve has a higher proportion of neural tissue than the distal nerve, which may make it more vulnerable.16 In a registry of 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks of all types, neurological symptoms lasted more than 6 months after 0.9 per 1000 blocks.49 Unintended intraneural injection was common in one subgluteal series but caused no injury there.39
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks the incidence was 0.87 per 1000, lower with ultrasound.44 In 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks seizures occurred in 0.08 per 1000.49 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation.45 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not this block alone. Management is set out under Block performance.
  • Vascular puncture. The inferior gluteal vessels lie next to the nerve at the parasacral level,819 and the femoral and profunda vessels lie in the path of the anterior approach. Ultrasound reduced vascular puncture against nerve stimulation in a meta-analysis (risk ratio 0.13).34
  • Hypotension with combined lumbar and sacral plexus blocks in elderly patients: mean arterial pressure fell by 27% initially, against 38% with spinal anaesthesia (not significantly different), but ephedrine was needed more often after spinal.10
  • Falls and a numb, weak leg. Hamstring and calf weakness, and loss of protective sensation in the foot, last as long as the block. Protect the heel and plan mobilisation.
  • Masking of surgical nerve injury. A complete sciatic block hides foot drop after knee or leg surgery; this is a clinical consideration rather than a measured outcome.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Decide what you need from the back of the thigh before you choose the approach. In the trials, the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve was blocked in most transgluteal, subgluteal and lateral blocks but rarely from the front; parasacral injections should also reach it, as it arises from the sacral plexus.24218
  • Covering the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve did not make a thigh tourniquet more comfortable in a trial of 120 patients. Do not promise tourniquet comfort from the sciatic block alone.24
  • For the parasacral scan, find the curved line of the ischium first; the nerve sits just medial to it.8
  • If the spread goes only to one side of the nerve, move the needle. Circumferential spread more than doubled the rate of complete sensory block.28
  • In large patients, a plane injection between gluteus maximus and quadratus femoris is quicker than chasing the nerve, with no detectable difference in success.12
  • For the anterior approach, rotate the hip outwards and flex it; the nerve comes nearer and is easier to see.21
  • A parasacral block does not dependably block the obturator nerve. Block it separately for knee surgery.29
  • Expect a higher stimulation threshold and a longer block in patients with diabetic neuropathy.4041

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

The sacral plexus as a target

Parasacral techniques are being refined: the parasacral parallel shift was quicker than the classical approach,36 and the parasacral ischial plane block has been used with catheters after knee arthroplasty.46 A single injection of liposomal bupivacaine by this route was non-inferior to a catheter for pain, though the catheter group used less opioid and rehabilitated better early.47

Hip fracture without a spinal

In 98 elderly patients, a suprainguinal fascia iliaca block with a sacral plexus block gave better early analgesia and less hypotension than spinal anaesthesia.23 Earlier work combined femoral, obturator, lateral femoral cutaneous and parasacral blocks for hemiarthroplasty in 20 patients, all with adequate anaesthesia.22 A deep posterior gluteal compartment block for the posterior hip capsule is at the proof-of-concept stage.20

Making it last

Anterior sciatic block with liposomal bupivacaine gave lower pain scores at 24 to 72 hours than ropivacaine after lower leg fracture fixation, but higher scores at 6 hours.27 Perineural dexamethasone prolonged lower limb block analgesia by only 2.73 hours more than intravenous dexamethasone, a difference unlikely to be clinically relevant, and perineural use remains off label.50

Outside theatre

Emergency physicians have used the transgluteal block for acute sciatica: in 63 patients median pain fell from 9 to 5, with one transient foot drop. There was no control group.48
