Femoral nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed around the femoral nerve at the inguinal crease, where the nerve lies deep to the fascia iliaca and lateral to the femoral artery. It relieves pain from the front of the thigh, the knee and the femur, and it is one of the small set of blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.1 The price is the quadriceps: in volunteers a femoral block cut quadriceps strength by 49% from baseline, against 8% with an adductor canal block.2

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The femoral nerve block is one of the Plan A blocks. Delphi studies have supported it as a key technique, and the framework has influenced the Royal College of Anaesthetists' curriculum.1 At the inguinal crease the nerve is broad and shallow: in cadavers it was 14.0 mm wide and 6.8 mm deep to the fascia lata, against 9.8 mm wide and 26.4 mm deep at the inguinal ligament.6 Emergency department doctors of all grades learned and consolidated the skill after an accreditation programme.12 The difficulty lies less in the needle than in the decision: for many operations a block that spares the quadriceps now does the same job.1314

Anatomy

The femoral nerve is the largest branch of the lumbar plexus, formed from the posterior divisions of the second, third and fourth lumbar ventral rami. It runs down in the groove between psoas major and iliacus, covered by the fascia iliaca, and passes beneath the inguinal ligament into the thigh lateral to the femoral artery. It lies outside the femoral sheath, which holds the artery and vein, so a layer of fascia separates nerve from vessels. Within a few centimetres it fans out into an anterior division (the anterior cutaneous nerves of the thigh and the branch to sartorius) and a posterior division (the branches to the quadriceps and the saphenous nerve). The saphenous nerve travels with the femoral artery through the femoral triangle and the adductor canal to supply the skin of the medial leg. This paragraph follows standard anatomical descriptions.

Where to find it. In a cadaver study of 17 femoral triangles, a needle placed at the inguinal crease immediately lateral to the femoral artery contacted the nerve in 71% of attempts, more often than at the inguinal ligament or 2 cm further lateral. At the crease the nerve was wider and lay much closer to the fascia lata.6

Arteries on the needle path. In 100 cadavers at least one superficial and one deep circumflex iliac artery was found around every femoral triangle. Every deep circumflex iliac artery ran between the fascia lata and the fascia iliaca, which is the layer the needle crosses on its way to the nerve. In 94% of femoral triangles at least one arterial branch ran laterally from the femoral artery between the inguinal ligament and its first bifurcation.15 That first bifurcation, usually the deep femoral artery, is regarded as the distal limit for the block.15

The three-in-one idea. A large femoral injection was once thought to spread upwards to the lumbar plexus and block the femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves together. Magnetic resonance imaging after 30 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% showed no cephalad spread and no lumbar plexus block. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve was reached by lateral spread and only the anterior branch of the obturator nerve by slight medial spread.10 The obturator nerve had no skin territory at all in 57% of 30 patients, so numbness of the medial thigh is a sign of femoral, not obturator, block. Adductor strength is the only reliable test.16

Femoral triangle or adductor canal. In 22 volunteers the midpoint of the thigh lay a mean 4.6 cm proximal to the start of the adductor canal in every case, so an injection there is a femoral triangle block.17 The ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus names lower limb blocks by the nerve and the place where it is blocked.18

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg straight and slightly turned out. The probe sits across the thigh along the inguinal crease, over the pulse of the femoral artery. The nerve lies lateral to the artery; at this level it is wider and lies closer to the fascia lata than at the inguinal ligament.6 Scan above the point where the deep femoral artery leaves the femoral artery.15 The needle usually enters in plane from the lateral end of the probe and travels from lateral to medial.15

Indications

  • Hip fracture, from the emergency department onwards. In a Cochrane review of 49 trials and 3061 patients, nerve blocks reduced pain on movement within 30 minutes of placement by the equivalent of 2.5 points on a 0 to 10 scale. They reduced acute confusion (risk ratio 0.67, number needed to treat 12) and probably chest infection (risk ratio 0.41), and probably brought first mobilisation forward by 10.8 hours.3 Femoral blocks specifically lowered pain and opioid use in emergency and ward trials, including in patients with dementia.121920 In one trial of 236 patients, preoperative delirium affected 38.9% with a femoral block and 49.2% without, a difference that did not reach significance.21
  • Positioning for spinal anaesthesia after femoral shaft or hip fracture.58
  • Knee replacement. Femoral block gave better analgesia than opioid PCA alone and analgesia similar to epidural.4 Current procedure-specific guidance prefers an adductor canal block with local infiltration analgesia.13
  • Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, alone or with a sciatic block, accepting the effect on quadriceps function.2223
  • Children with femoral fractures. Evidence is thin: a Cochrane review found only one eligible trial, and that trial used a fascia iliaca block.24

Nerves blocked

The femoral nerve and all of its branches below the inguinal ligament: the muscular branches to the quadriceps, sartorius and pectineus, the anterior cutaneous nerves of the thigh, the saphenous nerve, and the articular branches to the hip and knee. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve is reached only when the injectate spreads laterally, and the obturator nerve only through its anterior branch, inconsistently.1016 In volunteers, a femoral block reduced adductor strength compared with placebo, while an adductor canal block did not.2

The sciatic nerve is not affected, so the back of the thigh, the posterior knee capsule and the leg and foot outside the saphenous strip need a separate block.

What the block covers

The front of the thigh, the front and inner side of the knee, and a strip of skin down the medial leg.

Landmark technique

The classic approach places the needle at the inguinal crease just lateral to the femoral pulse and seeks a quadriceps twitch with a nerve stimulator. In cadavers that point gave the highest rate of needle-to-nerve contact, 71%.6 Emergency doctors in a UK district general hospital gave a three-in-one block to patients with a fractured neck of femur: pain reached its lowest point sooner (2.88 against 5.81 hours) and less morphine was needed (0.49 against 1.17 mg per hour).12

Ultrasound improves on this. It cut the minimum effective volume of ropivacaine 0.5% from 26 to 15 mL, a reduction of 42%.7 In 40 patients with hip trauma, onset was faster with ultrasound (16 against 27 minutes), and the three arterial punctures all occurred in the nerve stimulator group.25 Across upper and lower limb trials, ultrasound gave more blocks adequate for surgery (odds ratio 2.94) and fewer vascular punctures (odds ratio 0.19).26 This page does not describe a landmark technique further.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Place a linear probe across the inguinal crease and find the femoral artery, with the vein on its medial side.6
  2. Slide up or down until there is a single femoral artery. Stay above the origin of the deep femoral artery.15
  3. Name the layers. Fascia lata near the surface; the fascia iliaca deeper, running over iliopsoas; the femoral nerve as a flat, bright, triangular or oval structure lateral to the artery, under the fascia iliaca.
  4. Run colour Doppler along the planned needle path. Circumflex iliac branches can cross it, superficial to the fascia iliaca.15
  5. Advance in plane from lateral to medial and pierce the fascia iliaca lateral to the nerve.15
  6. Inject and watch the spread under the fascia iliaca along the nerve. Wrapping the nerve completely is not needed.8

RCT

Above, below or all round the nerve

In 52 patients with hip fracture given 15 mL of lidocaine 2%, pain on positioning for spinal anaesthesia was similar whether the injection was aimed above, below or around the nerve. Failure rates were 20%, 47% and 12%; the circumferential approach needed more needle passes. The authors found no clinical advantage in aiming for circumferential spread.8

RCT

Ultrasound lowers the volume

Ropivacaine 0.5%, up-and-down allocation in 60 patients having knee arthroscopy: the volume effective in half the patients was 15 mL with ultrasound and 26 mL with nerve stimulation. The estimated volume for 95% was 22 and 41 mL.7

RCT

Faster onset, fewer arterial punctures

Twenty millilitres of bupivacaine 0.5% in 40 patients after hip trauma: onset 16 against 27 minutes, good analgesia in 95% against 85%, and arterial puncture only with the nerve stimulator.25

Cadaver study

Vessels you may not expect

Deep circumflex iliac arteries ran between the fascia lata and fascia iliaca in all 100 cadavers examined. A lateral arterial branch arose from the femoral artery above its first bifurcation in 94% of femoral triangles.15

Needle path and nerve stimulator

The needle passes through skin, subcutaneous fat and the fascia lata, then through the fascia iliaca into the plane on iliopsoas beside the nerve. With a stimulator, the expected response is a quadriceps contraction with upward movement of the patella; a sartorius twitch alone means the needle is near the anterior division rather than the branches to the quadriceps. These responses follow standard descriptions.

Cadaver study

The crease, not the ligament

At the inguinal crease beside the artery the needle met the nerve in 71% of attempts. The nerve was wider there (14.0 against 9.8 mm) and nearer the fascia lata (6.8 against 26.4 mm) than at the inguinal ligament.6

MRI study

Where a large volume goes

Thirty millilitres given with a nerve stimulator spread laterally, caudally and slightly medially, never upwards to the lumbar plexus.10

Systematic review

Ultrasound and vessels

Against other methods of nerve location, ultrasound reduced vascular puncture (odds ratio 0.19, 387 participants).26

Registry study

Ultrasound and toxicity

In 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, toxicity occurred in 0.87 per 1000. Ultrasound was associated with a lower risk; site of injection, dose per weight and patient weight were predictors.27

Maximum dose. Work out the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient, based on lean body weight as ASRA advises,28 and include every other source of local anaesthetic given in the same period. Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk of toxicity.29

Continuous techniques

Better analgesia than a single shot. After knee replacement, four trials with 272 patients showed less pain with a catheter at 24 hours, at rest and on movement, and three trials with 236 patients showed 13.8 mg less morphine equivalent.4 In 36 patients a catheter lowered pain scores and oxycodone use (15 against 40 mg on the first day), but length of stay (3.8 against 3.9 days) and knee flexion at 12 weeks were unchanged.30 Sending patients home with a 4-day ambulatory infusion of ropivacaine 0.2% shortened the median time to meet discharge criteria from 71 to 25 hours, compared with an overnight infusion.31

The quadriceps stays weak. In volunteers, ropivacaine 0.1% at 4 mL per hour reduced quadriceps strength to 29 to 30% of baseline at 6 hours, whether the catheter tip lay in front of or behind the nerve; an anterior tip gave denser skin anaesthesia.32 Giving the same drug as hourly boluses rather than a basal infusion made no difference (strength fell by 83% against 84%).33

Infection. Of 208 femoral catheters cultured at 48 hours, 57% were colonised, but there was no cellulitis or abscess.34 In 1416 patients with peripheral catheters, 28.7% of cultures were positive and one psoas abscess (0.07%) occurred, in a woman with diabetes.35

Contraindications

  • Infection or broken skin at the groin, and patient refusal.
  • Previous vascular surgery or a graft in the femoral triangle calls for particular care with the needle path.
  • When early independent walking matters more than dense analgesia. PROSPECT does not recommend femoral block after elective hip replacement because adverse effects outweigh benefit,14 and prefers an adductor canal block for knee replacement.13

The first two points are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Quadriceps weakness is the expected side effect rather than a complication. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

Fall in quadriceps strength from baseline. The studies used different drugs, doses and measures, so compare within a study and not across them. After knee replacement, quadriceps strength fell to 16% of the post-operative baseline after a femoral block and rose to 193% after an adductor canal block, because pain relief allowed a stronger contraction.9

  • Falls. In three pooled placebo-controlled trials of femoral catheters after hip and knee replacement, 6 of 85 patients given ropivacaine fell (7 falls) against none of 86 given saline.11 A meta-analysis found more falls with continuous lumbar plexus blockade (Peto odds ratio 3.85; number needed to harm 59).36 In 191,570 knee replacements, falls occurred in 1.6% and peripheral nerve block was not significantly associated with them (odds ratio 0.85).37 After a femoral block, 7 of 25 patients could not do a timed up-and-go test, against none after an adductor canal block.9
  • Nerve injury. In a prospective audit of more than 7000 blocks, block-related nerve injury occurred in 0.4 per 1000, and most new neurological symptoms after surgery were unrelated to the block.38 In an ultrasound registry, symptoms lasting over 6 months followed 0.9 per 1000 blocks.39 In a multicentre study of 1416 patients with nerve catheters, three neural lesions (0.21%) followed continuous femoral blocks; all resolved within 10 weeks.35 For hip fracture blocks, the Cochrane review found low risks of permanent injury.3
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral blocks in an Australasian registry, lower with ultrasound;27 seizures in 0.08 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks.39 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation, partly from continuous infusions.29 These figures cover all peripheral blocks. If toxicity occurs, stop injecting, call for help, secure the airway, treat seizures promptly and give lipid emulsion early, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline.28
  • Vascular puncture. Twelve vascular punctures in 211 femoral catheter insertions;34 unintended arterial puncture in 1.2 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks of all types.39
  • Catheter colonisation is common (57% and 28.7% in two series), serious infection rare.3435
  • Strength after ligament reconstruction. In a cohort of 124 young patients, those given a femoral block had larger strength deficits at 6 months and fewer met return-to-sport criteria (67.7% against 90.2%). This was not randomised.23

Pearls of wisdom

  • Block at the inguinal crease, beside the artery and above the deep femoral branch. The nerve is wider and shallower there.615
  • Put colour Doppler on the needle path before you puncture. A circumflex iliac artery may sit right under the fascia lata.15
  • The injection must be under the fascia iliaca. Chasing a perfect ring around the nerve adds passes, not success.8
  • Do not call it a three-in-one block. The obturator nerve is rarely covered, and medial thigh numbness does not prove it is; test the adductors.1016
  • Block the fractured hip early, in the emergency department if you can. Dementia need not be a reason to withhold it.320
  • Tell the patient, the nurses and the physiotherapists that the leg will give way. With a catheter running, assume the patient is at risk of falling.112
  • Before a femoral block for a knee or elective hip, ask whether a motor-sparing block will do the same job.131440
  • Calculate the maximum dose from lean body weight in small, frail patients.2829

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Hip fracture: femoral, fascia iliaca or PENG

A 2026 meta-analysis of 19 trials and 1059 patients found no outcome difference large enough to matter clinically and judged the evidence insufficient to call PENG superior to femoral or fascia iliaca blocks. None of the trials reported time to mobilisation.47 A single 2026 trial favoured PENG with a lateral femoral cutaneous block over both.45

Knee replacement: how far down the thigh

A 2023 three-arm trial found femoral block weakest at 6 hours but with the lowest pain scores to 48 hours, and no difference in function.43 The trade-off between quadriceps sparing and analgesia is still being refined.

Ligament reconstruction

A network meta-analysis of 57 trials and 4069 patients ranked femoral plus sciatic block highest for reducing opioid use; adductor canal block performed poorly.22 Its effect on long-term strength is unresolved.23

Adjuncts and training

Perineural dexamethasone lengthened lower limb block analgesia by a mean 2.73 hours over intravenous dexamethasone, a difference unlikely to be clinically relevant; the perineural route remains off label.49 The 2026 Plan A review keeps the femoral block among the key techniques for all anaesthetists.1
