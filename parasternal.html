Parasternal intercostal plane blocks: superficial and deep

Local anaesthetic placed beside the sternum, either just under pectoralis major or deep to the internal intercostal muscle, to reach the anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves that supply the sternum.1 In the largest placebo-controlled trial, 318 patients after sternotomy, bilateral superficial catheters cut opioid use over 72 hours by 20.7 morphine milligram equivalents.2 Pooled across 27 trials the gain is real but small,3 and two trials found no better quality of recovery.45

Level of difficulty

Superficial: basic to intermediate Deep: intermediate

The superficial block is a shallow injection under pectoralis major, above the intercostal muscles and costal cartilages. Authors writing on artery safety consider it the safer of the two, because it stays further from the internal thoracic artery and the pleura, though they note this has not been proven.1 The deep block puts the needle tip in the layer that holds the internal thoracic artery, a few millimetres above the pleura. In a cadaver study the artery lay a median of 1.9 mm from the rib, separated only by soft tissue,10 and in a three-arm trial pneumothorax was more frequent after the deep block.4 Learn the superficial block first.

Anatomy

Names, old and new

The ASRA-ESRA Delphi consensus on chest wall blocks (2021) replaced a crowd of names with two.7

  • Superficial parasternal intercostal plane block (SPIP). Local anaesthetic between pectoralis major and the external intercostal muscle beside the sternum. Former names: pecto-intercostal fascial block (PIFB), first described in 2014 for breast analgesia,11 and the parasternal block.12
  • Deep parasternal intercostal plane block (DPIP). Local anaesthetic between the internal intercostal muscle and transversus thoracis. Former name: transversus thoracic (or transversus thoracis) muscle plane block, described in 2015 as a way to block the anterior branches of T2 to T6.13

Older trials and meta-analyses use the former names; this page uses the consensus names throughout, and notes the original term where a study used one.1415

The nerves

The skin and bone of the sternum and the medial chest are supplied by the anterior cutaneous branches of the T2 to T6 intercostal nerves.1 Each intercostal nerve runs forward in its space. Near the sternum it lies deep to the internal intercostal muscle, on transversus thoracis, then turns forward, pierces the internal intercostal muscle, the external intercostal membrane and pectoralis major, and ends in the skin beside the sternum. The deep block meets the nerve before it turns; the superficial block meets its anterior cutaneous branch after it has passed through the intercostal layer.

The layers beside the sternum

From skin to lung: subcutaneous fat, pectoralis major, the external intercostal layer (a thin membrane this far forward), the costal cartilages with the internal intercostal muscle between them, the internal thoracic artery and veins, transversus thoracis, endothoracic fascia and pleura. In 24 cadaver hemithoraces transversus thoracis was present in the second to sixth intercostal spaces, and ultrasound identified it correctly in more than 80% of the second to fifth spaces.16 It is thin and variable, and may be hard to see after the internal thoracic artery has been harvested.1

The internal thoracic artery

The artery runs vertically in the deep plane. At the fourth intercostal space it was seen as a pulsatile structure about 1.5 cm from the lateral border of the sternum.1 In cadavers it lay medial to the halfway point between the sternal border and the costochondral junction in the second to sixth spaces.16 Injury could make the artery unusable as a bypass graft; before surgery both arteries are at risk, after harvest of the left artery only the right.1 One small study measured the left artery with Doppler before and 30 minutes after a bilateral deep block in 29 patients having bypass grafting: its diameter increased and its resistive index fell.18

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine. For the superficial block, cadaver studies placed a linear probe in a parasagittal plane about 1 cm lateral to the sternum,17 and one placed the needle tip on the anterior surface of the costal cartilage.19 For the deep block, one cadaver study used a transverse probe beside the sternum with an in-plane needle.20 Count the ribs down from the second costal cartilage, which meets the sternal angle (standard landmark).

Indications

  • Cardiac surgery through a median sternotomy. The main use, and the one with most trials. Both blocks reduce 24-hour opioid use and early pain on pooled analysis, by amounts below the minimal clinically important difference.321 The deep block halved the odds of nausea and vomiting, with high-certainty evidence.21
  • Breast surgery, for the medial breast. As an addition to a serratus anterior or interpectoral-pectoserratus block, it widened sensory cover over the medial breast in two 2025 to 2026 trials, without improving pain scores, opioid use or recovery.2223 An earlier trial did find less pain on movement and less morphine.24 In cadavers, a superficial block combined with a deep serratus anterior block at T5/T6 stained the T2 to T6 nerve territories completely in 28 of 30 hemithoraces.25
  • Sternal fracture. Described in a case report and a small series, where it allowed awake sternal fixation.1226 There are no trials.
  • Paediatric cardiac surgery. A meta-analysis of six studies of the deep (transversus thoracis) block in children found low-certainty reductions in pain, opioid use, extubation time and intensive care stay.27

Nerves blocked

The anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves beside the sternum, T2 to T6 when the spread is wide enough.116 How many levels are reached depends on the plane and the number of injections.

  • Superficial, one injection. 20 mL stained a median of 2 intercostal muscles and 1 intercostal nerve.17 At T4, the median spread was 2 intercostal spaces.19
  • Superficial, two or three injections. Two injections at T3 and T5 spread to a median of 4 spaces, consistently T2 to T5.19 Three injections at the second, fourth and fifth spaces stained 5.3 levels on average.16 In patients, two injections of 10 mL at the second and fourth cartilages blocked T2 to T6 in 81%, against 51% after one injection of 20 mL.8
  • Deep, one injection. 20 mL between ribs 3 and 4 stained 4 intercostal nerves in every specimen, and the slips of transversus thoracis over 4 to 6 levels.20 In a side-by-side comparison at the third space, the deep block reached a median of 4 spaces against 2 for the superficial block.10
  • Deep, two injections. At the third and fifth spaces, 5.7 levels on average.16

One cadaver study disagrees on volume: a single superficial injection of 10 mL spread further than two injections of 5 mL and further than a 10 mL deep injection.32 With 20 mL at the fourth rib, the superficial block also stained the medial and lateral pectoral nerves in 14 of 16 specimens.33

What the block covers

The sternum and the skin and chest wall either side of it. It does not cover chest drain sites below the costal margin or the lateral chest.

Landmark technique

Surgeons can inject the parasternal planes under direct vision before closing the chest. Against surgeon-delivered injection, ultrasound-guided superficial block did not lower overall pain scores but cut fentanyl use and shortened intensive care stay in one trial of 88 patients.30 In another, a surgeon-performed deep block under direct vision used less tramadol than an ultrasound-guided superficial block.28 This page does not describe a blind percutaneous technique.

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, cartilage, artery, vein, nerve or pleura. Switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, then use Test me. There is one scan for each plane.

Superficial parasternal intercostal plane

  1. Place a linear probe parasagittally about 1 cm from the sternal edge.17 The costal cartilages appear as dark ovals in a row, with the bright pleura beneath them and pectoralis major on top.
  2. Count the level. Start from the second cartilage at the sternal angle.
  3. Look for the artery. Colour Doppler between the cartilages shows the internal thoracic artery in the deep layer, a level the superficial needle should not reach.1
  4. Bring the needle in plane and place the tip under pectoralis major, on the anterior surface of the costal cartilage.19
  5. Inject and watch the fluid lift pectoralis major off the cartilages and intercostal layer.
  6. Consider a second injection two levels lower. Two injections covered T2 to T6 more often than one.819

Deep parasternal intercostal plane

  1. Place the probe beside the sternum in the intercostal space chosen; one cadaver study used a transverse orientation with an in-plane needle.20
  2. Name the layers: pectoralis major, internal intercostal, a thin transversus thoracis, then pleura.16
  3. Find the internal thoracic artery and veins with colour Doppler before the needle goes in. It lies about 1.5 cm from the sternal edge at the fourth space.1
  4. Advance the tip through the internal intercostal muscle to lie between it and transversus thoracis, away from the vessels.
  5. Inject in small aliquots and watch transversus thoracis and the pleura being pushed down. If the tip is not seen, stop.

Probe orientation and needle direction vary between published descriptions. Anatomical relationships follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

RCT

One injection or two

In 70 patients, 10 mL at the second and fourth cartilages blocked T2 to T6 in 81% against 51% for 20 mL at the third cartilage. Pain and opioid use did not differ.8 For the deep block the opposite was found: one 10 mL injection at T4/5 gave lower early pain than 5 mL at two levels.9

Cadaver studies

Superficial or deep spread

At the same level and volume the deep block reached a median of 4 intercostal spaces and the superficial block 2.10 Two or three superficial injections can match it.1916

Trials

How much to inject

Trials used 10 mL per side for the deep block,9 15 mL per side,18 20 mL per side for the superficial block,82 and up to 40 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% in total.29 Count the dose for both sides together.34

RCT

Before or after surgery

Timing varied: before incision,299 at the end of surgery,35 or before extubation. Given before extubation, the deep block improved quality of recovery at 24 hours.36 No trial has compared timings directly.

Practice

No twitch to look for

These are fascial plane injections aimed at small sensory branches. None of the trials or cadaver studies cited here used nerve stimulation; the endpoint is the spread seen on ultrasound.

Cadaver study

How close the artery is

On histology the internal thoracic artery lay a median of 1.9 mm (IQR 1.7 to 2.2) from the fourth rib, with only soft tissue between.10

RCT

Pneumothorax

In 254 patients randomised to no block, superficial or deep block, pneumothorax was more frequent in the deep group (P=0.032).4 The authors of the superficial-against-deep meta-analysis found no difference in analgesia between the two.37

Continuous techniques

Catheters extend a block that otherwise lasts hours. The trials disagree.

  • Against placebo, multicentre. EPOCH CardioLink-10 randomised patients in Canada to bilateral superficial catheters with ropivacaine 0.2% or saline: a 20 mL bolus, a basal rate of 0.1 to 1 mL/h and 5 mL boluses every 3 to 4 hours for 48 hours, up to 592 mg of ropivacaine. Opioid use over 72 hours fell by 20.7 morphine milligram equivalents (95% CI −39.0 to −2.3). Pain scores and other secondary outcomes did not differ, and there were no serious block-related complications.2
  • Programmed intermittent bolus. After 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.4% per side, 8 mL of 0.2% every 2 hours for 48 hours reduced morphine use over 48 hours to 25.34 mg against 76.28 mg with saline in 60 patients. Nausea and vomiting fell from 12 of 29 to 3 of 29.38
  • Against a single injection. In 80 patients who all had a 20 mL bolus per side, a 3 mL/h ropivacaine infusion added nothing to sternal pain on coughing at 24 hours (4.0 against 3.9). One patient with the infusion had suspected local anaesthetic toxicity.39
  • Lidocaine. A 72-hour bilateral parasternal infusion at 35 mg/h per side reduced patient-controlled morphine to 10 mg against 28.2 mg in 45 patients, with no difference in pain, interleukin-6 or quality of recovery. Peak expiratory flow was lower with lidocaine at 72 hours.40
  • Liposomal bupivacaine in a single superficial block did not reduce opioid use over three days, but fewer patients had moderate to severe pain on coughing at 48 hours (53.85% against 78.13%).31

Contraindications

  • An internal thoracic artery that cannot be seen or avoided, especially before it is harvested for grafting.1
  • A tip that cannot be kept in view in the deep plane. The pleura lies directly beneath.104
  • A planned dose that exceeds the maximum once both sides are added together, particularly with other blocks or infiltration.3441

Anticoagulation for cardiopulmonary bypass is a frequent question. The authors of one trial describe these less invasive blocks as safer in anticoagulated patients,29 but no study has measured the bleeding risk of either plane.

Side effects and complications

Plasma levels and nausea. Bilateral blocks use large total doses. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies used different drugs, doses and thresholds, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Suspected in one patient with a ropivacaine infusion through bilateral catheters.39 A review of fascial plane blocks found mean plasma levels well below toxic thresholds but some individuals above them, and recommends weight-based dosing, aspiration, incremental injection, observation of the spread and lower concentrations as volume rises.34 Peak levels came 10 to 30 minutes after injection.4135
  • Pneumothorax. More frequent after the deep block than after the superficial block or no block in one trial.4 Not reported as block-related in the pooled analysis of ultrasound-guided superficial blocks.15
  • Haematoma. One block-site haematoma across 29 trials of the ultrasound-guided superficial block.15
  • Internal thoracic artery injury. A theoretical risk that would remove a bypass conduit; I found no case reported in the studies on this page.1
  • Fewer side effects of opioids. Pooled, the deep block reduced nausea and vomiting (odds ratio 0.30).21
  • Early extubation. In 2294 patients, extubation in the operating theatre with superficial blocks was not associated with more reintubation (1.2%, against 0.7% and 1.2% in the comparison groups) or non-invasive ventilation.42

Pearls of wisdom

  • Run colour Doppler before every deep block, and before a superficial block if the view is unclear. The artery may be needed as a graft.1
  • For the superficial block, two injections cover more levels than one.819
  • Add both sides together when you calculate the dose, and add any rectus sheath block or wound infiltration.3441
  • The block covers the sternum, not the drain sites. Plan something else for the upper abdomen.
  • Expect a modest opioid saving, not an opioid-free recovery.343
  • When the internal thoracic artery has been harvested and transversus thoracis cannot be seen, choose the superficial plane.1

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Does it improve recovery

Two trials using Quality of Recovery-15 as the primary outcome were negative,45 and one was positive when the deep block was given before extubation.36 In one trial an unplanned subgroup with internal thoracic artery harvest did better with the block; this needs confirmation.5

Is the opioid saving worth having

Every meta-analysis finds a saving with very high heterogeneity, and the authors describe it as modest or below the threshold for clinical importance.3211543 The largest placebo-controlled multicentre trial was positive for opioid use and neutral for pain.2

Superficial or deep

Equal on pooled clinical outcomes,37 but different in cadavers and in pneumothorax risk.104 The near-zero difference was repeated in the 2026 meta-analysis of ultrasound-guided superficial blocks.15

Catheters and long-acting drugs

Catheters reduced opioid use against placebo,238 but an infusion added nothing to a single injection in another trial.39 Liposomal bupivacaine helped pain on coughing, not opioid use.31
