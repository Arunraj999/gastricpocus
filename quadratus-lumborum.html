Quadratus lumborum blocks

A family of injections around the quadratus lumborum muscle in the posterior abdominal wall. The international consensus names three: lateral, posterior and anterior.1 Each puts local anaesthetic against a different face of the muscle, and each spreads differently.2 The block helps after caesarean section when intrathecal morphine is not given, and adds little when it is.3 Several well-run placebo-controlled trials in hip and bowel surgery found no benefit.45

Level of difficulty

Advanced block

The lateral and posterior approaches are fascial plane injections a few centimetres deep. The anterior approach is different. It is classed as a deep block, it is usually done in the lateral position, it becomes harder with obesity, and it sits beside the psoas compartment and the lumbar plexus.2 The names have also been a source of error: the older numbered labels (QL1, QL2, QL3) were used inconsistently, which is why an international Delphi consensus replaced them with anatomical names.12

The three names used on this page follow that consensus. Sixty experts took part, and the final list reached strong consensus for 16 block names across the abdominal wall, chest wall and paraspinal region.1

Anatomy

Quadratus lumborum arises from the iliac crest and inserts into the lower border of the 12th rib and the transverse processes of L1 to L4. Psoas major lies in front of its medial part, and the erector spinae behind it.2 Lateral to it, the aponeurosis of transversus abdominis runs back towards the thoracolumbar fascia. The kidney lies in front of the upper part of the muscle.8

The thoracolumbar fascia. Two models are described. In the two-layer model the posterior layer wraps the erector spinae, the anterior layer runs between erector spinae and quadratus lumborum, and the fascia on the front of quadratus lumborum and psoas is called transversalis fascia. In the three-layer model, which is more widely used in the block literature, there is a posterior layer around the erector spinae, a middle layer between erector spinae and quadratus lumborum, and an anterior layer in front of quadratus lumborum and psoas.2 The two models describe the same tissue with different names. This matters when reading trials, because "anterior layer" and "transversalis fascia" are sometimes the same sheet.

The arcuate ligaments. The medial arcuate ligament runs from the side of the L2 vertebral body to the L1 transverse process, over psoas. The lateral arcuate ligament runs from the L1 transverse process to the middle of the 12th rib, over quadratus lumborum. Together they form the lower edge of the diaphragm. As the fascia on the front of psoas and quadratus lumborum reaches these ligaments it splits: one sheet joins the ligaments and the other continues upwards behind them, in continuity with the endothoracic fascia.82 This is the proposed route by which an injection in the lumbar region reaches the lower thoracic paravertebral space.

Mechanisms of spread. Three are proposed, and none is proven for every approach. The first is spread upwards behind the arcuate ligaments into the thoracic paravertebral space. The second is spread medially and downwards along psoas to the branches of the lumbar plexus. The third is an effect on the thoracolumbar fascia itself, which carries sympathetic fibres.2 In the cadaver and imaging literature only about half of studies showed reliable thoracic paravertebral spread after the anterior approach, and most showed lumbar plexus spread.2

Surface anatomy

For the lateral and posterior approaches the patient lies on the side, or supine with a wedge. A curved probe goes transversely on the flank in the mid-axillary line between the costal margin and the iliac crest, and is slid backwards until quadratus lumborum is seen.213 For the anterior approach the probe moves further back, above the iliac crest at the posterior axillary line, until the muscle is seen attached to the tip of a lumbar transverse process.112

Indications

  • Caesarean section when intrathecal morphine is not used. In a meta-analysis of 12 trials (924 patients) the block reduced 24-hour morphine use by 18 mg and pain at 4 to 6 hours by 1.5 cm on a 10 cm scale against no block, but gave no benefit when added to, or compared with, spinal morphine.3 In a trial of 72 women the bilateral anterior block reduced 24-hour opioid use from 94 to 65 mg oral morphine equivalents against saline.10 The PROSPECT guideline recommends fascial plane blocks, including the quadratus lumborum block, only when intrathecal opioid has not been given.14
  • Percutaneous nephrolithotomy. A single anterior injection cut opioid use, and shortened time to walking and hospital stay, against a saline block.6
  • Laparoscopic nephrectomy. The anterior approach reduced 24-hour opioid use by about 43% against placebo.7 Pooled across 12 trials, though, the reduction did not reach the authors' threshold for a clinically important difference.15
  • Laparoscopic colorectal surgery. Results conflict. A placebo-controlled trial in an enhanced recovery pathway found no reduction in opioid use,5 while a smaller trial of the subcostal anterior approach against no block found a reduction.16
  • Hip surgery. Placebo-controlled and no-block trials of the posterior and anterior approaches found no benefit over multimodal analgesia after hip arthroplasty or hip arthroscopy.41718 This is not a first-choice indication.

Nerves blocked

Which nerves are reached depends on the approach. The most consistent finding is block of the lower thoracic and first lumbar nerves that supply the lower abdominal wall: the subcostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves.1124

  • Anterior (transmuscular). In cadavers, 30 mL spread to the thoracic paravertebral space and the thoracic sympathetic trunk in all successful injections, mainly behind the arcuate ligaments. The subcostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves were stained every time; the genitofemoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves to a varying degree. In that study no dye reached the lumbar plexus or femoral nerve.11 In a second cadaver study every transmuscular injection reached the L1 and L3 roots and spread within psoas.13 The two studies disagree on the lumbar plexus, and clinical reports of leg weakness are consistent with the second.2512
  • Anterior, subcostal. Dye passed the arcuate ligaments to the T9 to T12 roots in all specimens, with variable staining higher up.24
  • Posterior. In cadavers the subcostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves, the T11 to L1 roots and the middle layer of the thoracolumbar fascia were stained, with only variable staining of T10.24 A posterolateral injection at L2 spread over a mean of 7.3 levels and was held back by the lumbocostal ligament; paravertebral and ventral ramus involvement was inconsistent.26 In volunteers, MRI showed spread to the T10 to T12 paravertebral space after the posterior approach, with sensory loss over the lower and lateral abdomen but not the upper abdomen or the lower midline.27
  • Lateral. In cadavers, two of three lateral injections spread into the transversus abdominis plane.13

What the block covers

The lower anterolateral abdominal wall is covered most reliably. Coverage of the midline and upper abdomen is variable, and any visceral effect depends on paravertebral spread.

Landmark technique

The quadratus lumborum block was described as an ultrasound-guided technique, and the injection points are defined by fascial layers seen on ultrasound.21 There is no validated landmark technique, and this page does not describe one.

Ultrasound technique

There is one scan for each approach. Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, fascia, bone or organ. Switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, then use Test me. The needle has its own section, next.

Lateral quadratus lumborum block

  1. Position. Lateral or supine with a wedge. A curved probe goes transversely on the flank between the costal margin and the iliac crest.213
  2. Follow the three flank muscles backwards. External oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis taper into the aponeurosis of transversus abdominis.
  3. Find the lateral border of quadratus lumborum where the aponeurosis meets it.
  4. Bring the needle in plane from the anterior end of the probe, anteromedial to posterolateral.8
  5. Target: beneath the aponeurosis of transversus abdominis, just anterolateral to quadratus lumborum.8 The fluid should spread along the border of the muscle, deep to the aponeurosis.

Posterior quadratus lumborum block

  1. Same starting view as the lateral block, then slide or tilt slightly backwards so that the posterior surface of quadratus lumborum and the latissimus dorsi above it are clear.
  2. Name the triangle. The lumbar interfascial triangle lies between quadratus lumborum, latissimus dorsi and the erector spinae.8
  3. Bring the needle in plane from the anterior end of the probe and pass it beside the lateral edge of the muscle to its back surface.
  4. Target: the posterior surface of quadratus lumborum, in the lumbar interfascial triangle.8 The fluid should lie as a layer behind the muscle, not inside it.

Anterior (transmuscular) quadratus lumborum block

  1. Position. Lateral, operated side up, or prone.2
  2. Find the shamrock. Slide the probe back from the flank, above the iliac crest at the posterior axillary line, until quadratus lumborum is seen attached to the tip of a lumbar transverse process: psoas in front, erector spinae behind, quadratus lumborum beside the process. This three-leaf picture is the shamrock sign, usually at L3 to L4.112
  3. Bring the needle in plane, from either end of the probe.2 The model on this page brings it from the posterior end, through quadratus lumborum.
  4. Target: the plane between quadratus lumborum and psoas.11 The fluid should separate the two muscles along their interface.
  5. Stay on the quadratus lumborum side of the fascia. An injection between quadratus lumborum and the anterior layer of the thoracolumbar fascia caused much less leg weakness than one placed through that layer onto psoas.122

New evidence: anterior block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament

New evidence The most recent new variant with clinical trial data. It was proposed by Li and colleagues in 2020, and moves the anterior injection up to the level of the lateral arcuate ligament, above which the diaphragm lies directly in front of quadratus lumborum.830

  1. Rationale. If spread to the thoracic paravertebral space has to pass behind the arcuate ligaments, an injection above the ligament should get there faster and with less volume left in the lumbar fascial space.8
  2. Transverse approach. Lateral position. A curved probe 3 to 4 cm lateral to the midline at T12 to L1, marker lateral, tilted towards the midline. The 12th rib casts a shadow at the outer upper part of the image; the vertebral body, lamina and spinous process form the medial bone line.8
  3. The target is a triangle. The diaphragm runs towards the front of the vertebral body and quadratus lumborum towards the facet joint, leaving a triangular gap between them that opens onto the T12 paravertebral space. The diaphragm moves on deep breathing.8
  4. Needle. In plane through erector spinae and quadratus lumborum into the triangle; 15 to 20 mL after hydrodissection confirms the position.8 A parasagittal version, with the probe 6 to 8 cm from the midline between the 12th rib and the L1 transverse process, targets the zone where diaphragm and quadratus lumborum lie together.8
  5. What is known. Sensory block of T7 to L1 by 10 minutes in the first series, in patients having laparoscopic renal surgery.31 Less opioid than the transmuscular block after laparoscopic nephrectomy (23.5 against 34.3 mg morphine equivalents) and than the lateral block after colorectal surgery (29.2 against 40.5 mg).3233 Non-inferior to thoracic paravertebral block for pain after laparoscopic nephrectomy.34 A meta-analysis of eight trials (596 patients) found 10.9 mg less opioid over 24 hours and less nausea and vomiting.30
  6. What is not known. How it spreads: its developers wrote in 2021 that cadaver or radiographic studies were still needed, and I have not found one in PubMed.8 How it compares with no block or a sham block outside the developers' trials, and in surgery other than abdominal and renal. Whether it is safe in everyone: the pleura can reach the L1 transverse process on deep inspiration in a small number of patients, and quadriceps weakness followed in 4 patients (16.7%) in the first series.831

Anatomical relationships, motor supply and stimulation responses follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

Cadaver studies

Where the dye went

After three lateral and three posterior injections of 20 mL in cadavers, two of each spread into the transversus abdominis plane. All four transmuscular injections reached the L1 and L3 roots.13 With 30 mL, transmuscular dye reached the thoracic paravertebral space behind the arcuate ligaments.11

Volunteer study

Paravertebral spread on MRI

In five volunteers given 0.7 mL/kg, the posterior approach spread into the paravertebral space at T10 to T12. An injection into the muscle itself did not, and left only lateral abdominal sensory loss.27

RCT

Above or below the fascia

In 80 patients having laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the anterior block given between quadratus lumborum and the anterior layer of the fascia gave better port-site analgesia than an injection through the fascia onto psoas. One hour after surgery 65% had normal leg strength against 10%, and 15% against 80% needed help to walk.12

Volunteer RCT

Is the leg weak

Twenty volunteers had a unilateral anterior block with 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% and a placebo block on the other side. Quadriceps strength was not weaker on the active side, and the timed up-and-go test was slightly faster.28

The fascia matters more than the muscle

Placing the anterior injection through the fascia onto psoas made the block behave partly like a lumbar plexus block, with more weakness and more patients needing help to walk.122 Watch the first few millilitres: they should open the plane between the muscles, not track into psoas.

Case report

A quadriceps twitch is a warning

Hip flexion and knee extension weakness lasting about 18 hours followed a lateral block with 20 mL of levobupivacaine 0.25%, and led to an unplanned overnight stay after day-case laparoscopy.25 If the stimulator is on and the thigh twitches, the tip is near the lumbar plexus.

Continuous techniques

Catheters can be placed by the anterior approach. In 32 women after caesarean section, bilateral anterior catheters were loaded with ropivacaine and then randomised at 2 hours to ropivacaine 0.2% or saline infusions. The infusions did not prolong the time to first opioid: a median of 414 against 428 minutes.35 The active group received 697 mg of ropivacaine in the first 24 hours.35

Contraindications

  • Anticoagulation and coagulopathy, for the anterior approach. It is a deep block beside the psoas compartment, where small vessels are hard to see, and precautions similar to those for neuraxial techniques have been advised.2 Haematoma has been reported after quadratus lumborum blocks in children.36
  • Day-case surgery where early walking is planned, for the anterior approach. Weigh the risk of leg weakness.2512
  • Caesarean section with intrathecal morphine already given, as a routine addition. The evidence shows little or no benefit.319

Side effects and complications

Leg weakness is the complication that matters most for mobilisation. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies measured weakness in different ways, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Leg weakness and falls. Weakness of hip flexion and knee extension has followed the lateral approach,25 the anterior approach when the injection passes onto psoas,12 and the supra-arcuate approach.31 Against the lumbar plexus block, a lateral block left the quadriceps stronger in recovery and patients were 2.4 times more likely to walk in the first 12 hours.37 After hip arthroscopy the anterior block did not reduce quadriceps strength against no block.18 Treat any patient with leg weakness after this block as at risk of falling until it wears off.
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Bilateral blocks use large doses. After bilateral blocks with 150 mg of ropivacaine the mean peak arterial concentration was 1.0 µg/mL, lower than after a lateral transversus abdominis plane block with the same dose (1.8 µg/mL).29 Across fascial plane blocks average concentrations stay below toxic thresholds but some individual patients cross them; a 2025 review advises weight-based dosing, lower concentrations as volumes rise, and considering adrenaline.38
  • Hypotension. Two patients developed serious hypotension and tachycardia 30 to 40 minutes after a posterior block with general anaesthesia, attributed to sympathetic block from cephalad spread.39 In volunteers the anterior block did not lower mean arterial pressure.28
  • Kidney, viscera and peritoneum. The kidney lies in front of quadratus lumborum, and reviews list retroperitoneal haematoma and injury to abdominal organs as potential risks of the anterior approach, and diaphragm injury, neuraxial injection and visceral injury for the supra-arcuate variant.28 I found no published case of renal puncture in PubMed; the risk rests on anatomy and expert description.
  • Haematoma. Two cases are reported in children, both from case reports.36

Pearls of wisdom

  • Say which approach you mean. "QL block" without a qualifier does not tell anyone where the needle went.1
  • If the patient has had intrathecal morphine for caesarean section, the block is unlikely to add much.320
  • For the anterior approach, open the plane between quadratus lumborum and psoas with a small test volume and stop if the fluid enters psoas.12
  • Calculate the total dose before a bilateral block, especially in small patients and in pregnancy.38
  • Test the legs before mobilising, and warn the ward. A patient who cannot lift the leg off the bed should not walk unaided.25
  • Keep the kidney in view on the anterior and supra-arcuate approaches; know where it is before the needle moves.8

Other abdominal wall blocks

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

An alternative to intrathecal morphine?

Two 2026 trials compared the block directly with intrathecal morphine for caesarean section. The posterior block matched it for 24-hour morphine with less pruritus (2% against 35%);42 the lateral block reduced early resting pain, but non-inferiority for recovery was inconclusive in 58 women.9 A 2025 meta-analysis rated the certainty of evidence low.41

The supra-arcuate approach

Faster onset and good early trial results, almost all from a few groups.3034 It still lacks a spread study and an independent placebo-controlled trial.8

The subcostal anterior approach

A 2025 trial of 55 patients after laparoscopic colorectal surgery found 15 against 24.3 mg morphine equivalents at 24 hours against no block, and earlier mobilisation.16 The control group had no sham block, and an earlier placebo-controlled trial of the transmuscular approach in the same surgery was negative.5

Combined with a lumbar plexus block at the shamrock

For hip arthroplasty, a lumbar plexus block plus a transmuscular block from the same shamrock view gave effective sensory block in 67.6% against 7.1% with the lumbar plexus block alone, and less hypotension than a T12 paravertebral plus two-level plexus block.46
