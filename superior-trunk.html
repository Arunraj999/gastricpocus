Superior trunk block

Local anaesthetic placed beside the superior trunk, where C5 and C6 have joined and before the suprascapular nerve leaves. It gives analgesia for shoulder surgery comparable to an interscalene block from a point further from the phrenic nerve.123

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The superior trunk block is not one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.28 It uses the same probe, needle and in-plane approach as the interscalene block, but two more things have to be found on the scan: the point where C5 and C6 join, and the point where the suprascapular nerve leaves.1 In one randomised trial two patients allocated to this block could not have it: in one the transverse cervical artery lay over the trunk, and in the other the suprascapular nerve could not be identified.3

Anatomy

The C5 and C6 roots leave the interscalene groove and join to form the superior trunk. On ultrasound the trunk is a single structure with a bright connective tissue border, lying just beneath the prevertebral layer of the deep cervical fascia. The suprascapular nerve leaves its lateral side and runs laterally under omohyoid.1

Most of the nerve supply to the shoulder joint travels through this trunk: the suprascapular nerve to the posterior joint, the axillary nerve to the anterior and posterior joint, and the subscapular and lateral pectoral nerves to the anterior joint.21 The skin over the top of the shoulder is the exception. It is supplied by the supraclavicular nerves of the cervical plexus, and the original description added a separate 5 mL superficial to the middle scalene to block them.1

Why the diaphragm is spared more often

The phrenic nerve runs down the front of the anterior scalene. At the level of the cricoid cartilage, where an interscalene block is made, it is almost touching the C5 root. Further down the neck the two move apart, so an injection at the trunk starts further from the nerve.41 Both still lie under the same layer of fascia, which is why a large volume can reach the phrenic nerve from here as well.8

The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves run through the middle scalene, and at the interscalene level a nerve was visible in or on that muscle in 90% of patients.23 A lateral-to-medial needle for an interscalene block has to cross the muscle. The superior trunk needle passes over its surface.1

Surface anatomy

The probe lies in the posterior triangle behind the lateral border of sternocleidomastoid, lower than for an interscalene block: the roots are found first, then followed down the neck towards the clavicle until they join.1

Indications

  • Analgesia for arthroscopic shoulder surgery. Two randomised trials published in 2019 found it non-inferior to interscalene block: for the worst pain in the recovery room in 126 patients,2 and for pain at 24 hours in 78 patients.3
  • Patients in whom phrenic nerve block matters. The block was first described in a patient with moderate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease having arthroscopic shoulder surgery.1 Complete paresis was uncommon in the trials, but partial paresis was not, and it depended on the dose.38
  • Surgery under block and sedation. In a trial of 88 patients, every arthroscopy was completed under superior trunk block with dexmedetomidine sedation, at both doses tested.8 A trial of 123 patients having arthroscopy under block alone found comparable analgesia to interscalene block with smaller falls in diaphragm movement and spirometry.16 One smaller trial disagreed: anaesthesia was graded excellent in 16 of 24 patients after superior trunk block against 22 of 24 after interscalene block.7
  • Humeral fracture surgery. For plating of proximal and mid-shaft fractures in 62 patients, pain scores and opioid use were similar to interscalene block, with less diaphragmatic paresis.15

Nerves blocked

The block is aimed at the C5 and C6 fibres after they have joined and before any of them has left, so the suprascapular nerve is included.1 These are the fibres that reach the shoulder through the suprascapular, axillary, subscapular and lateral pectoral nerves.21

It is not aimed at the middle or inferior trunk, and it does not block the supraclavicular nerves unless they are injected separately.1 The hand is not untouched in the first hours, though. Grip strength in recovery fell by 80.8% after 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and by 68.5% after 10 mL of 0.25%.8 Compared with interscalene block, one trial found no difference in grip strength,2 and another found weakness still present at 24 hours in four patients after interscalene block and in none after superior trunk block.3

Landmark technique

There is no landmark version of this block. It was described with ultrasound and depends on seeing the trunk and the suprascapular nerve.1 A nerve stimulator can be added to the ultrasound technique: one trial used the same stimulation sequence as for its interscalene blocks, looking for a biceps or deltoid twitch.3

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, artery, vein, nerve or bone. Tap a nerve to see the muscles it supplies. Use Probe level to see what the picture looks like when the probe is too high or too low, switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, then use Test me. The needle has its own section, next.

Finding the level

  1. Start at the roots. Find C5 and C6 between the anterior and middle scalene, as for an interscalene block. C6 usually shows as two bundles.1 Some operators start in the supraclavicular fossa and follow the plexus up to the roots first, to be sure which is which.8
  2. Slide down the neck. The roots move deeper and merge into one structure with a bright border, just under the prevertebral fascia: the superior trunk.1
  3. Find where the suprascapular nerve leaves. Keep sliding until a small dark circle separates from the lateral side of the trunk and runs away under omohyoid.1
  4. Come back up. Stop where that circle has just rejoined the trunk. This is the block level: the trunk is formed and the suprascapular nerve is still inside it.1
  5. Look for the artery. Lighten the probe pressure and use colour Doppler. The transverse cervical artery may cross over the plexus here.1

RCT

Where the tip goes

In plane from lateral to medial, between the deep cervical fascia and the surface of the middle scalene, with the tip beside the lateral border of the trunk.3 One group gives part of the dose deep to the trunk and the rest superficial to it, moving the needle between the two.8

RCT

How much to inject

5 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% gave paralysis in 14.3% at 30 minutes against 65.7% with 15 mL. Pain scores, opioid use and satisfaction did not differ significantly; the extent of sensory and motor block did.5

RCT

Concentration matters too

10 mL of ropivacaine 0.25% against 15 mL of 0.5%: paresis in 31.8% against 84.1%, with every operation completed under block and sedation in both groups. The lower dose wore off sooner, at 11.9 against 14.5 hours.8

Dose-finding

The two aims pull apart

With ropivacaine 0.25%, 9.1 mL was needed for surgical anaesthesia in 90% of patients,11 and 7.6 mL was the largest volume that avoided paralysis in 90%.10 The volume that suits surgery sits a little above the volume that reliably spares the diaphragm.

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

These thresholds were measured at the trunks. During ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block in 39 patients, the median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it. A twitch at 0.2 mA or less was never seen outside the trunk. A twitch between 0.2 and 0.5 mA did not rule out an intraneural tip, and thresholds were higher in patients with diabetes.24

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.25

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root (mean peak 8.2 psi) and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.26

Descriptive

What the path leaves out

The needle does not cross the middle scalene, where the dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves run.1 At the interscalene level a nerve was seen in or on that muscle in 90% of 50 patients.23

Continuous techniques

In a double-blind randomised trial of 100 patients having arthroscopic shoulder surgery, a superior trunk catheter was compared with a suprascapular catheter placed by the surgeon under arthroscopic vision. At 24 hours the suprascapular catheter could not be shown to be non-inferior: worst pain was higher by a mean of 0.9 points (95% CI 0.1 to 1.7). It did, however, produce significantly less hemidiaphragmatic paresis at 24 hours than the superior trunk catheter.14

Contraindications

  • This block reduces phrenic nerve involvement; it does not remove it. With 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, some degree of paresis followed in 76.3%.3 For a patient who could not tolerate any loss of diaphragm function, lower the dose or choose a block further from the phrenic nerve.51314
  • An artery lying across the trunk, or a suprascapular nerve that cannot be found, were reasons to abandon the block in one trial.3
  • Choose the approach for the individual patient, taking account of comorbidity and the type of surgery.22

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is less frequent than after interscalene block but still occurs. Reported rates by trial and dose are shown below. Tap a bar for the detail.

The trials used different drugs, definitions of paresis and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Hoarseness was less frequent than after interscalene block.2
  • Breathlessness was reported by one patient after each block in a trial of 78; both had complete paresis and both settled within 12 hours.3
  • Numb fingers at 24 hours were reported by four patients after superior trunk block and two after interscalene block, and had resolved by 48 hours.3
  • Rebound pain followed in 6.8% after 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and 13.6% after 10 mL of 0.25%, a difference that was not significant.8

Pearls of wisdom

  • Follow, do not guess. Trace C5 and C6 down until they merge, go on until the suprascapular nerve leaves, then come back up to where it rejoins.1
  • Put colour Doppler over the trunk before the needle goes in.13
  • Keep the needle on the surface of the middle scalene. The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves are inside it.123
  • The dose decides what happens to the diaphragm. Lower volume and lower concentration each reduced paresis, at the cost of a shorter block.589
  • The skin over the top of the shoulder needs its own injection if it has to be numb.16
  • Warn the patient that the hand may be weak at first.8

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

How little is enough

Recent trials have moved from 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% towards 10 mL or less of 0.25%.89 Dose-finding studies put the volume for surgical anaesthesia (9.1 mL) above the volume that reliably spares the diaphragm (7.6 mL), so the choice depends on which matters more for the patient.1011

Not yet conclusive when pooled

A 2025 meta-analysis of 28 trials found conclusive evidence of less paralysis for extrafascial and lower-concentration interscalene block and for supraclavicular block. For the superior trunk block, trial sequential analysis had not reached significance.12

Longer-acting local anaesthetic

With a liposomal bupivacaine mixture in 68 patients, pain scores to 24 hours did not differ from interscalene block, and arm motor scores were better after superior trunk block.20

Guidelines

The PROSPECT recommendations for rotator cuff repair, published in 2019, name interscalene block, or suprascapular block with or without axillary nerve block.34 The randomised trials of superior trunk block appeared from the same year onwards.23
