Obturator nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed around the obturator nerve in the groin: proximally, in the fascial plane between pectineus and obturator externus just below the superior pubic ramus, or distally, around its anterior and posterior divisions between the adductor muscles. It has two main jobs: stopping the adductor jerk during resection of tumours on the lateral bladder wall, and adding analgesia to knee surgery. Skin testing is a poor guide to success. In 57% of 30 patients the nerve supplied no skin at all, so the block is judged by adductor weakness.1

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The distal technique is shallow and quick once the adductor muscles are recognised: in 30 patients an interfascial injection took a mean 122 seconds.6 What makes the block harder is the nerve itself. The obturator nerve and its divisions are among the flattest nerves described on ultrasound, and in volunteers the common trunk was identified on only 10 of 40 sides.14 Its branching is also inconsistent: in 84 cadavers it divided inside the pelvis in 23%, within the obturator canal in 52% and in the thigh in 25%.15 The proximal approach is deeper and lies close to the obturator vessels.12

An ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus agreed a naming template for limb blocks based on the nerve and the anatomical location of the injection.16 On this page, "proximal" means the plane between pectineus and obturator externus, and "distal" means the planes around adductor brevis at the inguinal crease.17

Anatomy

The obturator nerve arises from the anterior divisions of the second to fourth lumbar nerve roots, emerges at the medial border of psoas, runs along the lateral wall of the pelvis and leaves through the obturator canal with the obturator vessels. Its course along the side wall of the pelvis, close to the lateral wall of the bladder, is the reason electrical current from a resectoscope can stimulate it.

The divisions. In 84 cadavers the anterior branch most often divided into three muscular branches to adductor longus, adductor brevis and gracilis (66.7%), and the posterior branch most often into two branches to adductor brevis and adductor magnus (60.1%). The articular branch showed nine different branching patterns and most often arose from the common trunk.15 At the inguinal crease the anterior division lies in the fascia between pectineus or adductor longus and adductor brevis, and the posterior division in the fascia between adductor brevis and adductor magnus.67

Size and depth. In 20 volunteers the anterior division lay a mean 15.5 mm below the skin, the posterior division 29.3 mm and the common trunk 25.9 mm. All three were flat, with a mean thickness of 1.4 to 2.7 mm against a width of 9.0 to 10.9 mm.14

The proximal target. Just below the superior pubic ramus, a hyperechoic triangle is formed by the ramus, the posterior margin of pectineus and the anterior surface of obturator externus. It contains the obturator nerve and the obturator vessels.12 Obturator externus is not always a single muscle: a superior fasciculus was present on 9 of 13 cadaver sides, and dye placed above it did not reach the obturator canal.18

The hip. The anterior hip capsule received branches from the femoral and obturator nerves in all 13 hemipelvises studied, and from the accessory obturator nerve in 7.19 Fifteen millilitres of dye injected between pectineus and obturator externus spread into the obturator canal and stained all the obturator branches to the hip joint on all 14 cadaver sides; an accessory obturator nerve was present on 3 sides (21%) and was stained each time.11

The knee. Articular branches from the posterior division of the obturator nerve and from the tibial nerve supplied the posterior knee capsule in all 15 specimens; the obturator branches went to its superomedial part.20

The skin. The cutaneous contribution is small and inconsistent. A cutaneous branch of the anterior division was found on 43% of 14 cadaver sides, supplying an area of 4 to 9 cm² over the posteromedial popliteal fossa.21 Clinically, after a selective block in 30 patients, 57% had no sensory change, 23% had reduced sensation at the top of the popliteal fossa and 20% on the medial thigh.1

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg slightly abducted and externally rotated. For the distal approach the probe sits on the inguinal crease medial to the femoral vein, over the adductor muscles. For the proximal approach the probe starts in the same place and is tilted cranially until the superior pubic ramus comes into view.5 An alternative proximal approach uses the lithotomy position, with the probe on the medial thigh in line with the inguinal crease and the needle inserted at the pubic region, in plane.22 In volunteers the common trunk lay a mean 19.9 mm from the pubic tubercle and 12.9 mm from the femoral vein.12

Indications

  • Resection of tumours on the lateral bladder wall under spinal anaesthesia. Pooled across 8 trials and 635 patients, adding the block reduced the obturator reflex (risk ratio 0.22) and bladder perforation (risk ratio 0.24).2
  • Knee arthroplasty, as an adjunct to a femoral or femoral triangle block.3423
  • Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with a hamstring graft, where less intraoperative fentanyl was needed with the block than without it.24
  • Hip arthroscopy. A subpectineal block reduced opioid use in the first 3 hours by 40% against placebo.25
  • Adductor spasticity and some hip pain, among the indications listed in a focused review.17

Nerves blocked

The obturator nerve: both divisions with the distal technique, and the trunk with its hip articular branch when local anaesthetic reaches the obturator canal from the proximal plane.1711 The proximal plane also reached the accessory obturator nerve in cadavers whenever that nerve was present.11

Other blocks do not reliably reach it. After a femoral 3-in-1 block, adductor strength fell by only 18%, against 78% when a selective obturator block was added.3 In ten patients imaged with MRI after a fascia iliaca or 3-in-1 injection, no injectate reached the obturator nerve and no patient lost adductor strength.31 After a 40 mL suprainguinal fascia iliaca block, the obturator nerve was blocked in 2.4% of patients given a saline obturator injection.28 A parasacral sciatic block reduced adductor strength by 11.3%, and in cadavers its spread stayed within the sacral plexus.32

What the block covers

Mainly motor: the hip adductors. The sensory share is a variable patch of skin and parts of the hip and knee joints.

Landmark technique

Two stimulator techniques preceded ultrasound: the classic pubic approach, with the needle inserted below and lateral to the pubic tubercle towards the obturator canal, and the inguinal approach, with the needle inserted at the inguinal crease towards the divisions. In 50 patients having knee arthroscopy, the inguinal approach caused less pain and discomfort, took less time and needed less sedation, with the same fall in adductor strength.29 A meta-analysis of five trials and 312 patients found a higher success rate with the inguinal approach (risk ratio 1.15), fewer attempts and a shorter procedure.33

Neither reliably fills the obturator canal: with contrast and fluoroscopy, canal spread was seen in 42.6% after the pubic approach and 47.8% after the inguinal approach, against 84% with an ultrasound-guided proximal injection.34 In 122 patients having bladder tumour resection, no adductor contraction occurred in 91.4% after an ultrasound-guided block, against 76.6% after a blind block by the urologist.35 This page does not describe a landmark technique in detail.

Ultrasound technique

Two families of technique exist. The distal approach places two injections at the inguinal crease, one for each division; the proximal approach places one injection between pectineus and obturator externus.17

  1. Find the femoral vessels at the inguinal crease, then slide medially until pectineus and the adductor muscles fill the screen: adductor longus superficially, adductor brevis beneath it and adductor magnus deepest.
  2. Distal approach: name the two fascial planes. The anterior division runs in the plane superficial to adductor brevis and the posterior division in the plane deep to it. Inject into each plane.67 The divisions are thin and flat; the plane matters more than the nerve.14
  3. Proximal approach: tilt the probe cranially from the distal view, following pectineus, until the superior pubic ramus appears. Inject in the plane between pectineus and obturator externus, just below the ramus.5
  4. Run colour Doppler. The obturator vessels lie in the same triangle as the nerve.12
  5. Look for a superior fasciculus of obturator externus. If present, an injection above it may not reach the canal; in cadavers, an injection between it and the main belly reached the canal on 3 of 4 sides.18
  6. In the lithotomy position, an in-plane pubic approach to the same plane is an alternative.22

Case series

One injection, proximally

Taha injected between pectineus and obturator externus below the superior pubic ramus. The target was found in a median 4 seconds, motor block began at a median 4 minutes, and both branches were blocked in every patient.5 In 20 patients in the lithotomy position, 8 mL of levobupivacaine 0.75% by an in-plane pubic approach blocked every patient, and in a cadaver the dye travelled back through the obturator canal.22

Observational

Two injections, distally

In 30 patients having knee surgery, 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline, split between the two planes around adductor brevis, met the success definition (50% loss of adductor strength at 15 minutes) in 28 (93%), with a mean fall in strength of 82.2%.6

RCT

Proximal against distal

In 60 patients having bladder tumour resection, 10 mL proximally and 20 mL distally (bupivacaine 0.375%) gave success rates of 86.7% and 93.3%, with no significant difference.10 In 90 patients, no adductor jerk occurred in 85.7% after the proximal and 78.0% after the distal approach (36 of 42 against 32 of 41 analysed), which the authors reported as significant, but vascular puncture was recorded in nine patients against two.13

RCT

Filling the canal

With contrast and fluoroscopy in 143 patients, the ultrasound-guided proximal injection reached the obturator canal in 84%, against 42.6% and 47.8% with the two landmark approaches.34 In cadavers, 15 mL in the subpectineal plane reached the canal on all 14 sides.11

Needle path and nerve stimulator

With the distal technique the needle passes through adductor longus to the first plane, and on through adductor brevis to the second. With the proximal technique it passes through pectineus to the plane above obturator externus, close to the obturator vessels. Stimulation of the anterior division contracts adductor longus and gracilis on the medial thigh; stimulation of the posterior division contracts adductor magnus.

RCT

Interfascial alone or with a stimulator

In 50 patients with adductor spasm during bladder resection, interfascial injection was quicker to perform (1.6 against 3.0 minutes), with a similar onset and success of 88% against 100% (not significant).7 In a second trial, interfascial injection failed to give a complete block on stimulator testing in 19.4%.36

Meta-analysis

Adding the stimulator

Across five trials and 451 patients having bladder resection, adding nerve stimulation to ultrasound improved prevention of adductor spasm (odds ratio 3.20) and lengthened the procedure by 1.99 minutes.37

Observational

Stimulation thresholds and depth

In a small clinical series the mean current that produced adductor contraction was 0.70 mA.14 With the proximal technique the mean needle depth was 48.3 mm.12

Observational

How to test the block

Sensory testing misled: in 57% of patients the nerve had no skin territory. Adductor strength fell by a mean 77% after 7 mL of ropivacaine 0.75%, and the authors concluded that the only effective test is adductor strength.1

Continuous techniques

I have not found a randomised trial of a continuous obturator catheter. In patients having anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with a hamstring graft, Sakura and colleagues found the obturator nerve important during surgery but concluded that a continuous obturator block was not needed beyond 24 hours. That conclusion rested on a comparison of continuous femoral and posterior lumbar plexus infusions, not on a trial of obturator catheters.24

Contraindications

  • Infection or a wound in the groin, or an inguinal or femoral hernia at the puncture site.
  • Anticoagulation or a bleeding disorder deserves particular caution with the proximal approach, which lies beside the obturator vessels in a site that cannot be compressed easily. A massive thigh haematoma needing transfusion has been reported after a landmark block.1239
  • Patients at high risk of falls: adductor paralysis reduced control of the operated limb in one trial, though not the ability to walk.30

Side effects and complications

Vascular puncture is the commonest complication reported in trials of this block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Bar length shows the number of patients, not a percentage. The trials used different definitions, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Haematoma. A 95-year-old woman developed a large thigh haematoma after a landmark block, needing red cell transfusion; it resolved by day 53 and was attributed to puncture of the obturator artery.39
  • Loss of limb control. After hip arthroplasty, adductor paralysis reduced control of the operated leg compared with placebo but did not affect walking.30 In patients having bladder resection, most of whom were at high risk of falls before surgery, the Timed Up and Go test did not differ between obturator block and neuromuscular block.27
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In a meta-analysis of obturator block for bladder resection, no toxicity occurred in 159 patients.26 Across all peripheral blocks, an Australasian registry found 0.87 episodes per 1000 blocks, lower with ultrasound,40 and a registry of 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks found seizures in 0.08 per 1000.41 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation and a higher risk in small or sarcopenic patients.42 If toxicity occurs, stop injecting, call for help, maintain the airway with 100% oxygen, treat seizures promptly and give 20% lipid emulsion early. Follow the Association of Anaesthetists guideline on management of severe local anaesthetic toxicity for the full sequence.38
  • Nerve injury. No study of this block alone gives a rate. Across ultrasound-guided peripheral blocks, neurological symptoms lasting more than 6 months followed 0.9 per 1000 blocks.41
  • Bladder perforation is a complication of the surgery that the block aims to prevent: in one trial perforation occurred in 2 of 35 patients under spinal alone and none of 35 with the block.43

Pearls of wisdom

  • Test adductor strength, not skin. Half of patients have no obturator skin territory to test.121
  • A spinal does not stop the obturator jerk: adductor contraction occurred in 40% of patients under spinal alone.43 Under general anaesthesia without a relaxant, complete undisturbed resection of lateral wall tumours was achieved in only 61.9% with monopolar and 82.6% with bipolar resection.44
  • Do not assume a femoral or fascia iliaca injection has covered the obturator nerve. Test the adductors.31328
  • For bladder surgery with the distal technique, add the stimulator. It costs about 2 minutes and improves prevention of spasm.3736
  • Proximally, run colour Doppler before you inject; the obturator vessels share the target triangle.1213
  • If obturator externus has a superior fasciculus, go beneath it: in cadavers that reached the canal on 3 of 4 sides, whereas injecting above it reached the canal on none of 5.18
  • For total hip arthroplasty, an obturator block on its own has not reduced opioid use; for hip arthroscopy, it has.30925
  • For the posterior knee, remember the posterior division: it supplies the superomedial capsule, and a distal adductor canal injection can reach its genicular branch.2045

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Proximal or distal for bladder surgery

Two 2026 trials disagree: success 86.7% against 93.3% in one, with no significant difference,10 and freedom from jerk 85.7% against 78.0% in another, which its authors reported as significant in favour of the proximal approach despite the small absolute difference, with more vascular punctures proximally.13 A network meta-analysis ranked the distal stimulator-guided block best against the reflex and the proximal ultrasound-guided block best against perforation.46

Into the obturator canal

Injection at the exit of the obturator canal stained the trunk and anterior branch on all 20 cadaver hemipelvises and the posterior branch on 80%, with spread into the pelvis on 65%.49 A canal approach with 10 mL of lidocaine 1.5% prevented the jerk in six patients.50 These are anatomical and small clinical series.

The hip

Two placebo-controlled trials found no opioid saving after total hip arthroplasty,309 but a subpectineal block cut opioid use by 40% in the first 3 hours after hip arthroscopy.25 The FRONT block, combining iliopsoas plane and subpectineal deposits, has so far been tested in cadavers.48

PENG instead of an obturator block

In one 2026 trial, PENG prevented the adductor jerk as often as an obturator block during bladder resection.47 One trial is not enough to change practice.
