Axillary nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed beside the axillary nerve, either behind the neck of the humerus where it leaves the quadrangular space with the posterior circumflex humeral artery,1 or from the front in the axillary fossa.2 With a suprascapular nerve block it forms the shoulder block, described in 2007.3 The pair rarely affects the diaphragm,45 but gives less pain relief than an interscalene block in the first hours after surgery.67

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The axillary nerve block is not one of the core blocks that Delphi studies have proposed for every anaesthetist; those are the interscalene, axillary brachial plexus, femoral and sciatic blocks.11 The difficulty is in seeing the target. In the first ultrasound description the nerve itself was hard to see, and the block was guided by the artery and the surrounding muscles.1 At the quadrangular space the medial side of the humerus, beside the nerve, lay at a mean depth of 4.0 cm in men and 3.6 cm in women.12

Seen from the front, in the axillary fossa, the nerve was identified more often: in 99% of 92 cadaver sides,2 and on both sides in all 48 volunteers in a scanning study, where it was best displayed anterior to subscapularis.13

Anatomy

The axillary nerve is a terminal branch of the posterior cord. It runs downwards and backwards across the front of subscapularis, passes below the shoulder joint and leaves the axilla through the quadrangular space with the posterior circumflex humeral artery. The space is bordered by teres minor above, teres major below, the long head of triceps medially and the surgical neck of the humerus laterally. Behind the humerus the nerve divides into an anterior branch, which winds round the humeral neck under deltoid and supplies most of that muscle, and a posterior branch, which supplies teres minor and the skin over the lower deltoid.

The course in the paragraph above follows standard anatomical description. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

Dissection studies add detail. The posterior branch separated from the main nerve at the six o'clock position on the glenoid, ran close to the inferior glenoid rim for a mean of 10 mm, then divided into the nerve to teres minor and the superior lateral brachial cutaneous nerve, which reached the skin a mean of 8.7 cm below the posterolateral corner of the acromion.14

Its share of the shoulder joint

The axillary nerve is one of three main sensory supplies to the joint, with the suprascapular and lateral pectoral nerves.15 In 15 dissected specimens the posterosuperior quadrant of the glenohumeral joint was supplied by the suprascapular nerve, the posteroinferior quadrant by the posterior division of the axillary nerve, the anterosuperior quadrant by the superior nerve to subscapularis, and the anteroinferior quadrant by the main trunk of the axillary nerve, in every specimen.16 In a second series, articular branches of the axillary nerve reached the posterolateral humeral head and capsule in all 16 shoulders examined, rising from the quadrangular space deep to deltoid.17 Terminal sensory branches were found in 8 of 9 shoulders, entering the capsule at the posteroinferior-lateral aspect of the greater tuberosity.18 The inferior glenohumeral ligament was supplied by the axillary nerve in 95.08% of 61 shoulders and by the radial nerve in 3.28%.19

Axillary branches to the subacromial bursa, the area richest in nociceptors,15 were described in more than half of the shoulders in a cadaver study quoted by Rothe and colleagues.9

So the suprascapular nerve covers the back and top of the joint, and the axillary nerve much of the bottom and the front. Blocking one without the other leaves part of the joint with an intact supply. A review of the nerve's whole course and the windows where it can be reached with ultrasound was published in 2026.20

Surface anatomy

Posterior approach. The patient sits,21 lies semi-recumbent with the arm slightly flexed and adducted,22 or lies on the side.23 In 88 shoulders, the point where Doppler found the posterior circumflex humeral artery lay on the line from the posterolateral corner of the acromion to the axillary fold in 77%, at a mean of 0.6 of the distance from the acromion (SD 0.1).12 The probe is placed to give a short-axis view of the artery where it winds round the humeral neck, with teres minor cut across and triceps along its length.9

Anterior approach. The arm is abducted and the probe placed in the axillary fossa, on the axillary fold.224

Indications

  • Shoulder surgery, with a suprascapular nerve block. The PROSPECT recommendations for rotator cuff repair name interscalene block, or suprascapular nerve block with or without axillary nerve block, as the regional technique.8 Pooled trials show more pain than after interscalene block in the first hours and similar pain from about 8 hours.10
  • When the diaphragm matters. With the shoulder block, hemidiaphragmatic excursion fell below 25% of baseline in 2 of 15 patients, against 12 of 15 after interscalene block.4 A 2026 review puts the suprascapular block combined with an anterior axillary or infraclavicular block among the distal strategies that offer true phrenic sparing, and places the risk of interscalene block in patients with limited respiratory reserve.25
  • Adding to a suprascapular nerve block already planned. Adding the axillary nerve block improved pain scores in the first 24 hours after rotator cuff repair, against a saline axillary injection.26
  • Proximal humeral fracture fixation and shoulder arthroscopy, as reported cases.27
  • Completing an axillary brachial plexus block. The axillary nerve is not anaesthetised by an axillary plexus block, and the anterior approach was developed to reach it from the same window.2

Nerves blocked

One nerve: the axillary. Its motor fibres go to deltoid and teres minor, so a working block weakens abduction and some external rotation. Its sensory fibres go to the skin over the lower deltoid and to the inferior and anterior parts of the joint capsule.1614

Where the needle goes decides how much of the nerve is reached. After 7 mL injected into the quadrangular space from behind in cadavers, the main trunk was stained in 12 of 12 injections, the anterior division in 10 and the posterior division in 11. The radial nerve was lightly stained in 3 of 12; the posterior cord and musculocutaneous nerve were not stained.23 From the front, latex surrounded the nerve in 96% of 92 injections and spread to the axillary fossa, into subscapularis or to the radial nerve in 8%.2 In a second anterior cadaver study, 10 mL stained the anterior branch and both posterior branches in all six specimens.24

The axillary nerve block does not reach the suprascapular nerve, which is why it is paired with a suprascapular nerve block. Nor does it reach the lateral pectoral nerve or the nerve to subscapularis, which supply the front and top of the joint.1615

With deltoid paralysed by an isolated axillary nerve block in volunteers, abduction strength fell to 76% of baseline at 0° and to 25% at 120°; external rotation strength in 90° of abduction fell to 53%; internal rotation was unaffected.30

Landmark technique

Price described the shoulder block in 2007. No description of an axillary nerve block was available, so he developed a landmark technique for blocking the nerve as it crosses the posterior surface of the humerus, and combined it with a suprascapular block in the supraspinous fossa.3 The surface marks measured later support a point about six tenths of the way from the posterolateral acromion to the posterior axillary fold.12

Landmark and surgeon-placed blocks have been tested in trials. Blind suprascapular and axillary nerve blocks gave lower pain scores than a suprascapular block alone at 1, 6 and 12 hours after rotator cuff repair in 114 patients.31 In a three-arm trial of 130 patients, a surgeon-placed suprascapular block without ultrasound and an axillary nerve block under arthroscopic guidance gave analgesia similar to the same blocks placed with ultrasound by an anaesthetist; both needed more opioid than interscalene block in theatre and in recovery.32

Posterior approach: the quadrangular space

  1. Position. Sitting,21 semi-recumbent with the arm slightly flexed and adducted,22 or lateral.23 The lateral position needs no shoulder abduction.23
  2. Place a linear probe on the back of the arm below the posterolateral corner of the acromion, over the line to the axillary fold.12
  3. Name the landmarks. Deltoid on top; the neck of the humerus deep; teres minor cut across; the long head of triceps cut along its length.9
  4. Find the posterior circumflex humeral artery with colour Doppler, in short axis where it leaves the quadrangular space and winds round the humeral neck.912
  5. Look for the nerve on the cranial side of the artery. If it cannot be seen, the artery and the muscles are enough to place the needle.1
  6. Bring the needle in plane to the space between teres minor, deltoid, triceps and the humerus, with the tip cranial to the artery.1

Anterior approach: the axillary fossa

Why this anterior approach. Several windows to the nerve, front and back, are discussed in a 2026 review.20 For the anterior player I chose the axillary fossa approach because it has the largest anatomical study, 46 cadavers injected on both sides,2 a second cadaver study,24 and a volunteer scanning study showing the nerve best anterior to subscapularis.13 I found no study of comparable size for a lateral window on the humeral neck under deltoid, or for a block at the posterior cord, and no clinical trial of any anterior approach.

  1. Abduct the arm and place the probe in the axillary fossa.2 In one study the subject lay on the side with the arm abducted and the probe on the axillary fold.24
  2. Name the landmarks: the humerus, teres major and subscapularis.2
  3. Find the nerve at the inferolateral margin of subscapularis, or entering the quadrangular space.2 It lies in the superficial layer over the humeral head, underneath teres major in the second description.24
  4. Colour Doppler first. The axillary artery and vein and the circumflex humeral vessels lie in this window. This step follows standard anatomical description, not a cited study.
  5. Bring the needle in plane. In the cadaver study the needle passed from posterior to anterior.24

Anatomical relationships, motor supply and stimulation responses follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

Volunteer study

The first ultrasound description

In 12 volunteers, 8 mL of lidocaine 2% placed cranial to the posterior circumflex humeral artery blocked sensation in all 12 and gave complete motor block in 10. The block lasted about 120 minutes.1

RCTs

How much to inject

Trials of the shoulder block used 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.75%,22 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.5%,21 and 15 mL of levobupivacaine 0.33% on each nerve.33 One trial used 20 mL after imaging showed the best spread with that volume, and its authors thought a smaller volume would probably have been sufficient and safer.9

Observational

How deep

At the quadrangular space the medial side of the humerus, where the nerve lies, was a mean of 4.0 cm deep in men (SD 0.5) and 3.6 cm in women (SD 0.4).12 A long needle is usually needed: 80 mm and 100 mm needles were used in two trials.921

Cadaver studies

Seeing the nerve from the front

The nerve was seen in 99% of 92 cadaver sides in the axillary fossa,2 and in all 18 specimens in a second anterior study.24 In 48 volunteers it was visible on both sides from three windows and best displayed anterior to subscapularis.13

A deltoid twitch. Trials injected after a deltoid response at up to 0.6 mA22 or at 0.5 mA.21 In the volunteer study the needle placement was aided by simultaneous nerve stimulation.1 A teres minor twitch, external rotation, also comes from this nerve; this follows standard anatomical description.

Cadaver studies

Where it should not go

From behind, the radial nerve was lightly stained in 25% of injections.23 From the front, 8% of injections spread to the axillary fossa, into subscapularis or to the radial nerve.2

Observational

The artery is the landmark and the hazard

The nerve runs with the posterior circumflex humeral artery and lies on its cranial side at the target.19 Confirm it with colour Doppler and aspirate before injecting. In one trial, placement outside the vessel was confirmed before every injection.22

Continuous techniques

I found no randomised trial of an ultrasound-guided axillary nerve catheter. In a series of 24 patients having arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, catheters were placed beside the suprascapular and axillary nerves under arthroscopic guidance. Mean pain scores were between 0.44 and 0.63 from the end of surgery to 24 hours, against 6.38 before surgery, and placing the axillary catheter took a mean of 3.75 minutes.34 There was no comparison group. For a longer single shot, see liposomal bupivacaine under Evolving evidence.5

Contraindications

  • Expecting it to anaesthetise the shoulder on its own. After subacromial decompression, the axillary nerve block alone was unpredictable and far from sufficient in a large proportion of patients.9 Combined with a suprascapular block it was studied as analgesia alongside a general anaesthetic.3364
  • Major shoulder surgery where early analgesia is the priority and the diaphragm is not a concern. The combined block does not compare favourably with interscalene block for major procedures.35
  • A vessel that cannot be identified or avoided. The nerve runs with the posterior circumflex humeral artery.1

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is the reason to choose this block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different drugs, definitions and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Deltoid weakness is expected. Ten of 12 volunteers had complete motor block.1 In a trial, motor block was partial in every shoulder block patient, against complete in 88.6% after interscalene block, which weakens the whole arm.21 Selective blocks gave less motor block than interscalene block in a second trial.33
  • Shorter motor block than interscalene block. With liposomal bupivacaine, motor block lasted a mean of 0.88 hours after the combined block against 30.57 hours after interscalene block.5
  • Fewer side effects than interscalene block when pooled. Less numbness or tingling, weakness, Horner syndrome and subjective dyspnoea, with no difference in hoarseness.10 Dyspnoea was reduced with a risk ratio of 0.25 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.68).29 In a three-arm trial, patient-reported nerve complications were more frequent after interscalene block (6 patients).32
  • Spread to the radial nerve. Seen in cadavers from both directions.232
  • Rebound pain. Adding the axillary block to a suprascapular block made rebound pain less frequent in one trial;26 in another, every group had rebound pain.31
  • Nerve or vascular injury specific to this block. None of the trials cited here reports a complication attributed to the axillary nerve block itself in its abstract. The numbers are small, so this cannot be read as a rate.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Find the artery first. The nerve is on its cranial side, and you may not see the nerve at all.1
  • Expect the nerve at about 4 cm. Choose a needle long enough before you start.12
  • Look for a deltoid twitch. One trial injected once it appeared at 0.5 mA.21
  • This block is the second half of a pair. On its own it is not enough for shoulder surgery.9
  • Warn the patient that lifting the arm will be weak, but the elbow and hand will work.2130
  • Plan for the first hours. Give multimodal analgesia before the patient wakes: the gap with interscalene block is largest in recovery.6710

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Does the axillary nerve add anything

Most of the evidence says yes when it is added to a suprascapular block: lower pain in the first day in randomised trials and two pooled analyses.26312938 On its own the picture is different: after subacromial decompression, a selective axillary nerve block reduced pain scores but not early morphine use.9 The nerve matters, but not alone.

Which window

The posterior approach was used in the trials whose full methods I could read.22219 Newer cadaver work describes a trans-quadrangular posterior injection in the lateral position23 and an axillary approach from the front.224 None of these newer techniques has been tested in patients.

How it compares with interscalene block

The trials disagree on the first hours. Several found worse early pain with the combined block,67214 and one found equivalent pain scores.22 Two found less pain with the combined block at 24 hours.64 Pooled, the gap closes by about 8 hours.10 Network meta-analyses still rank interscalene block first for opioid use.37

Longer-acting local anaesthetic

With liposomal bupivacaine for the combined block, pain at rest over 72 hours did not differ from interscalene block in 48 patients, and diaphragm paralysis was 4% against 78.3%.5

Guidelines

The PROSPECT recommendations for rotator cuff repair, published in 2019, name suprascapular nerve block with or without axillary nerve block as the alternative to interscalene block.8
