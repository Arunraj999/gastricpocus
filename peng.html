Pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block

Local anaesthetic laid on the bone of the anterior pelvic brim, between the anterior inferior iliac spine and the iliopubic eminence, beneath the psoas tendon. It was designed to reach the articular branches that supply the front of the hip capsule while leaving the quadriceps working.1 It relieves hip fracture pain within the first hour,23 and it usually spares the quadriceps better than fascia iliaca or femoral blocks.45 It is not guaranteed to spare them: volume matters, and where the injectate actually goes is still debated.67

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The target is deep, lies on bone and is found with a curvilinear probe. In a trial from an experienced unit, each operator had about five years of regional anaesthesia practice after specialist training and had done almost 20 PENG blocks before the trial began.8 Once learnt, it is quick. Against a femoral nerve block, the PENG view was obtained faster in 60 patients with hip fracture.13 Against a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block in 64 older patients, it was quicker to perform and succeeded more often (90.62% against 68.75%).14 Emergency physicians trained in the block have used it safely in the emergency department.15

The name is the one given by its originators.1 The 2024 ASRA–ESRA consensus on limb block names recommends naming a block by the nerve and the place where it is blocked.16

Anatomy

Who supplies the hip capsule. In 13 dissected hemipelves, the femoral and obturator nerves supplied the anterior capsule in every specimen, and the accessory obturator nerve in 7. High branches of the femoral nerve, arising above the inguinal ligament, passed between the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) and the iliopubic eminence (IPE), and in 12 specimens they were the only femoral supply. The accessory obturator nerve, when present, crossed the IPE. Obturator branches arose high, low or both (4, 5 and 4 specimens), and their most constant landmark was the inferomedial acetabulum.17 The PENG block was built on this study, to reach the femoral and accessory obturator branches at the AIIS–IPE gap.1

Where the pain comes from. A review of 21 anatomical papers found the densest nociceptive supply in the superior part of the anterior capsule and the labrum. The femoral and obturator nerves consistently supply the anterior capsule, and the nerve to quadratus femoris the posterior capsule.18 An earlier dissection study described the same anterior and posterior split, and added articular branches from the sciatic and superior gluteal nerves to the back of the joint.19 A meta-analysis of gross anatomical studies concluded that capsular innervation is highly variable.20

Where the injectate goes. This is the unsettled part. In 18 cadavers injected with 10, 20 or 30 mL, the femoral nerve itself was stained in 6 of 12 specimens at 20 mL and in 12 of 12 at 30 mL. The obturator articular branches were rarely stained at any volume, and the obturator nerve was stained in only half even at 30 mL. An accessory obturator nerve was seen in only 3 of 36 specimens (9%).6 In 10 patients given 20 mL with contrast, CT showed the injectate mainly within the epimysium of iliacus and psoas, with minor spread to the capsule and none into the obturator foramen.7 Cryosections and histology in 10 cadavers placed the injectate mostly beneath the epimysium and inside the iliopsoas, close to the femoral nerve and its branches, without consistent spread to the capsule. Small nerve branches were seen within the muscle compartment.21 The block works in trials. Exactly which nerves it reaches is still argued.22

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine. The probe goes transversely just medial and caudal to the anterior superior iliac spine, and is moved until the AIIS, the iliopubic eminence and the psoas tendon are seen together.9 A curvilinear probe is usual because the bone is deep.8 The needle enters at the lateral end of the probe and travels in plane, lateral to medial, towards the bone beneath the tendon.98 Patients with a hip fracture can stay flat for the whole procedure.

Indications

  • Hip fracture, from the emergency department onwards. Peripheral nerve blocks for hip fracture reduce pain on movement within 30 minutes and the risk of acute confusion, in a Cochrane review of 49 trials covering all block types.23 The Association of Anaesthetists revised its analgesia recommendations for hip fracture in 2020,24 and fascia iliaca block is currently recommended as part of multimodal analgesia.2 PENG gave greater pain relief in the first hour than an infra-inguinal fascia iliaca block2 and more than intravenous morphine at 30 minutes.3
  • Positioning for spinal anaesthesia. Pooled across 15 trials and 890 patients, PENG reduced pain during positioning.25
  • Total hip arthroplasty. The 2026 PROSPECT update names a pre-operative PENG block and the supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block as the preferred regional techniques.26
  • Prehabilitation before delayed surgery. It reduced movement pain and rescue analgesia, but did not help patients complete a mobilisation session.27
  • Hip arthroscopy, selectively. Benefits were small and confined to recovery.28
  • Palliative care of an unfixed hip fracture, by cryoneurolysis at the PENG site, in a single case report.29

Nerves blocked

The intended targets are the articular branches of the femoral nerve and the accessory obturator nerve to the anterior hip capsule.1 Obturator articular branches are reached inconsistently or not at all.67 The femoral nerve itself can be reached, increasingly so as the volume rises, which explains the quadriceps weakness seen in some patients.631

The block does not cover the skin of the lateral thigh, which is supplied by the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve,1232 nor the posterior capsule, which is supplied by the nerve to quadratus femoris and branches of the sciatic and superior gluteal nerves.1819

What the block covers

The front of the hip joint. The skin incision and the back of the joint need something else.

Landmark technique

There is no landmark PENG block. The anatomical study behind it was designed for image-guided radiofrequency denervation, and it identified the AIIS–IPE gap and the inferomedial acetabulum as targets.17 The block was described as an ultrasound technique from the start.1 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Supine patient, curvilinear probe.8
  2. Place the probe transversely, medial and caudal to the anterior superior iliac spine. Find the AIIS laterally, the iliopubic eminence medially and the psoas tendon lying on the bone between them.9
  3. Find the femoral artery and nerve superficial and medial to the target. Run colour Doppler over the needle path.
  4. Advance the needle in plane, lateral to medial, until the tip touches bone beneath the psoas tendon, between the AIIS and the IPE.98
  5. Aspirate, then inject in the plane between the tendon and the periosteum. The tendon should lift off the bone.89
  6. Watch where the fluid goes. Spread running medially towards the femoral nerve may mean injection into the iliopectineal bursa and a risk of quadriceps weakness. A more lateral tip and a smaller volume have been suggested.33
  7. Add a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block if the incision is lateral.1232

Cadaver

Volume and the femoral nerve

The volume of dye that spared the femoral nerve in 90% of 54 hemipelves was 13.2 mL (95% CI 12.0 to 20.0).9 In a second cadaver study the femoral nerve was stained in half of the specimens at 20 mL and in all at 30 mL.6

RCT

Motor sparing in patients

After hip arthroplasty, quadriceps motor block occurred in 45% after PENG against 90% after supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block at 3 hours, and 25% against 85% at 6 hours. Pain and opioid use did not differ.4 After hip fracture surgery, quadriceps strength was intact in 60% after PENG against none after femoral block in recovery, and 90% against 50% on day 1.5

Imaging

Where it really goes

Contrast CT in 10 patients showed spread within the epimysium of iliacus and psoas, with minor spread to the capsule and none to the obturator foramen.7 Cryosections in cadavers agree.21 The analgesia may come from branches of the femoral nerve inside iliopsoas rather than from a true pericapsular spread.721

Review

Weakness still happens

Unexpected quadriceps weakness was reported soon after the first description.31 A scoping review put it at 25% or more in recent studies,33 and the originators have since reviewed the mechanisms.22

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. Enter lateral to the probe and keep the whole shaft in view; the bone is deep.8
  2. Pass deep to iliopsoas and stay lateral to the femoral nerve and artery.
  3. Stop on bone beneath the psoas tendon, between the AIIS and the IPE.9 A 22-gauge, 50 mm needle was used in a recent cadaver study.21
  4. Inject slowly in small aliquots, watching the tendon lift. Medial spread towards the femoral nerve suggests a bursal injection.33

Case series

Vessels in the plane

In 10 continuous PENG catheters, blood was aspirated through the catheter in three; each was withdrawn and repositioned 0.5 to 1.0 cm more medially.34

Registry

Ultrasound and toxicity

Across 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, ultrasound guidance was associated with less local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.35

Review

Bursal injection

A tip placed directly under the psoas tendon may enter the iliopectineal bursa, and spread from a ruptured bursa can reach the femoral nerve within the fascia iliaca compartment.33

Nerve stimulation

The target is a plane on bone, not a nerve, and the articular branches are not sought individually. A quadriceps twitch during needling means the needle is close to the femoral nerve. This is a clinical consideration, not the finding of a specific study.

Volumes studied. Most trials used 20 mL: levobupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline,4 bupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline,36 ropivacaine 0.375%,827 ropivacaine 0.5%,3728 ropivacaine 0.3%,38 and levobupivacaine 0.375% with dexamethasone 4 mg.2 Ten millilitres of bupivacaine 0.25% was non-inferior to 20 mL after hip arthroplasty, with no difference in quadriceps weakness.10 Forty millilitres of ropivacaine 0.375% was no better than 20 mL and did not increase quadriceps weakness in 37 analysed patients.11 These are the volumes the trials studied, not a recommendation.

Dose. Keep the total dose of local anaesthetic within the maximum safe dose calculated on lean body weight, and count any other local anaesthetic given, such as wound infiltration or a lateral femoral cutaneous block. Dose per weight and patient weight predicted toxicity in a registry of over 25,000 blocks,35 and small size and sarcopenia, common in patients with hip fracture, add to the risk.39 Monitor the patient during and after injection, and have lipid emulsion and the Association of Anaesthetists' local anaesthetic toxicity guidance to hand wherever the block is done.

Continuous techniques

In 10 patients with hip fracture, a catheter was threaded 3 cm beyond the needle tip. Median pain fell from 7 before the block to 2 at 20 minutes, and stayed at 2 at 12, 24 and 48 hours. Catheters were removed at 72 hours. Blood was aspirated through three catheters, which were repositioned.34

A randomised trial of 60 hip arthroplasties under general anaesthesia, described in its title as a continuous PENG block, reported lower pain at 12 and 24 hours and lower 24-hour opioid use (11.1 against 18.4 mg morphine equivalents) than supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block.40 The abstract does not describe the catheter regimen.

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Anticoagulation. The ESAIC/ESRA guideline applies drug time intervals to deep, non-compressible peripheral blocks and not to superficial, compressible ones.41 The PENG target is deep to iliopsoas on the pelvic brim; decide locally which category it belongs to.
  • When the quadriceps must work at once, use a small volume, or choose another option, because weakness can occur.336

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study, except where referenced.

Side effects and complications

Quadriceps weakness is the side effect that matters for this block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The studies used different volumes, drugs, definitions and time points, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In the Australasian registry, the incidence was 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks; ultrasound was associated with a lower risk, and dose per weight was a predictor.35 Small patient size and sarcopenia add to the risk.39 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not PENG alone.
  • Vascular puncture. Blood was aspirated through 3 of 10 PENG catheters.34
  • Nerve injury. The Cochrane review of blocks for hip fracture found low risks of permanent injury.23 In a meta-analysis of 26 trials and 2208 patients after hip arthroplasty, no major block-related complications were reported.43 No block-related adverse events occurred in a placebo-controlled trial of 100 patients with femoral neck fracture.27
  • Iliopectineal bursal injection, a proposed cause of femoral nerve spread and weakness.33

Pearls of wisdom

  • Volume is the lever for motor sparing. The femoral nerve escaped staining at about 13 mL in cadavers,9 and 10 mL was non-inferior to 20 mL in patients.10
  • Keep the tip lateral, away from the underside of the tendon, and stop if the spread runs medially.33
  • Do not promise a numb thigh. Add a lateral femoral cutaneous block when the incision is lateral; it lengthened analgesia from about 15 to 18 hours after hip fracture surgery.32
  • PENG alone does not reach the back of the joint, and the obturator branches only sometimes.186
  • Block early. In the emergency department, 24 of 32 patients had at least half their pain relieved in the first hour, against 7 of 32 after fascia iliaca block.2
  • Against a femoral block, the evidence on duration conflicts. One trial found PENG lasted about an hour longer (8.7 against 7.6 hours),44 another found more rescue analgesia was needed in the first 24 hours after PENG.13 Plan the next dose of analgesia before the block wears off.
  • Do not use it for subtrochanteric fractures.30
  • Aspirate through every catheter before you inject.34

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Is it really pericapsular?

Imaging in patients and cryosections in cadavers place most of the injectate inside the iliopsoas compartment, not on the capsule.721 The originators have reviewed the competing explanations.22 The answer will shape where the needle tip should go.

How small can the volume be

Ten millilitres was non-inferior to 20 mL,10 and 40 mL was no better than 20 mL.11 The cadaver femoral-sparing volume of 13.2 mL still needs testing in patients.9

PENG plus LFCN

A meta-analysis of 12 trials and 823 patients found that PENG combined with a lateral femoral cutaneous block, compared with a mix of other blocks or no block (PENG alone in only one trial), lowered pain, lengthened time to rescue analgesia and shortened time to walking.49 A second, against fascia iliaca block, found less weakness.48 Most trials are small.

Emergency department, prehabilitation and beyond

A Dutch multicentre trial plans to randomise 254 patients in the emergency department to PENG, fascia iliaca or femoral block, with quality of recovery as the primary outcome.50 Before delayed surgery, PENG eased movement pain but did not improve completion of prehabilitation.27 Cryoneurolysis at the PENG site has been used for palliative care of an unfixed fracture.29
