Phrenic-sparing blocks for shoulder surgery

Why the diaphragm is affected

The phrenic nerve runs on the anterior scalene close to the C5 root, which is the anatomical reason the diaphragm is so often affected by an interscalene block.3 With the landmark technique and 34 to 52 mL, every one of 13 patients studied developed hemidiaphragmatic paresis.1 At the cricoid cartilage the nerve lay a mean of 1.8 mm from C5, and it moved about 3 mm further from the plexus for every centimetre down the neck.2

That anatomy leaves three ways to spare the nerve, and the page is arranged around them:

  1. Change the interscalene block. Use less local anaesthetic, use it more dilute, or place it on the far side of the roots.568
  2. Move down the plexus. Inject where the phrenic nerve is further away: at the superior trunk, above the clavicle, or below it.111417
  3. Leave the plexus. Block the nerves to the shoulder individually, or use a fascial plane block or infiltration.243234

In general, the further the injection is from the roots, the less often the diaphragm is affected. The options furthest away pay for that with more pain in the first hours after surgery.192526

What a shoulder block has to cover

The suprascapular nerve supplies the posterior joint, the axillary nerve the anterior and posterior joint, and the subscapular and lateral pectoral nerves the anterior joint.4 Interscalene and superior trunk blocks reach all of them with one injection, by blocking C5 and C6 before those nerves separate.10 A block of the suprascapular nerve alone, or with the axillary nerve, leaves part of the anterior joint uncovered.4

The skin over the top of the shoulder is a separate matter. Blocks made below the roots do not set out to reach the supraclavicular nerves: one trial gave every patient a cervical plexus block as well,14 and the first description of the superior trunk block added 5 mL superficial to the middle scalene.10

When it matters

  • Phrenic nerve block limits the use of interscalene block in patients with reduced respiratory reserve, and it may be contraindicated in those with pulmonary disease.54
  • Paresis is often silent in other patients. In one trial of 78, only one patient after each block reported breathlessness, both with complete paresis, and both settled within 12 hours.12 In 212 patients having arthroscopy under block and sedation, breathlessness was reported by 10.38% after interscalene block and by none after costoclavicular block.17
  • Choose the approach for the individual patient, taking account of comorbidity and the type of surgery.3

Change the interscalene block

Low-volume interscalene block

Diaphragm
45% with 5 mL against 100% with 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%.5
Analgesia
Pain scores, sleep quality and morphine use to 24 hours did not differ.5 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% was enough for surgery under block.43
Also
Pooled across trials the fall in paralysis was significant (risk ratio 0.62), but the analysis had not reached the sample size needed to be definitive.36
Change the interscalene block

Dilute interscalene block

Diaphragm
Absent or paradoxical diaphragm movement in 21% with 20 mL of bupivacaine 0.125%, against 78% with 0.25%.6
Analgesia
No clinically significant difference in pain scores in that trial.6 With ropivacaine 0.1% against 0.2%, vital capacity was better preserved but the block lasted 11.9 against 18 hours and more opioid was used over 72 hours.7
Also
Less drug at the same site: the diaphragm benefit is paid for in duration.7
Change the interscalene block

Extrafascial interscalene block

Diaphragm
21% with 20 mL placed 4 mm lateral to the sheath, against 90% between C5 and C6.8
Analgesia
Similar analgesia in the trial.8 In a meta-analysis of six trials (485 patients) the risk of paresis was a third of that with intrafascial injection, with slower onset and no difference in the duration of sensory block.9
Also
Rated the best-supported modification in the 2025 meta-analysis of 28 trials.36
Move down the plexus

Superior trunk block

Diaphragm
4.8% against 71.4% after interscalene block in one trial.11 In another, with 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, complete paresis in 5.3% but some paresis in 76.3%.12 31.8% with 10 mL of 0.25%.13
Analgesia
Non-inferior to interscalene block in recovery and at 24 hours.1112 Used as the only anaesthetic, with sedation, in 88 of 88 arthroscopies,13 and compared with interscalene block as the only anaesthetic in 123 patients.44
Also
The effect on the diaphragm depends heavily on dose. Pooled evidence is not yet conclusive.36
Move down the plexus

Supraclavicular block, small volume

Diaphragm
9% against 95% after interscalene block, with 3 mL in the corner pocket and 17 mL posterolateral to the plexus.14
Analgesia
Equivalent pain scores from 30 minutes to 24 hours in that trial.14 In a three-arm trial of 189 patients, non-inferiority to interscalene block was not shown (difference 0.4 points, 95% CI -0.4 to 1.2).16
Also
Meta-analysis of 12 trials: risk of paresis roughly halved (risk ratio 0.56), Horner syndrome reduced, pain and opioid use at 24 hours comparable.15
Move down the plexus

Costoclavicular block

Diaphragm
7.55% against 92.45% after interscalene block in 212 patients.17
Analgesia
Used as the only block under sedation with no failed blocks and comparable pain scores; the suprascapular nerve was slower to block (complete at 20 minutes in 53% against 66%).17 Against superior trunk block, pain at 1 hour was higher by a median of 2 points.18
Also
No Horner syndrome and no breathlessness, against 18.87% and 10.38% after interscalene block.17
Move down the plexus

Infraclavicular block with suprascapular block

Diaphragm
None of 20 patients, against 18 of 20 after interscalene block.19
Analgesia
Pain at 30 minutes was higher by a median of 4 points and 6.1 mg more morphine was used over 24 hours; pain scores from 1 hour onwards were comparable.19
Also
The largest reduction in paralysis of any technique in the 2025 meta-analysis.36
Leave the plexus

Anterior suprascapular block

Diaphragm
Vital capacity 90% of baseline against 67% after interscalene block.16 The operated side kept 40% of ventilation against 3%.38 Not immune: with an axillary block as well, paralysis followed in 40%,22 and dye reached the phrenic nerve in 20% of cadaver injections.23
Analgesia
Non-inferior to interscalene block in trials of 329 and 189 patients.2016 Pooled, interscalene block was better only in recovery, by 0.4 points.21
Also
Grip strength unimpaired in 99% against 49%.20 Less Horner syndrome and hoarseness.21
Leave the plexus

Suprascapular with axillary nerve block

Diaphragm
2 of 15 against 12 of 15 after interscalene block.24 With a posterior approach to the suprascapular nerve, 2%.22
Analgesia
More pain in the first hours: 5.45 against 1.80 in recovery in one trial,26 and a difference of more than 2.5 points for 4 hours in another.25 By 24 hours pain was lower than after interscalene block.2624
Also
A recommended option for rotator cuff repair.37 Pooled across nine trials, breathlessness was a quarter as frequent as after interscalene block.27 Suprascapular block alone, by the landmark posterior approach, gave no clinically important benefit over no block.29
Leave the plexus

Fascial plane blocks and infiltration

Diaphragm
Partial paresis in 3.3% after erector spinae plane block against 46.7% after superior trunk block.32
Analgesia
Erector spinae block: movement pain at 1 hour 5.7 against 0.1.32 Infraspinatus-teres minor block: non-inferior to superior trunk block for the highest pain in 24 hours.33 Intra-articular infiltration: worse pain scores than interscalene block at 2, 4 and 24 hours.35
Also
Peri-articular infiltration did not differ from interscalene block in seven small trials; the authors asked for more.34

A highlighted card means the option met that test in at least one of the trials cited on it, not that it always does. The trials differ in drug, dose and the way paresis was defined.

Catheters

After total shoulder replacement in 75 patients, three catheter sites were compared using ropivacaine 0.2% at 6 mL an hour with no initial bolus. At 24 hours vital capacity had fallen by a mean of 991 mL with an interscalene catheter, 803 mL with a supraclavicular catheter and 464 mL with a suprascapular catheter; only the suprascapular result differed significantly from interscalene. Pain scores and opioid use did not differ significantly between the three.30

After arthroscopic surgery in 100 patients, a suprascapular catheter placed by the surgeon gave worse pain at 24 hours than a superior trunk catheter, by a mean of 0.9 points, with significantly less paresis.31

The approach to the suprascapular nerve matters as well. With 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% on each nerve, paralysis followed in 40% when the suprascapular nerve was blocked from the front of the neck and in 2% when it was blocked from behind; in a third of those affected it lasted beyond 8 hours.22 In cadavers, 5 mL at the anterior site stained the phrenic nerve in 20% of injections in one study and 41.7% in another.2341

Practical points

  • No block near the plexus is free of phrenic involvement: 9% after small-volume supraclavicular block,14 7.55% after costoclavicular block17 and 40% after an anterior suprascapular block combined with an axillary block.22
  • Dose matters at every site. Less volume or a lower concentration reduced paresis at the roots and at the superior trunk.563913
  • When the diaphragm must be preserved, block the suprascapular nerve from behind, not from the front of the neck.22
  • With the selective blocks, plan for more pain in recovery. By 24 hours they did as well as interscalene block or better.252624
  • If the hand has to work afterwards, the anterior suprascapular block left grip unimpaired in 99% of patients, against 49% after interscalene block.20

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

What the pooled evidence supports

A 2025 meta-analysis of 28 trials (1,737 patients) graded the evidence that each technique reduces complete paralysis. It was conclusive for the extrafascial interscalene block (high-level evidence), the dilute interscalene block (moderate) and the supraclavicular block (low). For low-volume interscalene block and for superior trunk block the result was not yet definitive.36

Ranking the blocks

A 2026 network meta-analysis of 25 trials (2,039 patients) ranked suprascapular with axillary block highest for pain scores in the first 24 hours and suprascapular block alone lowest. Paralysis was least frequent with infraclavicular-suprascapular block.42

Finding the dose at the superior trunk

The largest volume of ropivacaine 0.25% that avoided paralysis in 90% of patients was 7.6 mL.40 See the superior trunk block page for how that compares with the volume needed for surgery.

Blocks away from the neck

Erector spinae plane and infraspinatus-teres minor blocks have been compared with superior trunk block in single trials.3233 Neither has yet been compared with interscalene block in the trials collected here.
