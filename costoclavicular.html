Costoclavicular brachial plexus block

An infraclavicular block placed just behind the middle of the clavicle, where the three cords lie together beside the axillary artery. One needle position reaches all three cords,12 and the diaphragm is spared far more often than with blocks above the clavicle.2021

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

This is not one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.39 The view is easy to find once you know it, but the needle path is long and shallow, it passes close to the cephalic vein and the thoracoacromial artery, and in cadavers the lateral cord was the structure it met most often.6 The pleura lies beneath serratus anterior at the bottom of the screen.

Anatomy

Just behind the middle of the clavicle the brachial plexus passes through the costoclavicular space: the gap between the clavicle and subclavius in front, and the first rib and serratus anterior behind. Here the three cords lie together on the lateral side of the axillary artery, with the axillary vein further medial.12

Their arrangement is consistent. The lateral cord is the most superficial, in front of the other two. The medial cord lies directly behind it, and the posterior cord is the most lateral, beside the medial cord.2 Because the cords are so close together, one needle position can reach all three.1

Two layers matter for the spread. A paraneural sheath surrounds the cords; on high-definition ultrasound it was seen in 17.3% of patients before the block and in 94.9% after it, once the injectate had outlined it.3 Inside it, a septum often separates the lateral cord from the medial and posterior cords: it was seen in 46.2% of 120 patients on the first scan, and it kept the first 5 mL in one compartment in 94.16%.4

The pattern on the screen is not always three neat circles. In a retrospective review of scans the space held a median of 4.5 neural structures, from three to eight, in several different arrangements.7

Surface anatomy

The probe sits in the medial infraclavicular fossa, just below the middle of the clavicle, in a transverse plane parallel to it.1 The needle enters in plane from the lateral end of the probe and travels medially.46

Indications

  • Surgery of the elbow, forearm and hand. In the first series of 30 patients, surgery was completed under the block in 97%.1
  • When the diaphragm matters. Against a supraclavicular block, phrenic palsy fell from 45% to 5% in one trial and from 47.5% to 11.4% in another.2021
  • Shoulder surgery in a patient who cannot afford a paralysed hemidiaphragm. Against interscalene block for arthroscopy, pain scores were equivalent and no patient's diaphragm was paralysed.23 Add a cervical plexus block for the skin over the shoulder: every patient in that trial had an intermediate cervical plexus block.23
  • A continuous catheter after hand and wrist surgery.3536

Nerves blocked

All three cords, and so the median, ulnar, radial and musculocutaneous nerves and the axillary nerve. In the first series, complete sensory block of the median, radial, ulnar and musculocutaneous nerves was reached in a median of 30 minutes, and surgery was completed under the block in 29 of 30 patients.1

The suprascapular nerve leaves the plexus above the clavicle, yet the injectate can still reach it. In cadavers it spread up to the trunks above the clavicle and stained the suprascapular nerve in every specimen, without reaching the phrenic nerve.5 In patients having shoulder arthroscopy, complete suprascapular motor block was seen in 53% at 20 minutes and 87% at 30 minutes, against 66% and 91% after interscalene block.25 The skin over the top of the shoulder belongs to the supraclavicular nerves of the cervical plexus, which is why the shoulder trial against interscalene block added a cervical plexus block for every patient.23

What the block covers

The arm from the shoulder to the hand. For shoulder surgery, add a cervical plexus block for the skin, and wait the full time for the suprascapular nerve.

Landmark technique

The costoclavicular approach was described with ultrasound and is performed with ultrasound.1 There is no landmark version of this approach.

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, artery, vein, nerve or bone. Tap a cord to see what it supplies. Switch on Colour Doppler to look for the thoracoacromial artery and the cephalic vein, use the anatomy lens, and open the 3D probe view to see where the probe sits on the patient.

  1. Place the probe below the middle of the clavicle, parallel to it, in the medial infraclavicular fossa.1
  2. Find the axillary artery in short axis, with the vein on its medial side.1
  3. Find the three cords lateral to the artery, deep to subclavius: the lateral cord in front, the medial cord behind it, the posterior cord to the side.2
  4. Run colour Doppler along the needle path. The cephalic vein and the thoracoacromial artery were consistently close to it in cadavers.6
  5. Bring the needle in plane from lateral to medial, keeping the whole shaft in view. In cadavers this path crossed deltoid in six of eight cases.46
  6. Inject in the middle of the cluster, between the three cords, or split the dose above and below the septum.113

Anatomical relationships, motor supply and stimulation responses follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

RCT

One injection or two

A double injection shortened onset from 23.4 to 16.6 minutes, with one extra needle pass and the same success.12 Placed either side of the septum, it raised success at 30 minutes from 64.7% to 91.2%, and complete block of all four nerves from 44.1% to 91.2%.13 Three injections, one at each cord, blocked all four nerves in 85.3% against 52.9%, without lengthening the procedure.14

Dose-finding

How much to inject

Ropivacaine 0.5%: ED50 13.5 mL and ED95 18.9 mL in one study,9 and a minimum effective volume in 90% of 20.9 mL in another.10 Lidocaine 1.5% with adrenaline: 34 mL for 90%.8 For shoulder arthroscopy, 40 mL was no better than 20 mL.30

RCT

Which concentration

Ropivacaine 0.375% was non-inferior to 0.5%, with success of 97% in both groups. The sensory block lasted a median of 455 minutes against 610.11

Observational

How fast

In the first series the median onset of sensory and motor block was 5 minutes, and all four nerves were completely numb by 30 minutes.1 With 20.9 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% the mean time to readiness for surgery was 31.4 minutes.10

Needle path and nerve stimulator

Steer the needle yourself. The panel names each layer the tip crosses. Switch on the nerve stimulator to see which cord answers. Give a 1 mL test dose, then hold the injection button and watch where the fluid goes: in the right place it fills the space among the cords; in the wrong plane it stays above the clavipectoral fascia. Move the tip deeper and inject again to practise a double injection. Or press Play the block to watch the whole sequence first.

Cadaver study

What lies in the needle path

In eight cadaver needle paths the needle crossed deltoid in six and pectoralis minor in three. It touched the lateral cord in six and punctured it in three. The cephalic vein and thoracoacromial artery were always nearby, though no vessel was punctured.6

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

These thresholds were measured during supraclavicular block, not at this site. The median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it.26

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.27

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.28

Continuous techniques

The costoclavicular space has been described as a dependable site for a catheter, where the cords lie close together.35 In a randomised trial of 139 patients after ambulatory hand and wrist surgery, a costoclavicular catheter was no better than a paracoracoid one: complete sensory block in all four territories at 48 hours was 42.6% against 47.8%, with similar pain scores, opioid use, sleep and satisfaction. Choice of approach can follow the operator's preference.36

Before a catheter is placed, check the septum: one study argues for a separate catheter in each compartment.4

Contraindications

  • A needle path that cannot be cleared of vessels with colour Doppler.6
  • A needle tip that cannot be kept in view. The pleura lies beneath serratus anterior.
  • For shoulder surgery, a team that needs a dense block within minutes. Onset was slower than after interscalene block, 21.6 against 14.0 minutes.23

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is uncommon after this block, and far less common than after blocks above the clavicle. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different drugs, volumes, definitions and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Pooled evidence. Across trials against the supraclavicular block, paralysis followed in 16 patients after the costoclavicular block and 115 after the supraclavicular block, a risk difference of −0.41.22 In a meta-analysis of shoulder blocks, combining an infraclavicular with a suprascapular block reduced paralysis by 97% compared with interscalene block.33
  • Against the superior trunk block for shoulder arthroscopy, complete paralysis was 26% less frequent, but non-inferiority for pain at 1 hour was not shown and two patients needed a rescue interscalene block.29
  • Other complications were infrequent. Against other infraclavicular approaches there was no difference in complications,17 vascular puncture or Horner syndrome.18 None related to the technique were reported in the first series.1
  • In a propensity-matched cohort paralysis followed 2.5% of costoclavicular blocks against 39.8% of supraclavicular blocks, and the volume injected was also associated with it.19

Pearls of wisdom

  • Find the cords lateral to the artery before anything else. If you see a pattern other than three circles, look again: the space can hold up to eight neural structures.7
  • Colour Doppler along the whole needle path, not just over the artery.6
  • The lateral cord is the first thing the needle meets. Slow down as the tip reaches it.6
  • If the first millilitres stay on one side of a bright line, that is the septum. Put the rest of the dose on the other side.413
  • For shoulder surgery, add a cervical plexus block for the skin and allow time for the suprascapular nerve.2325

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

One injection, two or three

The septum explains why a single injection sometimes misses part of the plexus.4 Double and triple injections improve consistency in trials,121314 at the cost of more needle passes near the cords.

A shoulder block below the clavicle

The costoclavicular block is now tested as a diaphragm-sparing alternative for shoulder surgery. Analgesia was equivalent to interscalene block in some trials,2324 fell short of the superior trunk block in another,29 and seems to depend on spread above the clavicle.3032

Children

Early observational data show high success and no diaphragmatic palsy in children.3738 Randomised comparisons are still awaited.

Catheters

The tight cluster of cords suggested better continuous block, but a randomised trial found no advantage over the paracoracoid approach.36
