LL-08 · Lower limb

IPACK block

Infiltration of local anaesthetic between the popliteal artery and the capsule of the knee. The target is not a named nerve but a tissue plane: the small articular branches that supply the back of the knee joint, which come in every specimen from the tibial nerve and the posterior division of the obturator nerve.1 The main tibial and common fibular trunks lie nearby and are meant to be left alone. IPACK is almost always paired with an adductor canal or femoral triangle block for the front of the knee, and its added value depends heavily on what else the patient receives: in pooled trials it reduced pain at 6 hours when added to an adductor canal block only in the absence of the surgeon's periarticular infiltration.2

Main use
Posterior knee analgesia after knee arthroplasty, alongside an adductor canal or femoral triangle block34

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The sonoanatomy is not complex, but the working space is. The needle passes close to the popliteal artery and vein to reach a thin plane against the back of the femur, while the tibial and common fibular nerves lie a short distance away in the same fossa. At the distal popliteal fossa the tibial nerve runs most superficially and very close to the popliteal vessels.10 Spread to the nerve trunks is possible even when the injection is placed as intended: in ten cadaver knees, injectate reached the tibial nerve in three and possibly touched the common fibular nerve in another three.9 Operators also have to choose a level and an approach, and the published techniques differ.11

Anatomy

Who supplies the back of the knee. In 15 dissected specimens, articular branches from the posterior division of the obturator nerve and from the tibial nerve supplied the posterior capsule in every case. Branches from the common fibular nerve were present in eight (53%) and from the sciatic nerve in three (20%). The tibial territory spanned the whole posterior capsule; the obturator branch supplied its superomedial part, overlapping with the tibial nerve; and the superolateral part was supplied by the tibial nerve and, when present, by the common fibular or sciatic branches.1

The popliteal plexus. These genicular branches of the posterior obturator and tibial nerves form a popliteal plexus that carries pain from the posterior and intra-articular knee.12 In 30 cadavers the articular branch of the tibial nerve arose 0.5 to 16 cm (mean 5.9 cm) above the joint line. It ran lateral to the popliteal vessels, entwined with them to form a plexus, and was distributed to the posterior capsule below the medial side of the upper edge of the lateral femoral condyle. At this level the capsule lay close to the plexus and to the skin.10

Naming. The ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus of 2024 agreed a template that names limb blocks by the target nerve and the place where the drug is given.13 IPACK does not fit that template neatly, because it is defined by a tissue plane rather than a single nerve, and the acronym remains in general use. It is distinct from a selective tibial nerve block, from a popliteal plexus block given from the distal adductor canal, and from genicular nerve blocks, each of which has its own trial evidence on this page.

Where the injectate goes. In ten fresh-frozen cadaver knees, injectate spread across the popliteal fossa over a mean of 6.1 cm from side to side and 10.1 cm from top to bottom. It did not reach the sciatic nerve above its division, and it consistently surrounded the middle genicular artery, which travels with the articular nerves; but it reached the tibial nerve in three specimens and was possibly in contact with the common fibular nerve in three.9 When 10 mL of dye was injected at two different levels in 14 specimens, both produced a similar area of spread and stained the genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve every time. The proximal injection also stained the superior medial genicular nerve, through spread via the adductor hiatus, while the distal injection consistently stained the superior lateral genicular nerve and the anterior branch of the common fibular nerve.5

The adductor canal connection. The popliteal fossa can also be reached from the front. Ten millilitres of dye in the distal adductor canal stained the popliteal plexus and the genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve in all ten cadaver sides, whereas none of three distal femoral triangle injections reached the popliteal fossa.12 Patients given local anaesthetic at the level of the adductor hiatus had slightly diminished sensation in the sciatic territory, without motor deficit, and contrast was found in the popliteal fossa.14 This matters when an adductor canal block and an IPACK block are combined.

Surface anatomy

Two positions are in use: supine with the knee flexed and the hip turned out, with the probe on the medial side of the distal thigh, or prone, scanning from the popliteal fossa.1015 Two levels have been compared. The proximal level is about one fingerbreadth above the base of the patella; the distal level is at the upper border of the femoral condyles.5 A practical guide is to find the femoral condyles on ultrasound and then slide just proximal until the shaft of the femur appears as a single bright line with the popliteal artery lying on it.

Indications

  • Analgesia for posterior knee pain after total knee arthroplasty, added to an adductor canal or femoral triangle block. Against a sham block, it reduced posterior knee pain at 6 hours (21.7% against 45.8%), but opioid use, quality of recovery and function were similar.4 Given with an adductor canal block and a modified periarticular injection, it improved pain on walking on the first day by 3.3 points compared with periarticular injection alone.3
  • Knee arthroplasty without periarticular infiltration. Pooled trials show a benefit of adding IPACK to an adductor canal block mainly when the surgeon does not infiltrate.2
  • Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, where results are mixed: one trial found less opioid use after bone–patellar tendon–bone autograft,16 while another found no added benefit over a femoral triangle block with local infiltration.17
  • High tibial osteotomy. One trial of 60 patients found longer analgesia with IPACK than with an adductor canal block.18
  • Rescue or special cases. The authors of one negative trial suggested a role as a rescue block, or for patients who cannot take standard multimodal analgesia or who have chronic pain or opioid dependence.19

The ESRA PROSPECT recommendations for knee arthroplasty name a single-shot adductor canal block with periarticular infiltration, paracetamol, an anti-inflammatory drug and a single intraoperative dose of intravenous dexamethasone; they do not list a posterior knee block among the recommended interventions, and describe IPACK as promising but not recommendable on the limited evidence then available.20 The ICAROS group recommends peripheral nerve blocks for knee arthroplasty unless contraindicated.21

Nerves blocked

Articular branches of the tibial nerve to the posterior capsule, which form the popliteal plexus with the popliteal vessels.101

The genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve, stained in every specimen at both injection levels.5

Variable additional branches. A proximal injection stained the superior medial genicular nerve; a distal one stained the superior lateral genicular nerve and the anterior branch of the common fibular nerve.5 Articular branches from the common fibular and sciatic nerves are present only in some people.1

The tibial and common fibular nerve trunks are not targets. Spread to them is an unwanted effect.9 The front of the knee (saphenous nerve, nerve to vastus medialis and other femoral branches) needs a separate block.

What the block covers

The back of the knee joint. No skin is anaesthetised, and the front of the knee is not covered.

Landmark technique

There is no landmark version of IPACK. The nearest equivalent is the surgeon's injection into the posterior capsule during arthroplasty, which in one trial of 195 patients gave results not inferior to IPACK when either was added to an adductor canal block; the authors noted that it is quicker and needs no ultrasound.25 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Position. Supine with the knee flexed and the hip turned out for a medial approach, or prone for a posterior approach.1015
  2. Find the femoral condyles, then slide proximally until the femoral shaft appears. The popliteal artery and vein lie directly on the back of the femur; the tibial and common fibular nerves lie more superficially.10
  3. Choose the level. The distal level is at the upper border of the femoral condyles; the proximal level about one fingerbreadth above the base of the patella.5 In a randomised comparison, posterior knee pain was higher with the proximal level, and the distal level produced no complete tibial or common fibular block.6
  4. Advance the needle in plane, usually from the medial side, deep to the popliteal vessels, aiming for the plane between the artery and the femur. Colour Doppler helps to identify the popliteal and genicular vessels.
  5. Inject in small aliquots after negative aspiration, watching the fluid spread across the back of the femur towards its lateral edge. In one modified technique, part of the dose was placed at the lateral border of the artery and the rest between the artery and the capsule.10
  6. Stop and reassess if fluid tracks superficially around the tibial or common fibular nerve.

RCT

Proximal or distal

In 105 knee arthroplasty patients who all had spinal anaesthesia, local infiltration and a continuous adductor canal block, a proximal IPACK, a distal IPACK and a tibial nerve block were compared. Both IPACK levels preserved common fibular and tibial motor function better than the tibial nerve block. Posterior knee pain over the first 24 hours was higher with the proximal level, and the distal level produced no complete tibial or common fibular block.6

Cadaver

What the dye shows

With 10 mL of dye at each level, spread was similar in area. The proximal injection spread more anteromedially, reaching the superior medial genicular nerve through the adductor hiatus; the distal injection spread more anterolaterally, reaching the superior lateral genicular nerve and the anterior branch of the common fibular nerve.5

Cadaver and case series

A prone, plexus-level approach

Injecting at the level where the tibial articular branch forms the popliteal plexus placed dye on the articular branch and popliteal plexus in 10 cadavers, with mild staining of the tibial nerve and no spread to the common fibular nerve. In 15 patients, none had complete motor or sensory block of the tibial or common fibular nerves; two (13.3%) had partly reduced tibial sensory and motor function on the day of surgery, and four (26.7%) partly reduced common fibular sensation with intact motor function.10

RCT

Distal IPACK with a canal catheter

Added to a continuous adductor canal block, a distal IPACK reduced moderate to severe posterior knee pain against sham at 4 hours (17.1% against 42.8%), 8 hours (11.4% against 45.7%) and 24 hours (11.4% against 34.3%), with better range of movement and walking distance on day 1. Cumulative opioid use did not differ.26

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. Skin and soft tissue of the medial distal thigh, or of the popliteal fossa in the prone approach.10
  2. Deep to the popliteal vessels, keeping the tip away from the tibial nerve, which lies superficial to them.10
  3. Into the plane between the popliteal artery and the posterior femur and capsule.5
  4. Inject while watching spread laterally across the back of the bone, without advancing beyond the lateral edge of the femur.

A nerve stimulator is not needed to find the target, which has no motor nerve. If it is used, a foot movement (plantarflexion for the tibial nerve, dorsiflexion or eversion for the common fibular nerve) means the tip is near a main trunk and should be redirected.

Cadaver

Spread to the trunks

In ten cadaver knees, injectate reached the tibial nerve in three and was possibly in contact with the common fibular nerve in three, although none reached the sciatic nerve above its division.9

RCT

Against a tibial nerve block

In 90 knee arthroplasty patients with a femoral triangle block, IPACK (19 mL ropivacaine 0.2%) and a selective tibial nerve block (5 mL ropivacaine 0.75%) gave similar morphine use on day 1 (6 against 5 mg). Four patients (9%) given the tibial nerve block had motor block of the common fibular nerve.27

Registry study

Ultrasound and toxicity

In an Australian and New Zealand registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, ultrasound guidance was associated with a lower risk of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.28

Narrative review

Technique varies

A review of 35 articles found variation in how IPACK was performed in knee replacement studies and indications that a distal injection at the level of the femoral condyles may be better for posterior knee pain.11

Volumes studied in the cited trials

  • 10 mL of dye at each of two levels, in cadavers.5
  • 20 mL of levobupivacaine 0.25% with adrenaline 1:200,000: 5 mL at the lateral border of the popliteal artery and 15 mL between the artery and the capsule.10
  • 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.25% as a single injection.8
  • 19 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% after knee arthroplasty, and 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% after ligament reconstruction.2730
  • 15 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% combined with genicular nerve blocks.7
  • 20 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% with dexamethasone, for high tibial osteotomy.18
  • Ropivacaine 0.275%, with an estimated 17.99 mL for each of the adductor canal and IPACK blocks giving success in 90%.29

These are the volumes used in the studies, not a dosing recommendation. Whatever volume is chosen, the total dose of local anaesthetic must stay within the maximum safe dose for the drug, calculated on lean body weight. IPACK is nearly always one of two or three injections of local anaesthetic, along with an anterior knee block and often the surgeon's periarticular infiltration; in one trial the patients received 80 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% in total across the blocks and the infiltration.31 In the Australasian registry, dose per kilogram, total dose and patient weight all predicted toxicity,28 and the ASRA advisory names small patient size and sarcopenia as added risks.32

Continuous techniques

IPACK is used almost entirely as a single injection, often alongside an adductor canal catheter. We did not find a randomised trial of a catheter placed in the IPACK plane itself. The evidence for adding a single-shot IPACK to a canal catheter is mixed. In one trial it lowered rest pain only in the recovery room, with no difference in opioid use or walking distance.19 In another, added to local infiltration and a canal catheter, it did not reduce 24-hour morphine use (0.61 against 1.31 mg), but Timed Up and Go was faster on days 1 and 2 and hospital stay shorter.33 A network meta-analysis of 27 trials (2,317 patients) ranked a continuous adductor canal block with a single-shot IPACK highest for pain over 24 to 48 hours, yet only continuous adductor canal block was statistically better than infiltration alone, and the size of the benefit was of doubtful clinical importance.34

Repeated injections. In 46 patients with flexion contracture having knee arthroplasty, repeat IPACK and adductor canal injections on the mornings of days 1 and 2 gave less pain on movement, more range of movement, longer walking distance and less opioid use than repeat adductor canal injections alone.35

Prolonging a single shot. In 90 patients aged 65 or over, time to first rescue opioid after combined adductor canal and IPACK blocks was 4.5 hours with placebo, 13.9 hours with intravenous dexamethasone 4 mg and 18.5 hours with 4 mg given perineurally (2 mg per block), with no motor weakness or nerve injury.36 Perineural dexamethasone is an unlicensed use.

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the injection site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Caution with pre-existing tibial or common fibular nerve deficit, or where early postoperative assessment of the foot is important, because a block-related foot drop can mask a surgical nerve injury.15
  • Caution with anticoagulation: the needle passes close to the popliteal vessels in a non-compressible position. Follow local and national guidance on blocks in anticoagulated patients.
  • Surgery outside the territory, such as the front of the knee alone, without an anterior block.

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study, except where referenced.

Side effects and complications

Spread to the tibial and common fibular nerves is the main concern. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

Cadaver spread, partial sensory change and clinical foot drop are not the same outcome. The studies used different techniques and definitions, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Foot drop. Transient foot drop has been described after IPACK combined with an anterior knee block, including a delayed presentation.3715 In one case, a 69-year-old woman developed foot drop after a distal femoral triangle block and a proximal IPACK; it resolved by the next morning. The authors summarise a retrospective review in which foot drop occurred in 4 of 166 patients given an adductor canal block with IPACK and 2 of 267 given an adductor canal block alone, all resolving by the second day, and they suggest the distal IPACK technique, limited volumes and lower concentrations to reduce the risk.15 A review of the clinical studies found that IPACK did not commonly cause foot drop, and caused less than a tibial nerve block.11 A meta-analysis of 14 trials reported no IPACK-related complications.2
  • Masking a surgical nerve injury. Common fibular nerve palsy is a recognised complication of knee arthroplasty itself, quoted at 0.3% to 1.3%, and a block can delay its recognition. Record foot movement before the block, and treat a new foot drop that does not resolve as the block wears off as a possible nerve injury.15
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks in the Australian and New Zealand registry, with ultrasound associated with lower risk.28 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not this block alone. The ASRA advisory reports a trend towards delayed presentation, which may reflect ultrasound guidance, local infiltration techniques and continuous infusions.32 Keep the patient monitored and within reach of help after injection. If toxicity occurs, stop injecting, call for help, secure the airway and give oxygen, treat seizures promptly and give 20% lipid emulsion early, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline; the ASRA advisory sets out the same principles of treatment.32
  • Nerve injury. In more than 7000 peripheral nerve and plexus blocks, block-related nerve injury occurred at 0.4 per 1000; most neurological symptoms after surgery were unrelated to the block.38
  • Vascular puncture. The needle passes close to the popliteal artery and vein and the genicular vessels; injectate consistently surrounded the middle genicular artery in cadavers.9 Use colour Doppler and aspirate before each injection.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Ask the surgeon first. If the posterior capsule will be infiltrated, IPACK adds little or nothing in most trials.22223
  • Go distal. At the upper border of the femoral condyles, posterior knee pain was lower than with the proximal level, and complete tibial or common fibular block was not seen.611
  • Test and record dorsiflexion and plantarflexion before the block and after surgery. A foot drop after knee arthroplasty may be the block or a surgical nerve injury.15
  • Keep the tip deep to the vessels and watch the nerves. Even a well-placed injection reached the tibial nerve in three of ten cadavers.9
  • Add up the local anaesthetic across the anterior block, IPACK and the surgeon's infiltration before you start.3128
  • A distal adductor canal injection can already reach the popliteal plexus; consider this before stacking a second posterior injection.12
  • Alternatives give similar analgesia: a selective tibial nerve block or a popliteal plexus block. The tibial nerve block carried a risk of common fibular motor block.2739
  • Expect a short block. Perineural or intravenous dexamethasone lengthened the time to first opioid in one trial.36

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

IPACK or selective tibial nerve block

Two triple-blinded trials published in 2026 found no difference in morphine use: 6 against 5 mg on day 1 after knee arthroplasty,27 and 12 against 16 mg at 24 hours after ligament reconstruction, with no complications reported.30 The tibial nerve block caused common fibular motor block in 9% of arthroplasty patients.27

The popliteal plexus block

A 2025 non-inferiority trial found the popliteal plexus block as good as IPACK for posterior knee pain and quicker to perform.39 In volunteers, 10, 20 and 30 mL of lidocaine 1% for a popliteal plexus block produced no significant differences in leg muscle function.49

Does IPACK still add value?

A 2026 meta-analysis of 19 trials found no difference in pain or opioid use, though range of movement and walking distance improved.47 In 140 patients, periarticular injection alone gave outcomes comparable to adductor canal and IPACK blocks with injection over the first 14 days,31 and in bilateral knee arthroplasty IPACK added nothing to infiltration around the arthrotomy.23 Earlier meta-analyses had favoured adding it.5044

Making it last

Dexamethasone 4 mg, perineural or intravenous, lengthened time to first rescue opioid after combined adductor canal and IPACK blocks in older patients.36 Perineural dexamethasone is an unlicensed use.
