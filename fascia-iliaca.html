Fascia iliaca compartment block

Local anaesthetic is placed beneath the fascia iliaca, the sheet that covers iliacus and psoas, and spreads to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves without the needle going near either of them. It can be done below the inguinal ligament or above it in the iliac fossa. The suprainguinal injection spreads further towards the lumbar plexus: in volunteers it gave complete sensory block of the front, side and inner thigh in 80%, against 30% from below the ligament.1 It is one of the most widely used blocks for hip fracture pain, including in the emergency department.23

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The infrainguinal block is a plane block placed well away from the femoral nerve, and it is taught widely outside anaesthesia. In a Canadian trial across seven emergency departments, 208 of 213 emergency physicians completed a two-hour training session with a competency check; their blocks took a median 15 minutes, and 90% took less than 25 minutes.12 In a UK national survey, where the block was given it was most often performed by emergency medicine specialists (50.6%), then anaesthetists (34.6%) and orthopaedic surgeons (14.8%).3 Ultrasound makes the result more predictable: complete loss of sensation over the front, side and inner thigh rose from 47% with loss of resistance to 82% with ultrasound in 80 patients.13 In an earlier feasibility study, emergency physicians given brief training performed ultrasound-guided blocks in 20 patients; mean pain fell by 76% at 120 minutes, with no procedural complications.14 The suprainguinal approach asks a little more: in a hip fracture trial it took a median 4.6 minutes to perform, against 3.1 minutes for a PENG block.15

Anatomy

The ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus recommends naming limb blocks by the nerve or plane and the anatomical location of the injection.16 This page uses the names found in the trials it cites: suprainguinal and infrainguinal fascia iliaca block. The fascia iliaca lines the inner surface of iliacus and psoas. The femoral nerve and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve run in the iliac fossa beneath it; the femoral nerve leaves the pelvis between iliacus and psoas, and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve passes under the inguinal ligament close to the anterior superior iliac spine. The obturator nerve runs along the medial border of psoas, deep in the pelvis, and leaves through the obturator canal. Dalens described the fascia iliaca as bounding a potential space in which the femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves run for much of their course, and introduced the block on that basis.5

The space is real for two nerves, less so for the third. In 50 adults given a landmark fascia iliaca injection with contrast, sensory block was obtained in 88% for the femoral nerve, 90% for the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and 38% for the obturator nerve. Across 92 injections imaged in that study, the solution spread under the fascia iliaca and reached the lumbar plexus on only five radiographs.17 With needle placement checked on ultrasound and spread on MRI in ten patients given a 3-in-1 or fascia iliaca injection at the inguinal ligament, local anaesthetic tracked over iliacus and psoas up to the retroperitoneum, but never medially to the obturator nerve; no patient lost hip adductor strength.9

Above the ligament, spread goes further up. In ten volunteers given 40 mL of lidocaine 0.5% on each side, one approach per side, MRI showed spread to the expected position of the obturator nerve in 8 of 10 after the suprainguinal injection and in 1 of 10 after the infrainguinal one. The suprainguinal injection spread further cranially, and the infrainguinal injection further caudally.1

Cadaver studies of volume. With 20 mL of dye placed suprainguinally in six cadavers, the femoral nerve was stained in all twelve injections and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve in every cadaver where it was present. The ilioinguinal nerve crossed iliacus in eight hemipelves and was stained in seven of them.6 A pilot study using CT and dissection suggested 40 mL to reach all three target nerves.18 In a dose-finding study the volume of dye that stained the femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous and obturator nerves in 90% of specimens was 62.5 mL.19 With 30 mL, the obturator nerve was not stained by either approach; it was stained only after 60 mL given above the ligament.20

Vessels near the needle path. In 100 cadavers, at least one superficial and one deep circumflex iliac artery were found around each femoral triangle. Every deep circumflex iliac artery ran between the fascia lata and the fascia iliaca, and these vessels may lie on the needle path superficial to the fascia iliaca.21

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg straight. Mark the anterior superior iliac spine, the pubic tubercle and the inguinal ligament between them, and feel the femoral artery. The landmark injection point is just below the ligament at the junction of its lateral third with its medial two thirds.5 For the infrainguinal ultrasound block the probe lies across the thigh just below the ligament, lateral to the femoral artery. For the suprainguinal block the probe is turned along the long axis of the body, above the ligament and medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, where the needle is directed into the fascia iliaca compartment.719 The needle usually enters from the caudal end of the probe.

Indications

  • Hip fracture, from the emergency department to theatre. Across 11 trials and 503 patients, peripheral nerve blocks reduced pain on movement within 30 minutes by the equivalent of 2.5 points on a 0 to 10 scale.2 Fascia iliaca block is recommended as part of multimodal analgesia for hip fracture, and the Royal College of Emergency Medicine has a best practice guideline on its use in the emergency department.22 The Association of Anaesthetists revised its analgesia guidance in its 2020 hip fracture guideline.23
  • Positioning for spinal anaesthesia in hip fracture. Pooled trials found a shorter time to perform the spinal after fascia iliaca block than after opioids.24
  • Analgesia after total hip arthroplasty, particularly by the suprainguinal route. The 2026 PROSPECT update recommends the suprainguinal fascia iliaca block or a preoperative PENG block as the preferred regional techniques.4
  • Femoral shaft and thigh surgery, by single injection or catheter.2526
  • Children having thigh surgery. The block was first described in 60 children, with effective analgesia in more than 90%.5

Nerves blocked

Femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves: the reliable targets. After ultrasound-guided injections deep to the fascia iliaca at the inguinal ligament (3-in-1 or fascia iliaca landmarks), every one of ten patients had sensory loss over the front, side and inner thigh and significant weakness of knee extension.9 In a hip fracture dose-finding study, an effective block was defined as loss of pinprick in both territories.32

Obturator nerve: inconsistent, even from above the ligament. In a 2026 trial of 84 patients given a 40 mL suprainguinal block before knee arthroplasty, the obturator nerve was blocked in 2.4% of those who received a placebo obturator injection.10 An electromyographic trial did show a greater fall in adductor longus response after suprainguinal than infrainguinal injection of 30 mL.31

Other nerves: the genitofemoral nerve was blocked in 34% after a landmark injection.17 The ilioinguinal nerve can be reached in the iliac fossa.6 The sciatic nerve and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh lie outside the compartment and are not blocked.

Landmark technique

The original block was an injection behind the fascia iliaca at the junction of the lateral third with the medial two thirds of the inguinal ligament, with finger compression to push the solution upwards.5 The needle passes through the fascia lata and then the fascia iliaca, and the operator feels a loss of resistance at each. All 11 trials in a 2018 systematic review of preoperative fascia iliaca block for hip fracture, 1062 patients in total, used this loss of resistance technique; block success was high and adverse effects very few.24 Against intramuscular morphine in the emergency department, a landmark block with mepivacaine 1% gave better pain relief at rest and on movement, and median total morphine consumption was 0 mg against 6 mg.33 Ultrasound raised complete thigh anaesthesia from 47% to 82%.13 This page does not describe the landmark technique further.

Ultrasound technique

Infrainguinal approach

  1. Place the probe across the thigh just below the inguinal ligament and find the femoral artery and, lateral to it, the femoral nerve on iliopsoas.
  2. Slide laterally until iliacus fills the image, covered by the bright line of the fascia iliaca, with sartorius superficial and lateral.
  3. Advance the needle in plane from lateral to medial through the fascia lata and the fascia iliaca, well lateral to the nerve.
  4. Inject beneath the fascia iliaca and watch the fascia lift off iliacus and the solution spread medially towards the femoral nerve and laterally towards the iliac crest.

Suprainguinal approach

  1. Turn the probe along the long axis of the body, medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, and identify iliacus lying on the ilium with the fascia iliaca on its surface, and the abdominal wall muscles above.7
  2. Run colour Doppler. The deep circumflex iliac artery runs between the fascia lata and the fascia iliaca and can lie on the needle path.21
  3. Advance the needle in plane from caudal to cranial. Aim for the tip to lie between internal oblique and iliacus, beneath the fascia iliaca.19
  4. Inject and watch the fascia iliaca lift from iliacus as the solution spreads up into the iliac fossa.67

RCT

Above or below the ligament

In ten volunteers, each given one approach on each side, complete sensory block of the front, side and inner thigh followed 80% of suprainguinal and 30% of infrainguinal injections. Neither approach had a significant effect on motor function at that dose of lidocaine 0.5%.1 In a meta-analysis of seven studies and 660 hip surgery patients, the suprainguinal approach lengthened time to first rescue analgesia by a mean 121 minutes and reduced opioid consumption.34

RCT

Ultrasound against loss of resistance

In 80 patients having hip or knee replacement, ultrasound raised sensory loss of the inner thigh from 60% to 95%, and complete loss over the front, side and inner thigh from 47% to 82%. It also increased femoral and obturator motor block.13

Cadaver

Volume and spread

20 mL of dye placed above the ligament stained the femoral nerve in 12 of 12 injections.6 40 mL was the suggested volume to reach all three nerves in a pilot CT study.18 Staining all three in 90% of specimens needed 62.5 mL of dye, far more than is used clinically.19

MRI study

The obturator nerve

No medial spread to the obturator nerve and no loss of hip adductor strength in ten patients after injections at the inguinal ligament.9 Not stained at 30 mL by either approach in cadavers.20 Blocked in only 2.4% after 40 mL above the ligament in a 2026 trial.10

Needle path and nerve stimulator

This is a plane block: the needle tip goes to a fascial layer, not to a nerve. A nerve stimulator is not needed for the block itself; a catheter was placed faster without one than for a stimulator-guided 3-in-1 block.25

Cadaver

Arteries on the way in

In 100 cadavers, every deep circumflex iliac artery ran between the fascia lata and the fascia iliaca, and in 94% of femoral triangles at least one arterial branch heading for the lateral thigh arose from the femoral artery between the inguinal ligament and its first bifurcation.21

RCT

Time at the bedside

A suprainguinal block took a median 4.6 minutes, against 3.1 minutes for a PENG block.15 Emergency physicians took a median 15 minutes for the whole procedure after training.12

Systematic review

Two pops

The loss of resistance technique was the method in every trial of a 2018 systematic review; success was high and adverse effects very few.24 With a landmark technique, sensory block of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve was faster than after a 3-in-1 block.17

RCT

See the spread

Compared with loss of resistance, ultrasound also increased motor block of the femoral and obturator nerves.13 Ultrasound observation of the injectate is one of the recommended safeguards when large volumes are used for fascial plane blocks.35

Volumes and doses studied. These are the volumes used in the cited trials, not a dosing recommendation. Most adult trials used 20 to 40 mL: 30 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline in the emergency department,36 40 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% above the ligament after hip arthroplasty, a mean 2.6 mg/kg (range 2 to 3.4 mg/kg), with no total or free ropivacaine level above the maximum tolerated concentration in the 10 patients sampled.7 For ropivacaine 0.25% above the ligament in hip fracture, the estimated 95% effective volume for femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous block was 26.99 mL.32 In children given 0.7 mL/kg of ropivacaine 0.5% (3.5 mg/kg), three of four had plasma concentrations outside the tolerable range, and the study was stopped.8

Maximum safe dose. Fascial plane blocks rely on volume, and volume pushes the total dose towards the maximum. Work out the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient, based on lean body weight, before choosing volume and concentration. A narrative review of fascial plane blocks recommends weight-based doses and a lower concentration as the volume rises, so that the total dose stays below the maximum.35 Small patient size and sarcopenia, both common in hip fracture, increase the risk of systemic toxicity.37

Continuous techniques

In 44 patients after cruciate ligament or femur surgery, a fascia iliaca catheter placed without a nerve stimulator was quicker and cheaper to insert than a 3-in-1 catheter placed with one, gave the same analgesia over 48 hours and lay under the fascia iliaca just as often. The bolus was 5 mg/kg of ropivacaine 0.5% and the infusion 0.1 mL/kg/h of ropivacaine 0.2%.25

In a UK propensity-matched cohort of 2187 hip fracture patients, 915 received a suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter placed by an anaesthetist. There were no serious complications from the catheter, and unplanned removal occurred in 146 of 852 (17.1%). Thirty-day mortality, pressure ulcers and length of stay did not differ; return to usual residence was 79.3% against 75.1% (P = 0.06).28 In a trial of 60 patients after proximal femur fracture surgery, 15% of those with a suprainguinal catheter needed rescue morphine, against 40% with an infrainguinal catheter and 50% with a femoral catheter.27 A continuous PENG block gave better pain scores and quadriceps strength than a continuous fascia iliaca block after hip arthroplasty in 57 patients.38

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the injection site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Previous groin surgery or a femoral vascular graft on the side to be blocked (relative): the anatomy may be changed.
  • Anticoagulation: follow local and national guidance. In the UK paramedic feasibility trial, nine patients allocated to a block did not receive one because they were taking anticoagulants under the trial protocol.39
  • When early full weight bearing and unassisted walking are planned, as after day-case hip arthroscopy: quadriceps weakness and falls outweighed any benefit in one trial.30

Apart from the referenced points, these are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Quadriceps weakness is the commonest effect that changes care. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

Quadriceps motor block was defined as paresis or paralysis of knee extension. Compare within the trial, not across trials.

  • Falls. After 40 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% before hip arthroscopy there were four falls in the block group and one with placebo, with no improvement in pain.30 After hip arthroplasty, more patients had muscle weakness at 6 hours with a suprainguinal block than with periarticular infiltration.40
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In studies of fascial plane blocks, mean plasma concentrations were usually well below toxic thresholds, but several patients crossed them, and mild neurological symptoms and even seizures have been reported.35 High concentrations were found in children after 3.5 mg/kg of ropivacaine.8 The ASRA advisory notes a trend to delayed presentation and a higher risk in small and sarcopenic patients.37 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, maintain the airway with 100% oxygen, control seizures and give 20% lipid emulsion, following the Association of Anaesthetists safety guideline.
  • Bleeding. In a cluster randomised trial of emergency physician blocks, the only safety event reported was one minor haematoma.12
  • Nerve injury. No fascia iliaca specific incidence was found for this page. The Cochrane review of nerve blocks for hip fracture confirmed low risks of permanent injury.2 In nine emergency department trials of femoral, 3-in-1 and fascia iliaca blocks, no patient had a life-threatening complication, although minor complications were probably under-reported.41
  • Catheter problems. Unplanned removal in 17.1% of suprainguinal catheters, with no serious complications.28

Pearls of wisdom

  • For the hip, go above the ligament. The suprainguinal injection spreads further up the iliac fossa and gives more complete thigh anaesthesia.134
  • Do not promise an obturator block. Even 40 mL above the ligament rarely reaches it.910
  • Tell the ward the leg will be weak. Quadriceps weakness is the rule after a suprainguinal block, and falls have followed.1130
  • Dose on lean body weight. The patients are often small, old and sarcopenic, and the volumes are large.3735
  • Scan with colour Doppler before you needle. The circumflex iliac arteries cross the path superficial to the fascia iliaca.21
  • Give it time before positioning for a spinal: pain at rest fell significantly only by 30 minutes after a suprainguinal block.15
  • If the patient is already comfortable after prehospital morphine, a landmark block may add little.36
  • Teach it. After two hours of training, emergency physicians went from blocking 2.2% to 52.9% of their hip fracture patients, and delirium fell.12

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Blocks in the emergency department and delirium

In a 2025 stepped-wedge trial in seven Canadian emergency departments, training emergency physicians raised fascia iliaca block use from 2.2% to 52.9% of patients with hip fracture, and delirium within 7 days fell (odds ratio 0.72). The number of days of delirium did not.12 In the UK, 44% of emergency departments responding to a 2010 survey used regional blocks for hip fracture, fascia iliaca block in 22% of those;49 by 2014 to 2015, 61.8% of trusts routinely gave the block.3

Fascia iliaca or PENG

An emergency department trial in Anaesthesia found more pain relief in the first hour with PENG than with the infrainguinal block (summed pain intensity difference 62.7% against 38.0%), without a difference in rescue opioid.22 Against the suprainguinal block, results are mixed: equal dynamic pain relief in one trial,45 higher PENG success in another.47

New hip arthroplasty guidance

The 2026 PROSPECT update reviewed 103 studies and recommends the suprainguinal fascia iliaca block or a preoperative PENG block as the preferred regional techniques, with local infiltration as the alternative.4

The obturator question

A 2026 trial found the obturator nerve rarely blocked by 40 mL above the ligament, and adding a separate obturator block did not reduce morphine use after knee arthroplasty.10 A 2026 meta-analysis favoured the suprainguinal approach for hip surgery.34
