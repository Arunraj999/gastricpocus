Information for patients

Plain-language information for patients offered a nerve block. A leaflet for each block will follow as the block pages are completed.

For clinicians: these are drafts. Patient information must pass your hospital's patient-information approval process before it is given to patients.

Draft Nerve block for surgery on the shoulder, arm or hand

A nerve block is an injection of local anaesthetic around the nerves that supply your arm. It makes the arm numb and heavy. It can be used on its own, so that you stay awake, or together with a general anaesthetic to give pain relief afterwards.

What to expect

  • You will have a small cannula placed in a vein and be attached to monitors.
  • The anaesthetist uses an ultrasound scanner to see the nerves, cleans the skin and numbs it before the block needle goes in.
  • Your arm becomes warm, heavy and numb over about 15 to 30 minutes.
  • If you are staying awake, the operation does not start until the anaesthetist is satisfied that the block is working.

Benefits

  • You avoid, or need less of, a general anaesthetic.
  • Good pain relief in the first hours after surgery, with less need for strong painkillers.
  • You can usually eat and drink soon after the operation.

Risks

  • The block may not work fully. Your anaesthetist can add more local anaesthetic, give other pain relief, or give a general anaesthetic.
  • With a block in the neck for shoulder surgery, a hoarse voice, a drooping eyelid or a feeling of not taking a full breath on that side are common and wear off with the block. Tell your anaesthetist beforehand if you have a lung condition.
  • Nerve injury related to the block is uncommon. In an audit of more than 7,000 blocks it occurred in about 4 in every 10,000.
  • A serious reaction to the local anaesthetic is rare, about 1 in 1,000 blocks in the same audit. Your anaesthetist is trained to treat it.

Afterwards

  • The arm usually stays numb for between 6 and 24 hours, occasionally longer.
  • Keep the arm in the sling and protect it from heat, sharp objects and pressure. Do not sleep on it.
  • Start your regular painkillers before the block wears off, including before bed. Pain can return quite suddenly.
  • Contact the anaesthetic department if the numbness or weakness has not gone by 48 to 72 hours.

Your anaesthetist will discuss the choices with you and answer your questions. You can change your mind at any time.

Draft Spinal anaesthetic

A spinal anaesthetic is an injection of local anaesthetic into the fluid around the nerves in your lower back. It makes you numb from about the waist down for a few hours, so that an operation on the lower body can be done while you are awake or lightly sedated.

What to expect

  • You sit or lie on your side and curl your back. The skin is cleaned and numbed first.
  • Your legs become warm, heavy and numb within a few minutes.
  • You may feel pushing or movement during the operation, but it should not be painful. Tell your anaesthetist if anything is uncomfortable.
  • You can usually listen to music, and sedation can be given if you would like it.

Benefits

  • You avoid a general anaesthetic.
  • You are comfortable when the operation finishes and can usually eat and drink soon afterwards.

Risks

  • Low blood pressure, itching and difficulty passing urine can happen while the spinal is working, and are treated if they occur.
  • A headache that is worse on sitting up can follow a spinal. Tell the team if this happens, as it can be treated.
  • Permanent harm is very rare. A national UK audit of more than 700,000 spinals and epidurals found it in between 1 in 24,000 and 1 in 50,000.

Afterwards

  • Feeling and strength return over a few hours. Do not try to stand until the staff have checked your legs.
  • Tell the staff straight away if new numbness, weakness or severe back pain develops after the spinal has worn off.

Your anaesthetist will discuss the choices with you and answer your questions. You can change your mind at any time.

Draft Nerve blocks for hip and knee surgery

A nerve block for hip or knee surgery is an injection of local anaesthetic near the nerves that carry pain from the joint. It is usually given in addition to a spinal or general anaesthetic, to give pain relief afterwards.

What to expect

  • The block is done with an ultrasound scanner, in the groin or thigh, usually just before the operation.
  • You may be awake, sedated or already numb from a spinal when it is done.

Benefits

  • Less pain in the first hours after surgery and less need for strong painkillers.
  • Some blocks are chosen because they reduce pain while leaving the thigh muscle working, to help you walk early.

Risks

  • The leg can feel weak or unsteady while the block is working. Do not walk until the physiotherapy or nursing team have checked you.
  • The block may not take away all the pain. Other pain relief is always prescribed as well.
  • Nerve injury related to a block is uncommon, about 4 in every 10,000 in an audit of more than 7,000 blocks.

Afterwards

  • Take the regular painkillers you are offered, even if you are comfortable, so that they are working when the block wears off.
  • Tell the staff if numbness or weakness lasts longer than you were told to expect.

Your anaesthetist will discuss the choices with you and answer your questions. You can change your mind at any time.

Draft Nerve blocks for breast and chest wall surgery

These blocks place local anaesthetic between the muscles of the chest wall, where it spreads to the nerves that supply the breast, armpit or ribs. They are given with a general anaesthetic, or occasionally with sedation, to reduce pain after the operation.

What to expect

  • The block is done with an ultrasound scanner, usually after you are asleep.
  • More than one injection may be used, depending on your operation.

Benefits

  • Less pain after surgery and less need for strong painkillers.

Risks

  • The numb area may not cover the whole wound. Other pain relief is always prescribed as well.
  • Because a larger volume of local anaesthetic is used, your anaesthetist works out a safe maximum dose for your size before the block.
  • Bruising at the injection site can occur. Serious complications are rare.

Afterwards

  • Numbness over the chest wall wears off over several hours.
  • Take the regular painkillers you are offered so that they are working when the block wears off.

Your anaesthetist will discuss the choices with you and answer your questions. You can change your mind at any time.
