Supraclavicular brachial plexus block

Local anaesthetic placed around the trunks and divisions of the brachial plexus where they cross the first rib beside the subclavian artery. One quick injection site gives reliable anaesthesia of the arm: 94.6% after a single attempt in 510 consecutive patients.3 The price is its neighbours. The pleura is beside the target, and the hemidiaphragm is paralysed in about a third of patients.21

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

This is not one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.41 The view is easy to find and the block is quick, but the target lies a few millimetres from the pleura. In the largest published series the blocks were done by 47 operators at different levels of training, with no symptomatic pneumothorax.3 Authors who have reported a pneumothorax recommend specific training in keeping the needle in view for this block.33

Anatomy

In the supraclavicular fossa the trunks of the brachial plexus are dividing. They lie together in a cluster on the lateral side of the subclavian artery, above the first rib, and on ultrasound they look like a bunch of grapes. The upper trunk is the most superficial and lateral, and the lower trunk the deepest, next to the artery and the rib. The pleura lies on either side of the rib.

Two features of this arrangement shape the technique. The angle between the artery and the rib, beneath the cluster, is the corner pocket.25 And between the upper and middle trunks, and between the middle and lower trunks, there are planes of fatty tissue.10

A branch of the subclavian artery, the dorsal scapular or the transverse cervical artery, can pass through the plexus at this level. It has been seen on the scan before the block and avoided,35 and in one report it walled off part of the plexus from the local anaesthetic and left a partial block.34

Surface anatomy

The probe sits in the supraclavicular fossa, just above the clavicle, in a coronal-oblique plane.44 The head is turned to the other side. The needle enters in plane, in most descriptions at the lateral end of the probe.544

Indications

  • Surgery of the elbow, forearm and hand. In 510 consecutive patients surgical anaesthesia followed a single attempt in 94.6%.3 In a three-way randomised trial, success was 95 to 97.5% for supraclavicular, infraclavicular and axillary blocks alike.15
  • When a quick block is wanted. Supraclavicular and infraclavicular blocks took fewer needle passes and less time to perform than axillary block.15
  • Shoulder surgery, as an alternative to interscalene block. A meta-analysis found no difference in rescue analgesia, with less hoarseness and Horner syndrome.37 With a small volume, 3 mL in the corner pocket and 17 mL posterolateral to the plexus, pain scores were equivalent to interscalene block and the hemidiaphragm was paralysed in 9% against 95%.38 In a three-arm trial, however, supraclavicular block did not meet the margin for non-inferiority to interscalene block.39
  • Closed reduction of distal radius fractures in the emergency department.43

Nerves blocked

All three trunks and their divisions, and so every nerve of the plexus beyond this point. The weak spot is the lower trunk. After a single injection in the corner pocket, nine of 11 failures were incomplete in the ulnar territory,14 and a pooled analysis found incomplete ulnar block less often after a double or triple injection infraclavicular block.17

The shoulder is better covered than with blocks below the clavicle: the suprascapular and axillary nerves were blocked more consistently than after retroclavicular block.30 The skin over the top of the shoulder belongs to the supraclavicular nerves of the cervical plexus; one trial of shoulder surgery added a cervical plexus block for this reason.38 The inner upper arm is supplied by the intercostobrachial nerve, which is not part of the plexus.

What the block covers

The limb from the shoulder to the hand, with the ulnar side of the hand the least reliable.

Landmark technique

Before ultrasound this block was done by landmarks and nerve stimulation, and the reported risk of pneumothorax was as high as 6.1%.31 In a randomised comparison the nerve stimulator technique also paralysed the hemidiaphragm in 53% of patients, against none with ultrasound.20 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, artery, vein, nerve or bone. Tap a trunk to see what it supplies. Switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, open Variations to see a vessel crossing the plexus, then use Test me.

  1. Find the subclavian artery in short axis and the plexus beside it.3
  2. Name the two bright lines. The first rib is beneath the artery and casts a clean shadow. The pleura lies on either side of it and slides with breathing.
  3. Run colour Doppler over the plexus to look for a vessel crossing it.3435
  4. Bring the needle in plane. A lateral-to-medial direction is the one used in the technique studies cited here.544 Both directions were used in the 510-case series;3 the first reported pneumothorax followed a medial-to-lateral insertion.32
  5. Go to the corner pocket first: the tip where the first rib meets the artery.25
  6. Many then add a second injection among the trunks.57

Anatomical relationships, motor supply and stimulation responses follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

RCT

One injection or two

A second injection brought a faster onset (17.5 against 21.7 minutes) at the cost of a longer block time, so the total time was the same and success was 95.7% either way.7 Across trials of brachial plexus blocks, success did not differ with the number of injections, and one injection caused less paraesthesia.19

RCT

Inside the cluster

Targeted intracluster injection shortened onset to 10.1 minutes against 18.5 for a double injection, with more needle passes.8 In cadavers, though, a single intracluster injection left ink beneath the perineurium in 24% of cases, and the authors could not recommend the technique.9

RCT

Between the trunks

The intertruncal approach places the local anaesthetic in the fatty planes between the trunks, away from the pleura and outside the nerves.10 Against the corner pocket it blocked the ulnar nerve faster in two trials.1112 Against the classical approach, non-inferiority was not confirmed, and paresis of the hemidiaphragm was more frequent.13

Dose-finding

How much to inject

Reported volumes vary with the drug and the method. Lidocaine and bupivacaine mixture: 23 mL for 50% of patients and a calculated 42 mL for 95%.4 Lidocaine 1.5%, double injection: 32 mL for 90%.5 Mepivacaine 1.5%: 17 mL for 95%, with a wide interval (13 to 42 mL).6 With ropivacaine 0.375%, 20 mL worked as the sole anaesthetic in 81% and 30 mL in 91%.23

Needle path and nerve stimulator

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

These thresholds were measured during this block. In 39 patients having an ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block, the median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it. A twitch at 0.2 mA or less was never seen outside the trunk. A twitch between 0.2 and 0.5 mA did not rule out an intraneural tip, and thresholds were higher in patients with diabetes.24

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.25

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root (mean peak 8.2 psi) and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.26

Case reports

Keep the tip in view

The first pneumothorax reported after an ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block was published in 2010.32 In a later case the patient had no breathing symptoms, and pain began late and on the side that had not been operated on, which delayed the diagnosis.33

Block performance

94.6%

Surgical anaesthesia after a single attempt in 510 consecutive patients; 2.6% needed an unplanned general anaesthetic.3

95%

Success after one attempt in the first ultrasound-guided series of 40 outpatients.1

99.5%

Surgery completed without general anaesthesia in 201 of 202 blocks performed by orthopaedic surgeons.44

8.5 min

Time to perform an axillary block in the three-way trial; supraclavicular and infraclavicular blocks took about 6 minutes.15

Continuous techniques

After total shoulder arthroplasty, 75 patients were randomised to a continuous interscalene, supraclavicular or suprascapular block. At 24 hours vital capacity had fallen by a mean of 991 mL with the interscalene catheter, 803 mL with the supraclavicular catheter and 464 mL with the suprascapular catheter. Pain scores and opioid use did not differ significantly between the groups.45

Contraindications

  • A patient who cannot afford to lose one hemidiaphragm. Complete paralysis followed in about a third of patients,21 and no volume from 5 to 40 mL avoided paresis.22
  • A needle tip that cannot be kept in view. The pleura is beside the target.33
  • A vessel through the plexus that cannot be avoided.3435

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is common, and more common than after blocks below the clavicle. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different drugs, definitions and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Volume does not solve it. In a dose-escalation study paresis was present at every volume, from 33% at 5 mL to 100% at 30 to 35 mL. It was well tolerated by patients without respiratory disease.22 In cohorts it followed in 59.5% of 42 patients after 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375%,42 and in 10 of 16 in an emergency department.43
  • Injecting outside the fascia did not help. It did not reduce paresis and doubled the time to readiness for surgery, from 18 to 37 minutes.27
  • Pneumothorax. Clinically symptomatic in 0.06% of 6366 ultrasound-guided blocks around the clavicle; three of the four cases presented after two days.31 Another estimate is 0.4 per 1000.33
  • Horner syndrome followed 37.5% of blocks in one trial, against 0 to 5% after infraclavicular and axillary blocks,15 and 10% in a surgical series.44
  • In the 510-case series: symptomatic hemidiaphragmatic paresis 1%, Horner syndrome 1%, vascular puncture 0.4%, transient sensory deficit 0.4%.3
  • Pooled across 25 trials, complications were infrequent and did not differ between supraclavicular, infraclavicular and axillary blocks.40 A second meta-analysis found more paraesthesia, phrenic palsy and Horner syndrome with the supraclavicular block.17

Pearls of wisdom

  • Name the rib and the pleura before anything else. The rib has a clean black shadow; the pleura slides.
  • Colour Doppler first. A vessel in the cluster changes the plan.34
  • If the tip disappears, stop. Do not advance a needle you cannot see in this fossa.33
  • For hand surgery, make sure the local anaesthetic reaches the lower trunk: the ulnar side is the usual failure.14
  • Warn the patient about a droopy eyelid, and assume the diaphragm may be affected whatever the volume.1522
  • Tell the patient to report chest pain or breathlessness over the next days, not just hours.31

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Can the diaphragm be spared

The trials disagree. One found no paresis at all with 20 mL placed under ultrasound;20 later studies found it in a third to two thirds of patients, and at every volume tested.212242 Plan as if it will happen.

Where to put the local anaesthetic

The corner pocket is simple but can miss the lower trunk.14 Intracluster injection is faster but may be intraneural.89 The intertruncal approach is the newest answer and has not yet matched the classical approach in a non-inferiority trial.1013

Supraclavicular or infraclavicular

The two were equal for success in earlier reviews.1917 A 2024 meta-analysis of 18 trials favours the infraclavicular block, and notes wide variation in how trials defined their outcomes.18

Its place in shoulder surgery

Small-volume and two-site techniques are being tested as alternatives to interscalene block, with conflicting results so far.3839
