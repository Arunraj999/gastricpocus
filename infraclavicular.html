Infraclavicular brachial plexus block: costoclavicular and lateral sagittal approaches

Local anaesthetic placed around the three cords of the brachial plexus below the clavicle. In the costoclavicular space, behind the middle of the clavicle, the cords lie close together on the lateral side of the axillary artery.12 Further out, in the lateral sagittal or paracoracoid view, they surround the artery beneath pectoralis minor.34 Both give reliable anaesthesia from the elbow to the hand, and both spare the diaphragm far more often than a supraclavicular block.567

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The cords are deeper than the trunks above the clavicle, and the needle has to pass the pectoral muscles and come close to vessels. In the lateral sagittal view the needle runs steeply: in 23 patients the median angle to the chest wall was 50 degrees, and the needle was rated difficult to see in 39%.15 The costoclavicular view is shallower and the cords are grouped together, which is the reason it was proposed.162 In a randomised comparison of the medial and lateral ways into the costoclavicular space, the block took longer and was rated harder as body mass index and the depth of the cords increased, more so with the medial approach.17

Anatomy

Below the clavicle the divisions of the plexus have joined to form the lateral, posterior and medial cords. They travel with the axillary artery towards the axilla. Two places on that path are used for blocks.

The costoclavicular space

Karmakar and colleagues described the costoclavicular space as the site for an infraclavicular block in 2015.16 The space lies deep and posterior to the midpoint of the clavicle.12 On ultrasound the plexus lies lateral to the artery, between subclavius and pectoralis minor above and serratus anterior below.18 In eight fresh cadavers the cords lay lateral and parallel to the axillary artery, in the same arrangement every time.1

  • Lateral cord: the most superficial, in front of both the medial and posterior cords.1
  • Medial cord: directly behind the lateral cord, and medial to the posterior cord, so it is the cord closest to the artery.1
  • Posterior cord: the most lateral of the three, immediately lateral to the medial cord and posterolateral to the lateral cord.1

On ultrasound in 30 patients the space appeared as a well-defined gap between muscles, with the cords clustered together lateral to the artery.2 The pattern is not always three neat cords. In a retrospective review of scans the median number of neural structures seen in the space was 4.5, with a range of 3 to 8, and several arrangements were described.18

The cords sit inside a paraneural sheath. A septum inside it separates the lateral cord from the medial and posterior cords. Before injection the septum was seen in only 7.1% of 98 patients on high-definition ultrasound, but after injection it was visible in 75.5%.19 In another series of 120 patients the septum was displaced by the first injection and stopped local anaesthetic passing between the two compartments in 94%; piercing it brought a motor response from the medial or posterior cord.20 This is the anatomical case for a second injection.

The lateral infraclavicular fossa

Further lateral, medial to the coracoid process, the cords lie around the artery beneath pectoralis major and pectoralis minor. In the usual arrangement the lateral cord lies on the cranial side of the artery, the posterior cord behind it, and the medial cord behind and between the artery and the vein. The arrangement varies between patients. In magnetic resonance images of 20 volunteers, a simulated needle in the sagittal plane reached at least one cord in 19 of 20 within a depth of 6.5 cm.3

The costoclavicular space is crossed by vessels. In a cadaver study of the needle path the cephalic vein and the thoracoacromial artery were consistently close to the needle, though none was punctured.21 The axillary vein lies medial to the artery in the costoclavicular view.22

Surface anatomy

Costoclavicular approach. The probe is placed in the medial infraclavicular fossa, just below the middle of the clavicle and parallel to it.222 Tilting it towards the head brings the space beneath the bone into view. The needle is usually brought in plane from the lateral end of the probe,9 although a medial-to-lateral route has been described.2317

Lateral sagittal approach. The arm lies at the side.3 The probe sits in a sagittal plane immediately medial to the coracoid process, below the clavicle.22 The landmark version used a puncture site at the point where the coracoid process meets the clavicle, with the needle kept strictly in the sagittal plane.3

Indications

  • Surgery of the elbow, forearm and hand. In the first ultrasound series of the costoclavicular block, 29 of 30 patients (97%) had surgery under the block alone.2 Against the paracoracoid approach in 90 patients, surgical anaesthesia was no different.8
  • When the diaphragm matters. Paralysis of the hemidiaphragm is much less common than after supraclavicular block.56712
  • Arthroscopic shoulder surgery, as a diaphragm-sparing alternative to interscalene block. Pain scores were equivalent in one trial, with no hemidiaphragmatic paralysis after the costoclavicular block.24 Another trial found less paralysis and no difference in opioid use.25 Analgesia is not always complete, and larger volumes did not fix that.13
  • Postoperative analgesia by catheter. Catheters have been placed by both approaches.2627

Nerves blocked

The three cords and every branch that leaves them: the musculocutaneous, median, ulnar, radial and axillary nerves, and the medial cutaneous nerves of the arm and forearm. Compared with a single-injection axillary block, the infraclavicular block was better at blocking the musculocutaneous nerve.29

The suprascapular nerve leaves the upper trunk above the clavicle, so it is not a branch of the cords. Injectate placed in the costoclavicular space can still reach it: in five cadaver injections of 20 mL the dye spread up to the trunks and stained the suprascapular nerve every time, but not the phrenic nerve.30 In patients having shoulder surgery, complete motor block of the suprascapular nerve at 30 minutes was seen in 87% after costoclavicular block and 91% after interscalene block.14 The skin over the top of the shoulder belongs to the supraclavicular nerves of the cervical plexus; in one shoulder trial every patient also had an intermediate cervical plexus block.24 The inner upper arm is supplied by the intercostobrachial nerve, which is not part of the plexus.

Landmark technique

The lateral sagittal block was developed as a nerve stimulator technique from magnetic resonance studies. The needle entered where the coracoid process meets the clavicle and was advanced in the sagittal plane, angled 0 to 30 degrees posteriorly. In 20 volunteers a cord was within 6.5 cm, and the shortest distance to the pleura in that plane was 7.5 cm.3 In a randomised trial of 80 patients, the stimulator technique and ultrasound gave similar results: success 85% and 95% (not significant), and the same 18.1 minutes until readiness for surgery.4 The costoclavicular approach has been described with ultrasound only, and this page describes both approaches with ultrasound.

Costoclavicular approach

In this picture lateral is on the left, so the needle comes in from the side of the cords.

  1. Place the probe below the middle of the clavicle, parallel to it.222 Tilt it towards the head to look beneath the bone.
  2. Find the axillary artery in short axis, with the vein on its medial side and the cords grouped on its lateral side, deep to subclavius.22218
  3. Run colour Doppler over the needle path. The cephalic vein and the thoracoacromial artery lie close to it.21
  4. Bring the needle in plane from lateral to medial.98 In a cadaver study it passed through deltoid in six of eight paths and sometimes through pectoralis minor or the subclavius tendon.21
  5. Place the tip in the middle of the three cords and inject.98
  6. Consider a second injection between the medial cord and the artery, or into the deep compartment below the septum.3120

Lateral sagittal approach

In this picture cranial is on the left, so the needle comes in from the side of the clavicle and runs towards the feet.

  1. Place the probe in a sagittal plane just medial to the coracoid process, below the clavicle.22
  2. Find the axillary artery in short axis beneath pectoralis major and pectoralis minor, with the cords around it.3 The vein lies on its caudal side.
  3. Enter just below the clavicle, medial to the coracoid process, and keep the needle in the sagittal plane, in plane with the probe, from its cranial end.3 The angle is steep, a median of 50 degrees to the chest wall, so expect a poor view of the needle.15
  4. Aim behind the artery, on its cranial side.410
  5. Inject and watch the spread around the back of the artery. A single injection here worked as well as three separate injections around each cord.32

RCT

One injection or two in the costoclavicular space

With 35 mL in 90 patients, a second injection between the medial cord and the artery shortened onset from 23.4 to 16.6 minutes and the total anaesthesia time from 28.9 to 22.5 minutes, with no difference in success and one more needle pass.31 A triple injection, one at each cord, blocked all four nerves at 30 minutes in 85.3% against 52.9% with the same 25 mL.33 A single injection placed in the corner between the artery and the medial cord had the same onset as a double injection (22.1 against 22.4 minutes).34

Dose-finding

How much to inject in the costoclavicular space

Lidocaine 1.5% with adrenaline, single injection: 34 mL for 90% of patients (95% CI 33.4 to 34.4).9 Ropivacaine 0.5%: 20.9 mL for 90% (95% CI 20.7 to 21.8), with a mean of 31.4 minutes to readiness for surgery.35 In a second ropivacaine 0.5% study, 13.5 mL for 50% and a calculated 18.9 mL for 95%, with a wide interval (17.9 to 27.5 mL).11

Dose-finding

How much to inject in the lateral sagittal view

Ropivacaine 0.75%: 19 mL for 50% and an estimated 31 mL for 95%, with a wide interval (18 to 45 mL).36 For a single injection behind the artery with lidocaine 1.5% and adrenaline, 35 mL for 90% (95% CI 30 to 37.5).10

RCT

From the lateral or the medial side

The medial-to-lateral route into the costoclavicular space was proposed to avoid the coracoid process and a needle aimed towards the vessels and pleura.23 In a trial it took longer to perform (11.9 against 9.4 minutes) and was rated harder,17 and in a three-arm trial it had the slowest motor onset and a vascular puncture rate of 28%.22

Cadaver study

The lateral cord is in the way

In eight cadaver needle paths to the costoclavicular space the needle touched the lateral cord in six and pierced it in three. It touched the posterior cord twice and the medial cord once. It also came close to the medial cutaneous nerves and the pectoral nerves.21

Observational

Crossing the septum

When the needle was pushed through the septum below the lateral cord, the stimulator produced a response from the medial or posterior cord.20 The lateral cord answers with elbow flexion, the posterior cord with wrist and finger extension, and the medial cord with finger flexion (standard descriptions).

RCT

Stimulator or ultrasound for the lateral sagittal block

In 80 patients the two methods took the same time to perform (4.3 and 4.1 minutes) and had the same onset. Success was 85% with the stimulator and 95% with ultrasound, a difference that did not reach significance.4

Imaging study

How deep in the sagittal plane

In volunteers a cord was reached within 6.5 cm, and the pleura was never closer than 7.5 cm in that plane.3 This margin does not remove the risk: a pneumothorax has been reported after an ultrasound-guided lateral sagittal block.37

Continuous techniques

Catheters have been placed by both approaches. In 80 patients having upper limb orthopaedic surgery, patient-controlled boluses through a costoclavicular catheter used a mean of 83.35 mL of bupivacaine 0.125% in 24 hours, against 121.40 mL through a lateral sagittal catheter. Pain scores were the same, and satisfaction was higher with the costoclavicular catheter.26

A larger and more recent trial did not find the costoclavicular catheter better. In 139 outpatients having hand and wrist surgery, with bupivacaine 0.15% at 5 mL per hour for 48 hours, sensory block in all four territories at 48 hours was present in 42.6% with the costoclavicular catheter and 47.8% with the paracoracoid catheter. Pain, opioid use, sleep and satisfaction did not differ.27 The authors suggest choosing by operator preference.

The septum between the lateral cord and the deeper cords may matter for catheters as well; its authors suggested two catheters, one in each compartment, for continuous block in the costoclavicular space.20 This has not been tested in a trial.

Contraindications

  • A needle tip that cannot be kept in view. The pleura lies beneath the target in both views, and pneumothorax has followed ultrasound-guided blocks around the clavicle.3937
  • A vessel in the needle path that cannot be avoided.21
  • A patient who cannot afford any loss of diaphragm function. The risk is low but not zero.57

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is uncommon after blocks below the clavicle, and much less common than after supraclavicular block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different drugs, volumes, definitions and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Pooled evidence. Across six randomised trials and 414 patients, hemidiaphragmatic paralysis was less frequent after costoclavicular block than after other brachial plexus blocks (relative risk 0.21, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.36), with similar onset and performance times.12
  • Higher volumes. In the retrospective cohort the injected volume was associated with paralysis.40 In a shoulder trial, 40 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% did not cause more paralysis than 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.75%.13 In cadavers, 20 mL in the space spread up to the trunks without staining the phrenic nerve.30
  • Shoulder surgery. After interscalene block the hemidiaphragm was paralysed in 100% of patients and after costoclavicular block in none.24 In a trial of 212 patients the figures were 92.45% and 7.55%.14
  • Pneumothorax. Clinically symptomatic in 0.06% of 6366 ultrasound-guided infraclavicular and supraclavicular blocks; three of the four cases presented after two days.39 A case has been reported after an ultrasound-guided lateral sagittal block.37
  • Vascular puncture. The cephalic vein and thoracoacromial artery lie close to the costoclavicular needle path.21 Vascular puncture occurred in 28% of medial-approach costoclavicular blocks in one trial.22
  • Nerve contact. In cadavers the needle pierced the lateral cord in three of eight paths.21
  • Horner syndrome followed 0 to 5% of infraclavicular and axillary blocks, against 37.5% of supraclavicular blocks, in a three-way trial.41 Costoclavicular block caused no Horner syndrome in a shoulder trial, against 18.87% after interscalene block.14

Pearls of wisdom

  • In the costoclavicular space, name the three cords before you needle. The lateral cord is in front, the medial cord behind it beside the artery, the posterior cord lateral.1
  • Expect more than three neural shapes. Divisions and variant patterns are common in this space.18
  • Colour Doppler first: the cephalic vein and thoracoacromial artery are close to the path.21
  • If the onset is slow or one territory is missing, think of the septum. A second injection deep to it is quicker.3120
  • For the lateral sagittal block, inject behind the artery on its cranial side and watch the spread wrap round the back.410
  • For shoulder surgery, the block spares the diaphragm but may not cover the shoulder. Look for spread above the clavicle.13
  • Tell the patient to report chest pain or breathlessness over the next days, not just hours.39

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Is the costoclavicular approach better

Small trials favour a faster onset.2822 Larger trials and pooled analyses mostly do not, and one review calculated that the trials so far are far too small to settle it.84243 The newest meta-analysis found a small advantage in onset only.44

One injection or two

The septum explains why a single injection can be slow,2019 and two or three injections were faster or more complete.3133 A single injection placed beside the medial cord matched a double injection.34

Its place in shoulder surgery

The diaphragm is spared,2425 but analgesia depends on spread above the clavicle, which a larger volume did not guarantee.13

Catheters

One trial favoured costoclavicular catheters for local anaesthetic use;26 a larger one found no advantage at 48 hours.27
