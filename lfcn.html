Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed around the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve just below the anterior superior iliac spine, where the nerve runs in a thin layer of fat between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae. The nerve is purely sensory, so the block numbs the lateral thigh and leaves the leg strong.12 Its course out of the pelvis and its skin territory both vary widely. The block reliably numbs the middle of the lateral thigh, but it covers the incisions used for hip surgery much less reliably.324

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The nerve is superficial, has no motor fibres and lies away from the femoral vessels at the usual level of the block. It is also small and often divided. In 120 volunteers scanned with an 18 MHz probe, every one of the 240 nerves was found, in a mean of 7 seconds a side, by starting in the gap between tensor fasciae latae and sartorius.10 In volunteers, performance times were 162 to 231 seconds with either ultrasound or nerve stimulation.11 The hard part is not the needle but knowing what the block will and will not cover.24

The 2024 ASRA–ESRA consensus on limb block names recommends naming a block by the nerve and the place where it is blocked.12

Anatomy

Origin and course. The nerve usually arises from the L2 and L3 spinal nerves, crosses iliacus towards the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and passes beneath the inguinal ligament into the thigh.8 It is a purely sensory branch of the lumbar plexus.1

Where it leaves the pelvis varies. In 52 dissected specimens the nerve left the pelvis in five ways: behind the ASIS across the iliac crest (4%); in front of the ASIS within the inguinal ligament, superficial to sartorius (27%); medial to the ASIS, inside the tendinous origin of sartorius (23%); medial to sartorius, deep to the inguinal ligament (26%); and most medially, in loose tissue over iliopsoas (20%). The first three courses were judged the most vulnerable to mechanical injury.3 In 200 cadavers an abnormal course was found in about one in four.13 A meta-analysis of 24 studies and 1720 nerves found the commonest pattern to be a single nerve leaving the pelvis medial to sartorius, on average 1.90 cm medial to the ASIS.14

On ultrasound. In 240 nerves in volunteers, the mean distance from the ASIS was 15.6 mm and the cross-sectional area 1.04 mm²; just after the inguinal ligament the nerve had one to four branches.10 In a smaller study the mean distance from the anterior iliac spine was 2.9 cm on the right and 2.8 cm on the left.9

Branching in the thigh. In 28 dissected hemipelves the nerve followed three patterns: a dominant anterior branch along the lateral border of sartorius (36%), a strong posterior branch (32%) or a fan of equal branches (32%). In half the specimens it had already divided into two or more branches above the inguinal ligament. Of the branches, 62% entered the thigh medial to the ASIS, 27% over it and 11% lateral to it, always in the deep layer of the subcutaneous fat.15 In 44 limbs the proportions were 70.5%, 13.6% and 15.9%, and a bikini incision or a longitudinal extension of the direct anterior approach crossed the main branch in every specimen.16

Meralgia paraesthetica. In a meta-analysis of 18 studies and 1512 nerves, the nerve in limbs with meralgia paraesthetica lay closer to the ASIS, more often left the pelvis lateral to or over the ASIS, and had a larger cross-sectional area than in healthy limbs.17

Skin territory. The area numbed by an LFCN block varies widely between people and even between the two sides of one person; the commonest area is the middle of the lateral thigh.114 After a 10 mL injection, the numb area began on average 7.9 cm below the greater trochanter, which was rarely included.2

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the legs straight in a neutral position. Feel the ASIS. Place a linear probe parallel to the inguinal ligament, find the lateral edge of sartorius and slide about 3 cm down the thigh until the nerve branches appear in the fat between sartorius medially and tensor fasciae latae laterally.8 Starting in the gap between these two muscles is the quickest way to find the nerve.10 The needle usually enters in plane from the lateral end of the probe.185

Indications

  • Hip surgery through a lateral or posterior incision, usually added to a PENG block, which does not cover the skin.19520 The added benefit is not consistent across trials.2118
  • Hip fracture fixation in high-risk patients, with a femoral nerve block and sedation, or as part of a combined block with articular branches of the femoral and accessory obturator nerves.2223
  • Split-skin graft donor sites on the lateral thigh. All 16 blocks in one series gave full anaesthesia of the donor site.6
  • Diagnosis and treatment of meralgia paraesthetica. Response to an injection was the second commonest criterion, after the history, used to select patients for surgery.24 The condition is not rare: in Dutch general practice the incidence was 4.3 per 10,000 person-years, with links to carpal tunnel syndrome and pregnancy.25

Nerves blocked

The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and its branches. The nerve has no motor fibres.1 In volunteers, 8 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% caused no clinically significant change in quadriceps strength,4 and with 8 or 16 mL, quadriceps strength fell below 80% of baseline in 2 of 20 legs at each volume.8 The femoral nerve and vessels lie medially; locate them before the needle goes in.18

The skin over the greater trochanter and the upper buttock is often outside the block.2 By standard descriptions it is supplied by the lateral cutaneous branches of the subcostal and iliohypogastric nerves. The hip joint, the anterior thigh and the posterior thigh belong to other nerves.

What the block covers

The skin of the lateral thigh, with a territory that varies from person to person. Nothing else.

Landmark technique

The traditional block is a fan of local anaesthetic below and medial to the ASIS. Landmarks place the needle poorly. In 20 cadavers, 16 of 19 ultrasound-guided needles touched the nerve, against 1 of 19 placed by landmarks; in volunteers, 16 of 20 ultrasound marks lay on the nerve and none of 20 landmark marks did, the landmark marks lying a median 15.0 mm away.32 In a burns unit, landmark blocks for skin graft donor sites did not reduce opioid use over the first 5 days compared with a historical control group, and the authors suggested ultrasound guidance instead.33 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Use a high-frequency linear probe placed parallel to the inguinal ligament, just below and medial to the ASIS.188
  2. Find sartorius and tensor fasciae latae. The gap between them, with its fat-filled tunnel of fascia lata, is the starting landmark.102
  3. Look for one or more small oval structures in that fat. The nerve may already be divided into one to four branches.10
  4. Scan up to the inguinal ligament and the ASIS. If nothing is seen, the nerve may cross over or lateral to the ASIS, a course more common in meralgia paraesthetica.317
  5. Locate the femoral vessels and nerve medially before inserting the needle.18
  6. Insert the needle in plane from lateral to medial and confirm the tip position with 1 to 2 mL of saline.18
  7. Inject into the fat-filled tunnel, following the needle tip as it moves; in cadavers 10 mL given this way reached the proximal branches.2 If the nerve cannot be seen, an injection just under the inguinal ligament, 1 to 2 cm medial to the ASIS, is an alternative.34

RCT

Under the ligament or at the nerve

In 106 patients having knee surgery, 5 mL of mepivacaine 1% was given either immediately under the inguinal ligament, 1 to 2 cm medial to the ASIS, or around the nerve once found. Time to perform and onset were similar, but more patients lost pinprick sensation on the lateral thigh within 10 minutes with the injection under the ligament.34

RCT, volunteers

The fat-filled flat tunnel

In cadavers, 10 mL injected with dynamic needle-tip tracking within the tunnel between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae reached the proximal branches. In 20 volunteers this gave anaesthesia of the lateral thigh in 95%, against 0% with placebo, and anaesthesia of the proximal branches in 68%.2

RCT, volunteers

More volume, same incision cover

In 20 volunteers, 16 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% numbed a larger area than 8 mL but did not cover more of the posterior or lateral incision lines for hip arthroplasty.8

Cadaver and volunteers

Ultrasound against landmarks

Ultrasound-guided needles touched the nerve in 16 of 19 cadaver attempts, landmark needles in 1 of 19.32 With an 18 MHz probe the nerve was found in all 240 sides scanned.10

Needle path and nerve stimulator

The nerve is sensory, so a stimulator produces paraesthesia in the lateral thigh, not a twitch.

RCT, volunteers

Stimulator or ultrasound

In 21 volunteers, one thigh was blocked with nerve stimulation (seeking lateral thigh paraesthesia at 0.6 mA or less) and the other with ultrasound, using 5 mL of lidocaine 2%. Success (76.2 to 95.2%) and total anaesthesia-related time were similar, but ultrasound needed fewer needle passes (3.2 against 9.5).11

Volunteers

A very small volume

An ultrasound-guided injection of 0.3 mL blocked the nerve in all but one of 8 volunteers, and the block lasted a mean of 4.4 hours.9

RCT

Finding the nerve with the skin

In 50 patients with meralgia paraesthetica blocked by trainees, adding transcutaneous nerve mapping to ultrasound gave lower pain scores at every time point than ultrasound alone.35

Technique

Keep away from the femoral bundle

In a trial of 100 patients, the femoral nerve, vein and artery were located before needle insertion, the needle was passed in plane from lateral to medial, and no adverse events occurred.18

Safe dose. Calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic on lean body weight, and count every block and infiltration given to the patient in the same period, which matters most when this block is added to a PENG, femoral or fascia iliaca block. Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk of toxicity.36 If toxicity occurs, stop injecting, call for help, manage the airway with 100% oxygen, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline on local anaesthetic toxicity. The ASRA advisory summarises the evidence behind lipid emulsion.36

Continuous techniques

No trial of continuous LFCN catheters was found for this page. In burns, a catheter may carry an infection risk, and a single injection lasts on average only about 9 hours, which led one group to try ultrasound-guided cryoneurolysis of the nerve: analgesia lasted more than a week in three patients.37 In a randomised pilot study of 12 patients after skin grafting, cryoneurolysis added to a ropivacaine block gave lower pain scores and less opioid use than sham for the first week; only descriptive statistics were reported.29

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, or infection or a wound at the injection site.
  • Pre-existing numbness or pain in the lateral thigh is not a contraindication, but record it before the block. After direct anterior hip arthroplasty, LFCN injury was found in 31.9% of 122 hips.38
  • Surgery that needs more than skin anaesthesia. In knee arthroscopy an LFCN block in place of a femoral nerve block left 50% of patients with pain at the incision.30

These are standard clinical considerations unless a reference is given.

Side effects and complications

  • Incomplete block. The commonest problem. Failure was 15% in one volunteer trial,4 the proximal branches were anaesthetised in only 68% of volunteers in another,2 and 42% of patients with moderate to severe pain after hip arthroplasty did not respond (less than 15 mm reduction in pain).1
  • Quadriceps weakness. Not clinically significant in volunteers,4 although strength fell below 80% of baseline in 2 of 20 legs at both 8 and 16 mL.8
  • Adverse events in trials. None in 100 patients after hip arthroplasty,18 and the block was described as safe in 16 patients having skin grafts.6 In a meta-analysis of treatments for meralgia paraesthetica, complication rates were similar for injection, neurolysis and neurectomy, ranging from 0 to 5%.31
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, the incidence was 0.87 per 1000; ultrasound guidance was associated with a lower risk.39 These figures cover all peripheral nerve blocks, not this block alone.
  • Nerve injury. In a prospective audit of 7156 blocks, block-related nerve injury occurred at 0.4 per 1000, and most neurological symptoms after surgery were unrelated to the block.40 No case specific to the LFCN block was found for this page.
  • Numbness after hip surgery that is not from the block. Surgical injury to the nerve is common after the direct anterior approach: 36.4% with a bikini incision and 21.9% with a longitudinal incision in one trial.41 A prolonged numb lateral thigh after anterior hip surgery should not be blamed on the block without thought.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Start in the gap between tensor fasciae latae and sartorius, a few centimetres below the ASIS, then trace the nerve upwards.108
  • If the nerve will not show itself, inject just under the inguinal ligament 1 to 2 cm medial to the ASIS. In one trial this gave loss of pinprick within 10 minutes more often than injecting around the located nerve.34
  • Do not promise the greater trochanter or the posterior incision. The block rarely reaches the first and covers the second poorly.24
  • Doubling the volume makes the numb area bigger but did not cover more of the incision.8
  • In meralgia paraesthetica, look over and lateral to the ASIS: the nerve is more often there.173
  • Before a skin graft harvest, map the numb area and show it to the surgeon. Donor sites can stray outside the block.629
  • Test and record lateral thigh sensation before the block in anterior hip surgery, where surgical injury to the nerve is common.3841
  • Use ultrasound. Landmark needles rarely reach the nerve.3233

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

PENG with LFCN as a motor-sparing hip block

A 2025 meta-analysis of 12 trials found lower pain scores and less rescue analgesia with the pair,45 and a 2026 meta-analysis found less pain and better quadriceps strength than with fascia iliaca block.46 The trials are small and their results differ, with one finding no added benefit,21 and the most recent trial again found longer analgesia.20

Longer than local anaesthetic

Cryoneurolysis of the nerve gave analgesia lasting more than a week after skin grafting in a case series and a randomised pilot study.3729 A definitive trial has not yet been reported.

Meralgia paraesthetica: steroid or not

Two small randomised trials found no benefit from steroid: one compared local anaesthetic with and without steroid, the other a steroid and lidocaine injection with saline.743 In a third trial, an ultrasound-guided injection improved painDETECT and monofilament scores more than TENS or sham TENS, with no difference between groups in pain scores.50 Response to the block remains a key test before surgery.24

Better localisation

In a 2025 trial in which trainees performed the block for meralgia paraesthetica, adding transcutaneous nerve mapping to ultrasound gave lower pain scores.35 Whether this matters in experienced hands is unknown.
