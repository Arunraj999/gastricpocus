Retroclavicular block (RAPTIR)

The retroclavicular approach to the infraclavicular region. The needle enters behind the clavicle and reaches the cords of the brachial plexus almost parallel to the probe, so it is easier to see than in the classic infraclavicular block. The target and the result are the same.16

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

This is not one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.27 It is, though, quick to learn for someone who already does infraclavicular blocks. In the multicentre trial more than three quarters of the blocks were done by trainees who had done at least three before, and their success rate matched that of experienced clinicians: 92.9% against 92.0%.6

Anatomy

The retroclavicular approach is a way of doing an infraclavicular block, not a different block. The target is the same: the three cords of the brachial plexus where they lie around the axillary artery, beneath pectoralis major and pectoralis minor.16 In the usual arrangement the lateral cord lies on the cephalad side of the artery, the posterior cord behind it, and the medial cord between the artery and the vein. The arrangement varies between patients.

What changes is the route. Hebbard and Royse described a posterior route to the cords in 2007.2 In the retroclavicular modification the needle enters the supraclavicular fossa just behind the clavicle, in front of the edge of trapezius, and runs caudad beneath the bone to the back of the artery.8 The emergency medicine literature calls it RAPTIR: the retroclavicular approach to the infraclavicular region.3 It has also been called the posterior approach infraclavicular block.17

The blind spot

The first part of the needle path lies in the acoustic shadow of the clavicle and cannot be seen. In six cadaver dissections the suprascapular nerve was touching the needle there in four and lay within 2 cm of it in the other two; in one the suprascapular vein was indented. Trapezius was the only muscle the needle crossed.11 A second cadaver study of four infraclavicular approaches also found nerves and vessels in the blind spot.12

The authors of one clinical trial answer that the entry point they use, in front of trapezius and immediately behind the clavicle, is not the one studied in those cadavers and crosses only subclavius.8 The question is not settled.

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the head turned to the other side.8 The arm stays by the side in a comfortable position, which suits an injured limb.3 The probe sits just below the clavicle in a sagittal plane, medial to the coracoid process.8 The needle enters the supraclavicular fossa immediately behind the clavicle.68

Feel the supraclavicular fossa first. A fossa that is full, or that cannot be pressed in, makes the block harder,6 and the shape of the clavicle can make it impossible.17

Indications

  • Surgery of the elbow, forearm and hand. In the first clinical study, 40 mL of mepivacaine 1.5% gave surgical anaesthesia in 96% of 50 patients.1 Against supraclavicular block the success rate was the same, 98.3% in both groups of a 120-patient trial.7
  • When the usual infraclavicular needle is hard to see. Needle visibility was better than with the coracoid approach in three randomised trials.456
  • A limb that cannot be moved. The arm stays at the patient’s side. The block has been used in the emergency department for reduction of fractures and dislocations.32120
  • When the diaphragm matters and a supraclavicular block was the alternative. Phrenic block on ultrasound followed in 15% against 70%.8
  • Shoulder surgery is being explored and is not established.1022

Nerves blocked

The three cords, and through them the musculocutaneous, median, ulnar and radial nerves and the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm. The feasibility study defined success as a complete sensory block of the five nerves supplying the limb beyond the elbow at 30 minutes, and reached it in 90% of patients.1

The shoulder is less certain. The suprascapular and axillary nerves were blocked less consistently than after supraclavicular block.8 The skin of the inner upper arm is supplied by the intercostobrachial nerve, which is not part of the plexus.

What the block covers

The whole limb beyond the elbow, with a variable effect at the shoulder.

Landmark technique

There is no landmark version. The approach is defined by its ultrasound view, and the needle is followed in plane from the moment it clears the clavicle.16

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, artery, vein, nerve or bone. Tap a cord to see what it supplies. Switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, then use Test me. In this picture the head is to the right, so that the clavicle and its shadow are on the side the needle comes from.

  1. Scan below the clavicle. Probe in a sagittal plane, medial to the coracoid process. Find the axillary artery and the cords in short axis beneath the two pectoral muscles.8
  2. Decide whether it can be done. The trial protocol required a scan before the block to rule out a clavicle or fossa that would not allow it.6
  3. Enter immediately behind the clavicle, in front of trapezius, and walk the needle off the back of the bone.8
  4. Tilt the probe towards the clavicle to pick the needle up as early as possible and shorten the blind spot.8
  5. Stay strictly in plane, parallel to the probe, and never aim the needle backwards.6
  6. Stop behind the artery. Inject so that the artery lifts forwards.68

RCT

One injection or two

Two trials used a single injection behind the artery with no repositioning.67 A third injected at the lateral cord, then between the posterior cord and the artery.8

Dose-finding

How much to inject

For bupivacaine 0.5% the volume effective in half of patients was 9.6 mL, and the calculated volume for 95% was 23.2 mL (95% CI 18.8 to 36.7).9 Trials have used 25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%,8 30 mL of a mepivacaine and ropivacaine mixture,7 and 40 mL.16

Feasibility study

How deep

The axillary artery lay at a mean depth of 3.1 cm (SD 0.7).1 The authors of the multicentre trial found the block feasible in heavier patients as long as the supraclavicular fossa could be felt and pressed in, up to an artery depth of 5 cm in their experience.6

Secondary analysis

Obesity

In a trial of retroclavicular against supraclavicular block, the 16 obese patients took longer to scan and to needle, with no significant difference in success, complications or satisfaction.15

Needle path and nerve stimulator

Steer the needle yourself. It appears at the edge of the clavicle’s shadow, as it does in practice. The panel names what the tip is in. Switch on the nerve stimulator to see which muscles answer, then inject and watch where the fluid goes. Or press Play the block to watch the whole sequence first.

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

These thresholds were measured at the trunks. During ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block in 39 patients, the median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it. A twitch at 0.2 mA or less was never seen outside the trunk. A twitch between 0.2 and 0.5 mA did not rule out an intraneural tip, and thresholds were higher in patients with diabetes.24

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.25

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root (mean peak 8.2 psi) and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.26

RCT

Fewer passes, less paraesthesia

Against the coracoid approach in 100 patients: better visibility of tip and shaft, fewer needle passes, a shorter block time and less paraesthesia during the block, with no difference in success.4

Continuous techniques

Catheters placed by this route have been described in letters, including one for total elbow replacement.1819 Two cautions come from the evidence so far. In cadavers a catheter advanced 4 cm beyond the needle tip had entered the posterior cord in three of six dissections.11 And a haemothorax was diagnosed 24 hours after a continuous block in a critically ill patient with chest and arm injuries; computed tomography angiography showed no vascular damage.16

Contraindications

  • A clavicle or supraclavicular fossa that does not allow the needle path.617
  • Disordered coagulation calls for caution. Vessels behind the clavicle cannot be compressed.11
  • An operator who cannot keep the needle in plane. The safety of the approach rests on a needle that stays parallel to the probe and is never aimed backwards.6

Side effects and complications

Phrenic nerve block was much less frequent than after supraclavicular block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

  • In the first 50 patients: one vascular puncture, two transient paraesthesias, and no pneumothorax on the chest radiograph taken before discharge.1
  • Pooled across ten trials (806 patients): no significant difference in complications from other approaches.14 The trials were too small to detect rare events.8
  • Suprascapular nerve and vessels lie in or near the unseen part of the path.1112
  • Haemothorax has been reported once, with a catheter.16
  • With larger volumes for shoulder surgery, the estimated rate of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis was 0.01 with 25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and 0.09 with 35 mL.10

Pearls of wisdom

  • Scan first and feel the fossa. If the clavicle will not let the needle through, choose another approach.617
  • Keep the unseen part short: enter right against the back of the clavicle and tilt the probe towards it.8
  • Do not advance on faith. If the needle has not appeared where it should, stop.6
  • Aim behind the artery and watch it lift.68
  • It is the same block as the infraclavicular block, so expect the same result at the elbow, forearm and hand, not a shoulder block.18

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

How safe is the blind spot

Cadaver studies show nerves and vessels in the unseen part of the path.1112 Clinical trials have not shown more complications, but none was large enough to detect uncommon ones.148

Shoulder surgery

A phase I/II study in arthroscopic shoulder surgery found 25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% gave consistent block success with a very low rate of diaphragm paralysis, and called for confirmatory trials.10 A 2025 narrative review covers the same question.22

How strong is the pooled evidence

The 2026 meta-analysis found the approach neither better nor worse than other brachial plexus blocks for success, time or complications. The finding of better needle visibility rested on low-quality evidence.14

Devices in the way

The approach has been reported in a patient with an implanted pacemaker.23
