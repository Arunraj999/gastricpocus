Popliteal sciatic nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed around the sciatic nerve just above the knee, where it divides into the tibial and common peroneal nerves. It numbs most of the leg below the knee and the whole foot except a strip on the inner side, which belongs to the saphenous nerve. With ultrasound, success in a randomised trial rose from 60.6% to 89.2%.1 Most of the modern debate is about one thin layer of tissue: the paraneural sheath, and whether to inject inside it.23

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The nerve is large, lay a mean 1.6 to 1.7 cm deep in volunteers, and sits next to a landmark that is hard to miss, the popliteal artery.10 Under ultrasound it is a forgiving block: in one trial every block in both groups worked by 30 minutes.11 What lifts it above a basic block is the sheath. Placing the needle tip inside the paraneural sheath but outside the nerve itself, and recognising when the tip has gone one layer too far, takes practice. In trials of subparaneural injection, the nerve swelled on ultrasound in 2 of 100 patients,12 and unintended injection inside the epineurium was recorded in 4 patients (8%) in another.8 The 2024 ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus has standardised the names of limb blocks, and is the reference for terminology on this page.13

Anatomy

The sciatic nerve is two nerves bound together. The tibial and common peroneal components run as separate trunks inside one common sheath down the back of the thigh and separate in or above the popliteal fossa.14 The common peroneal nerve then follows the tendon of biceps femoris towards the head of the fibula; the tibial nerve runs straight down the middle of the fossa beside the popliteal vessels. In the fossa the nerves lie superficial to the popliteal vein and artery.

Where the nerve divides. In 28 cadaver legs the sciatic nerve divided a mean 60.5 mm above the popliteal crease, with a range from 0 to 115 mm.14 A meta-analysis of anatomical studies put the pooled mean distance from the popliteal fossa at 65.43 mm.15 On 289 thigh MRI scans the division lay a mean 7.5 cm above the lateral joint line (range 1.5 to 12.8 cm), and at 10 cm or less in 93%.16 The practical message is the same in all three: the level varies, so find it with the probe.

The paraneural sheath. Around the epineurium of the sciatic nerve sits a thin, separate layer. In 28 unembalmed cadaver legs it was a multilayered circular fascia, distinct from the nerve. Ten millilitres injected inside it spread 10 to 15 cm along the nerve, against 5 to 6 cm when injected outside, although the spread inside was not completely circumferential.2 High-definition ultrasound in patients showed the same sheath, distinct from the epineurium, wrapping the sciatic nerve and then the tibial and common peroneal nerves separately; it is easier to see once local anaesthetic has been injected.17 An earlier cadaver study had already described a common sheath around both divisions: dye injected into the sheath of the tibial nerve travelled 147 mm (15 mL) and 172 mm (30 mL) upwards and in most legs reached both nerves.18

Two spaces, two spreads. Local anaesthetic outside the sheath sits in the space between the sheath and the surrounding muscles; local anaesthetic inside it sits between the sheath and the epineurium.17 Injection inside the sheath gives a longer column of local anaesthetic along the nerve.219

The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve is not part of the sciatic nerve; it arises separately from the sacral plexus. In 83 cadaver limbs it ended in the popliteal region in only 9.7%; it continued to the upper or lower calf in 45.7% and 44.6%, and in one limb reached beyond the medial malleolus.20 It can explain numb toes with sensation still present over the back of the calf.

Surface anatomy

The popliteal crease is the reference line. The fossa above it is bounded by biceps femoris laterally and by semitendinosus and semimembranosus medially. The block can be done with the patient prone, in the lateral position with the leg to be blocked uppermost, or supine with the leg raised on a support so that the probe can reach the back of the knee. In 38 volunteers scanned both ways, the supine position gave a slightly shallower nerve (1.6 against 1.7 cm), found closer to the crease (5.9 against 6.6 cm) and faster (36 against 47 seconds); observers preferred supine images in 53.8% and prone in 22.5%.10 For the lateral approach in the supine patient, the needle enters the outer thigh in the groove between biceps femoris and vastus lateralis.

Indications

  • Foot and ankle surgery, as anaesthesia or as analgesia with general anaesthesia, combined with a saphenous block when the medial ankle or the incision is in saphenous territory.14
  • Ankle fracture fixation. In the AnAnkle trial of 150 patients, popliteal sciatic and saphenous blocks gave a better combined pain and opioid score than spinal anaesthesia over 27 hours, and 99% of patients would choose the same anaesthetic again.21
  • Prolonged analgesia at home through a popliteal catheter.2223
  • Below-knee surgery, combined with a femoral block, as studied in a 2026 trial of 80 patients.24

Nerves blocked

The tibial nerve and the common peroneal nerve, and their branches below the knee: the sural nerve, the medial and lateral plantar nerves, and the superficial and deep peroneal nerves. Both divisions must be blocked; a trial defined success as loss of sensation in the territory of both nerves within 30 minutes.1

The saphenous nerve, the end of the femoral nerve, is not touched and supplies the inner side of the leg and ankle. Its importance was shown in a placebo-controlled trial: after major ankle surgery under popliteal block, significant pain occurred in 8 of 9 patients without a saphenous block and 1 of 9 with one, and breakthrough pain in the saphenous area started within 30 minutes of the end of surgery.4 The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve arises from the sacral plexus separately from the sciatic nerve and is not blocked at this level.20

What the block covers

The leg below the knee and the foot, except the saphenous strip on the inner side and, in some patients, skin over the back of the calf.

Landmark technique

Before ultrasound the nerve was found with a stimulator, from behind with the patient prone or from the outer thigh with the patient supine. In 50 patients both approaches, each with 40 mL of mepivacaine 1.5%, gave acceptable anaesthesia, though the lateral approach took longer.28 On MRI, the needle angle needed to meet the nerve from the side averaged 30 degrees and ranged from 15 to 45 degrees in 95%, smaller in larger thighs.16 Because the division can be high, an entry point 10 cm above the crease was more likely to be above the division than the classical 5 to 7 cm.14 Volumes were large: with a stimulator, the ED95 of mepivacaine 1.5% at the popliteal level was 30 mL.29 Against a single stimulator-guided injection, ultrasound raised success from 60.6% to 89.2%.1 This page does not describe a landmark technique further.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Position the patient prone, lateral or supine with the leg supported. In volunteers, supine scanning found the nerve slightly faster and more superficially than prone,10 and a supine approach worked as well as the lateral position in a trial.24
  2. Place a linear probe across the popliteal crease and find the popliteal artery. The tibial nerve is the bright, round, honeycomb structure superficial to the vessels.
  3. Slide the probe upwards, following the tibial nerve, until the common peroneal nerve joins it from the lateral side. Note the level at which the two meet; in cadavers it ranged from the crease to 11.5 cm above it.14
  4. Tilt the probe towards the feet to make the nerve stand out against the surrounding fat and muscle.
  5. Enter in plane from the lateral side, through or under biceps femoris, aiming between the tibial and common peroneal nerves at the division.303
  6. Pass through the paraneural sheath and inject a small test volume. Spread inside the sheath separates the two nerves and runs along them; spread outside lifts the sheath and stays among the muscles.172
  7. Stop if the nerve itself swells. In trials the needle was withdrawn slightly and the injection completed.3012
  8. Add a saphenous block for surgery on the medial ankle or foot.4

RCT

Inside the sheath, at the division

In 50 patients, a single injection inside the common sheath between the two nerves succeeded in 84% against 56% for separate injections around each nerve below the division, and was quicker to perform and to work.30 In 89 patients, injection through the common sheath at the division shortened onset by 30% against separate injections outside the sheath, with no change in nerve size after injection.3

RCT

Subparaneural against circumferential

In a trial using 0.3 mL/kg of mepivacaine 1%, subparaneural injection had a shorter onset than extraneural circumferential spread (median 11 against 17 minutes) and longer sensory block (397 against 265 minutes).8 In the lateral approach, injection beneath the sheath left 5.57 against 1.48 mL of local anaesthetic in contact with the nerve, spread 9.3 against 5.6 cm along it, and gave complete sensory block in 90% against 63%.19

RCT

At or above the division

Once inside the sheath, the level mattered little. In 68 patients, injections at the division and on the common trunk above it gave success of 85 to 88% and onset of 15.0 to 17.7 minutes.5 In an earlier trial that did not target the sheath, separate injections 3 cm below the division worked 30% faster than a single injection 5 cm above it (sensory onset 21.4 against 31.4 minutes).31

RCT

One injection or three

With 15 mL inside the sheath, one injection between the nerves and three injections around them had the same success (92%) and total anaesthesia-related time. One injection needed one needle pass against three; three injections had a faster onset (12.5 against 15.8 minutes).12

Needle path and nerve stimulator

The needle passes through skin, fat and biceps femoris (lateral approach) or between the hamstring muscles (posterior view), towards the gap between the tibial and common peroneal nerves. The aim is to puncture the paraneural sheath without entering either nerve. A stimulator can be used as a safety monitor rather than as the means of finding the nerve.

Observational

Stimulation does not keep the tip out

In 42 stimulator-guided popliteal blocks with a motor response below 0.5 mA, ultrasound criteria for intraneural injection were met in 66%, without neurological complications.32 In 17 patients studied with CT and serial nerve conduction tests, 94% of injections were intraneural and no clinical or electrophysiological injury was found.33

RCT

Intraneural against subparaneural

In 88 patients given 15 mL of ropivacaine 1%, deliberate intraneural injection had a faster onset (median 10 against 25 minutes) and higher success (95.3% against 62.5%) than subparaneural injection. Both groups showed the same subclinical fall in nerve action potential amplitude at 5 weeks, and no clinical deficit at 6 months. The authors warned that the findings should not be extended to other approaches.9 This is not a technique to adopt.

Imaging study

What “intraneural” means here

On MRI in 22 patients, subparaneural injection doubled the cross-sectional area of the nerve (57.8 to 110.8 mm²) and met the usual ultrasound definition of intraneural injection in 21, yet true intrafascicular injection was suspected in only two.34 With a stimulator, ultrasound signs of injection inside the epineurium were seen in 73% and with ultrasound at the division in 100%.11

RCT

Pressure plus low-current stimulation

In 123 blocks, 27 of them popliteal, combining injection pressure monitoring with stimulation started at 1.5 mA reduced intraneural injection against fixed thresholds (odds ratio 17.1 for the control group).35

Volumes studied. Trials on this page used 15 mL inside the sheath,129 20 mL of mepivacaine 1.5%,11 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%,24 30 mL of mixed lidocaine and bupivacaine,130 0.3 mL/kg of mepivacaine 1%8 and, through a catheter, 40 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%.7 These are study protocols, not recommendations. The dose-finding studies show that much smaller volumes can work inside the sheath.366

Maximum safe dose. Most patients also receive a saphenous block, and sometimes a femoral block, so add the doses together. Calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic for each patient, based on lean body weight as recommended by ASRA guidance, and stay below it.37 In a registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, dose per weight and patient weight were among the predictors of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.38 Small patient size and sarcopenia increase the risk.39 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, support the airway and circulation, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion following Association of Anaesthetists or ASRA guidance.39

Continuous techniques

A popliteal catheter is a well-studied way to send patients home with a nerve block. In a placebo-controlled trial of 30 patients, ropivacaine 0.2% for 3 days at home reduced median pain at rest on day 1 from 4.0 to 0.0 and median opioid tablets from 8.0 to 0.0.22 A meta-analysis of 5 trials found lower pain scores at 24 and 48 hours than with a single injection; leakage was the commonest minor problem (26 of 187, 13.9%), and there were no neuropathies or infections.23 In a survey of 1001 continuous popliteal blocks, success was 97.5%, with no neuropathy or infection at 3 months.7

Where to put the catheter. Unlike single injections, catheters placed 5 cm above the division gave better analgesia than catheters at the division (median pain 2.0 against 3.0, 128 patients).40 A comparison of a catheter deep to the sheath just below the division with one superficial to it above the division found no difference in pain the morning after surgery (median 1.5 in both).41

How to run it. In 70 patients, automated boluses of 8 mL every 2 hours started 5 hours after discharge gave lower pain the next day (median 0.0 against 3.0) and made the reservoir last longer (119 against 74 hours) than a 6 mL/h infusion.42

Contraindications

  • Infection or wounds at the needle site and refusal by the patient.
  • Pre-existing sciatic or peroneal nerve deficit, which should be documented before the block. A recent trial excluded patients with sciatic nerve deficit or peripheral neuropathy.25
  • Surgery where early detection of compartment syndrome depends on pain, after discussion with the surgeon.
  • Anticoagulation: the popliteal fossa is compressible, but follow local and national guidance.

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Neurological symptoms after foot and ankle surgery are more common than registry figures for all blocks suggest, though most resolve. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The series used different definitions and follow-up, and neurological symptoms after foot and ankle surgery have several causes besides the block, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Nerve injury. Two retrospective series of more than 800 patients each found persistent symptoms in 0.7 to 0.8%.4344 Across 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks of all types, symptoms lasted more than 6 months after 0.9 per 1000.45 Trial cohorts with follow-up at 1 week reported residual numbness in 2 of 68, both resolved by 1 month,5 and none in 100.12
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks in an Australasian registry, lower with ultrasound;38 seizures in 0.08 per 1000 ultrasound-guided blocks.45 The ASRA advisory notes a trend towards delayed presentation.39 These figures cover all peripheral blocks.
  • Paraesthesia during the block occurred in 14 to 20% with subparaneural injection12 and 25 to 34% in another trial.5
  • Rebound pain. After popliteal block for ankle fracture, pain was lower than after general anaesthesia at 2 to 8 hours but higher at 24 hours.27 The AnAnkle trial still favoured the block overall, despite substantial rebound pain as it wore off.21
  • Catheter problems: leakage in 13.9%,23 two dislodgements in 30 patients,22 blood aspiration in 0.4% and site inflammation in 2 of 1001.7

Pearls of wisdom

  • Find the division before you choose the injection point. It can be anywhere from the crease to more than 10 cm above it.1416
  • Aim between the two nerves, inside the common sheath. One well-placed injection does the work of three.1230
  • A doughnut around the outside of the nerve is not the end point. Spread that separates the two nerves and runs along them is.819
  • If the nerve swells, stop and pull back. The subparaneural target is one layer away from the epineurium.3034
  • Always ask whether the saphenous nerve matters. After ankle surgery it usually does, within 30 minutes.4
  • Numb toes with pain behind the calf may be the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, not a failed block.20
  • Warn patients about rebound pain and start oral analgesia before the block wears off.2721
  • For a catheter, go higher: 5 cm above the division gave better analgesia than at it.40

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

How far inside is too far

Subparaneural injection is now the usual target. Deliberate intraneural injection was faster and more successful in one trial, with a subclinical change at 5 weeks in both groups,9 and MRI suggests that most “intraneural” spread on ultrasound is still outside the fascicles.34 The safety question is not settled, and the authors themselves warned against generalising.9

Blocking without turning the patient

A 2026 trial of 80 patients found a supine crosswise approach as effective as the classical lateral-position block and slightly quicker.24 Volunteer scanning also favoured the supine view.10

Making it last

Intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg extended the time to first pain after popliteal and saphenous blocks from 870 to 1400 minutes; adding dexmedetomidine gave no more.46 Intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg was non-inferior to 7.5 mg perineurally (26.2 against 27.9 hours, against 17.6 hours with placebo).49 A 2026 trial of liposomal bupivacaine for popliteal and saphenous blocks reported lower opioid use up to 72 hours than ropivacaine.50

Smarter catheters

Programmed boluses with a delayed start improved next-day analgesia and extended reservoir life by about 47 hours (median 119 against 74 hours).42 For hallux valgus repair, PROSPECT found no evidence to support a continuous popliteal block.26
