Axillary brachial plexus block

Local anaesthetic placed around the terminal nerves of the brachial plexus where they surround the axillary artery at the top of the arm, with a separate injection for the musculocutaneous nerve. The nerves are superficial and far from the pleura and the phrenic nerve. The price is a variable arrangement of nerves, a cluster of veins and more needle passes: 6.1 against 2.0 to 2.6 for the blocks above the clavicle and below it, with the same success.1

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The axillary brachial plexus block is one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for anaesthetists.8 The artery is easy to find, the nerves are shallow and nothing dangerous lies beneath them except vessels. Ultrasound raised the success rate over nerve stimulation alone, from 62.9% to 82.8%, in a randomised trial of 188 patients.2 What makes it harder than it looks is the anatomy: the four nerves are arranged in the most common pattern in fewer than two thirds of patients.9

Anatomy

At the junction of the axilla and the arm the cords of the brachial plexus have become terminal nerves. The median, ulnar and radial nerves lie around the axillary artery, with the axillary veins on the medial side. The musculocutaneous nerve usually leaves the group higher up and runs in or near the coracobrachialis muscle. Deep to the artery lies the conjoint tendon of latissimus dorsi and teres major.

The nerves move around the artery. In 153 patients the order around the artery was always the same (median, ulnar, radial, musculocutaneous), but the most frequent arrangement was seen in only 65%, and the four nerves could be told apart on a static image in 78%.9 In 69 volunteers, at the usual level of the block, the ulnar nerve lay posteromedial to the artery in 59%. The radial nerve had two common positions, posterolateral (38%) and anterolateral (20%), and so did the median nerve, anteromedial (30%) and posteromedial (26%). Further down the arm the nerves spread away from the artery: at the most distal level studied, 30 to 81% of them were no longer in the same image as the artery, and the authors advised blocking as proximally as possible.10 In 248 patients only 59% matched the positions given in anatomy textbooks.11

A usual pattern still exists. On a clock face around the artery in 131 patients, the median nerve was at 10 to 11 o'clock in 79.2%, the ulnar nerve at 1 to 2 o'clock in 79.3%, the radial nerve at 3 to 5 o'clock in 78.4% and the musculocutaneous nerve at 8 to 9 o'clock in 86.9%.7 In 60 blocks the ulnar nerve lay deep to the brachial vein in 81% and superficial to it in 19%.12 The median nerve sat close to the artery, a mean 2.1 mm away; the musculocutaneous nerve was further off, a mean 9.3 mm.13 Gentle probe pressure moves the nerves, especially those anterior to the artery.107

Veins and arteries. More than one axillary vein was seen in 87% of patients, and a double axillary artery in 9.2%.7 The veins collapse under probe pressure and can disappear from the image.

The musculocutaneous nerve. As it passes through coracobrachialis the nerve changes from oval to flat to triangular, moves away from the artery and becomes more lateral.14 It can also be found between coracobrachialis and biceps.15 In 387 blocks it lay outside coracobrachialis in 22%, close to the artery in 6%, and fused with the median nerve as a single structure in 16%.16

The conjoint tendon of latissimus dorsi and teres major is a landmark for this block.17 It is the bright line deep to the artery, and the radial nerve usually lies on it. Scanning over this tendon with a high-resolution probe, the intercostobrachial and medial brachial cutaneous nerves were found in all 21 volunteers. The intercostobrachial branches lay a mean 35 mm (men) and 27 mm (women) from the artery, in a constant relation to the muscle-tendon junction of latissimus dorsi.18

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the arm abducted to about 90 degrees and the elbow flexed. The probe sits across the long axis of the arm, at the lateral border of pectoralis major, where the pulsation of the axillary artery is felt.711 The needle enters in plane, usually from the lateral (biceps) end of the probe, and passes through or past coracobrachialis towards the artery.

Indications

  • Surgery of the elbow, forearm, wrist and hand. In a three-way randomised trial success was 95 to 97.5% for axillary, supraclavicular and infraclavicular blocks alike.1
  • When the diaphragm and pleura must be left alone. A 2024 meta-analysis concluded that the axillary block may be preferred when there are significant concerns about block-related side effects.20 The phrenic nerve and pleura are far from the axilla.
  • Surgery under an upper arm tourniquet, with separate blocks of the intercostobrachial and medial brachial cutaneous nerves.2122
  • Analgesia after elbow surgery by catheter.2324

Nerves blocked

The median, ulnar and radial nerves around the artery, and the musculocutaneous nerve with its own injection. The musculocutaneous nerve needs its own needle position because it usually lies away from the artery; a dose-finding study gave it a separate volume, 5.5 mL of lidocaine 1.5%.4 The medial cutaneous nerves of the arm and forearm run with the ulnar nerve and the veins. The intercostobrachial nerve comes from the second intercostal nerve, not the plexus, and needs a separate subcutaneous or ultrasound-guided injection if the inner arm matters.2118

The axillary and suprascapular nerves have already left the plexus at this level, so the shoulder is not covered.

What the block covers

The limb from the elbow down, with the inner upper arm only if the cutaneous nerves are added.

Landmark technique

Before ultrasound the axillary block was done by transfixing the artery or by finding each nerve with a stimulator. Without ultrasound, multiple injections beat a single injection: a Cochrane review of 22 trials and 2193 patients found fewer primary failures with multiple injections than with one (risk ratio 0.25) or two (risk ratio 0.27).25 After a transarterial axillary block, 23.9% of patients reported dysaesthesia at 2 days and 11.0% at 10 days, none permanent; the figures after an ultrasound-guided infraclavicular block were not significantly different.26 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

Tap a structure on the scan and it lights up in its own colour: muscle, artery, vein, nerve, tendon or bone. Tap a nerve to see what it supplies. Switch on Structures to avoid, wipe across to the anatomy beneath the image, open Variations to see the musculocutaneous nerve fused with the median nerve or a second artery, then use Test me.

  1. Find the axillary artery in short axis at the lateral border of pectoralis major.11
  2. Ease off the pressure. The veins reappear, and the nerves may shift.107 Run colour Doppler: there may be several veins and, occasionally, a second artery.7
  3. Name the bright line deep to the artery, the conjoint tendon.17 The radial nerve usually lies on it.
  4. Name each nerve. Trace them up and down the arm if unsure; positions vary from patient to patient.9
  5. Find the musculocutaneous nerve in coracobrachialis or between it and biceps. If it is not there, look beside the artery or for a combined median and musculocutaneous structure.1615
  6. Block the musculocutaneous nerve first, then either inject deep to the artery (perivascular) or around each nerve in turn (perineural).34

RCT

Perivascular or perineural

In 50 patients, perivascular injection (24 mL deep to the artery) and perineural injection (8 mL per nerve) gave the same success (92 to 96%) and total anaesthesia time. Perivascular took fewer needle passes (3.5 against 8.2), less time to perform (8.2 against 15.7 minutes) and caused less paraesthesia (8% against 52%). Perineural had a faster onset (13.8 against 18.9 minutes) and less vascular puncture (0 against 24%).3 Later trials agree on the main point: success was similar and the perivascular block quicker to perform.192728

Systematic review

How many injections

Across 25 ultrasound trials, success did not differ with the number of injections, and one injection caused less paraesthesia (risk ratio 0.6).29 Two injections against four: success 89.3% and 87.9%.6 Two, three or four perivascular injections: success 90 to 97.5%, with fewest needle passes for two.30 The advantage of multiple injections belongs to nerve stimulator techniques.25

Dose-finding

How much to inject

Lidocaine 2% with adrenaline: 1 mL per nerve worked in five consecutive patients, with blocks lasting 190 minutes (range 120 to 310).5 Lidocaine 1.5% with adrenaline, double injection: 5.5 mL for the musculocutaneous nerve and 23.5 mL deep to the artery for 90% of patients.4 Mepivacaine 1% in volunteers: a mean 4.0 mL gave complete blocks in 8 of 10, against 10 of 10 with 14.8 mL, and onset was slower (25.0 against 15.8 minutes).31

Observational

The musculocutaneous nerve

It was outside coracobrachialis in one block in five, and appeared fused with the median nerve in 16%. After injection, 61% of these fused structures separated into two nerves.16 When it lies close to the artery, its relation to the artery differs from when it lies inside the muscle.12

Observational

Contact without a twitch

This was measured during axillary block. With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5%, and paraesthesia in 38.2%.32

Observational

Two monitors at once

In 60 axillary blocks guided by ultrasound, with no injection when a twitch appeared below 0.3 mA, three intraneural injections were seen (one median, two radial) and no patient had a neuropathy afterwards.12

RCT

Veins in the way

Vascular puncture was more common when the needle went deep to the artery: 22.5% below the artery, 16.3% around it and 5% with perineural injections in 240 patients.27 Probe pressure hides the veins; release it before injecting and watch for the spread.7

Systematic review

Ultrasound and vessels

Against other methods of nerve location, ultrasound reduced vascular puncture (odds ratio 0.19, 387 participants).33 In a registry it was associated with less local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.34

Continuous techniques

In 405 consecutive continuous axillary catheters, three quarters placed for rehabilitation after major elbow surgery, catheters stayed in for a mean 55 hours. Complications occurred in 2.2%, including two patients with early signs of local anaesthetic toxicity. New neurological deficits were reported in four patients (1.0%), two of them unrelated to the anaesthetic.23

In 328 patients after elbow surgery, early failure was similar for axillary and infraclavicular catheters: 13 of 209 (6.2%) against 8 of 119 (6.7%).24 I have not found a randomised trial comparing ultrasound-guided axillary catheters with other sites.

Contraindications

  • An arm that cannot be abducted, or infection, wounds or enlarged nodes in the axilla.
  • Shoulder and upper arm surgery without additional blocks: the axillary nerve and the skin of the inner arm are outside the block.21

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Vascular puncture is the commonest complication in trials of this block. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different needles, volumes and definitions, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, the incidence was 0.87 per 1000; ultrasound was associated with a lower risk, and the site of injection was one of the predictors.34 In 12,668 ultrasound-guided blocks seizures occurred in 0.08 per 1000.35 In a trainee database, toxicity followed 6 of 5436 nerve stimulator blocks and none of over 9000 ultrasound-guided blocks.36 The ASRA advisory notes fewer intravascular injections with ultrasound and a trend towards delayed presentation.37 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not the axillary block alone.
  • Nerve injury. The first report of the Australasian Regional Anaesthesia Collaboration found block-related nerve injury in 3 patients, 0.4 per 1000 blocks, across all block types.38 In the ultrasound registry, neurological symptoms lasted more than 6 months after 0.9 per 1000 blocks.35 Most neurological symptoms after surgery were judged unrelated to the block.38
  • Paraesthesia during the block was more common with the perineural technique, 52% against 8%.3
  • Horner syndrome followed 0 to 5% of axillary and infraclavicular blocks, against 37.5% of supraclavicular blocks.1
  • Pooled across 25 trials, complications were few and did not differ between axillary, infraclavicular and supraclavicular blocks.39

Pearls of wisdom

  • Lift the probe pressure before every injection. The veins you cannot see are the ones you will enter.7
  • Expect the nerves to be in a different place from the textbook picture in a third or more of patients. Trace them, or stimulate.911
  • Always look for the musculocutaneous nerve. If it is not in coracobrachialis, check beside the artery and inside the median nerve.16
  • Scan high, close to the axilla. The nerves drift away from the artery down the arm.10
  • If a tourniquet goes on the upper arm, add the intercostobrachial and medial brachial cutaneous nerves.21
  • A perivascular injection is quicker; a perineural one has a faster onset. Choose by the list, not by habit.3

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Around the artery or around each nerve

Success is the same in most trials, so the choice is between speed and fewer passes (perivascular) and faster onset with fewer vessel punctures (perineural).32719 No meta-analysis focused on this question was found for this page.

How low can the volume go

Very small volumes have worked in small studies, at the cost of a slower onset.531 The 90% effective volume for a double injection is closer to 29 mL of lidocaine 1.5% in total.4

Tourniquet cover

Ultrasound-guided intercostobrachial and medial brachial cutaneous blocks were far more often complete than the conventional technique (88% against 19%), and tourniquet comfort followed (97.6% against 38.1%).21 A proximal intercostobrachial approach covered the axilla more often than a distal one.43 But in a small trial under dense supraclavicular block, adding an intercostobrachial block was not associated with less tourniquet pain.44

Making it last

Perineural dexamethasone 8 mg prolonged analgesia after axillary block by a mean 4 hours over intravenous dexamethasone (21.1 against 17.1 hours).45
