Airway POCUS

Ultrasound of the upper airway answers four questions at the bedside: where is the cricothyroid membrane, is the tube in the trachea, is laryngoscopy likely to be difficult, and what size of tube does this child need.

Front of neck

81% against 8%

With poorly defined neck landmarks, ultrasound located the cricothyroid membrane within 5 mm in 81% of attempts; palpation managed 8%.4

Tube position

98.7% sensitive

Across 17 studies and 1595 patients, transtracheal ultrasound confirmed tracheal intubation with 98.7% sensitivity and 97.1% specificity, in a mean of 13 seconds.7

Learning

About 14 scans

After a two-hour session, four of six trainees became competent at finding the cricothyroid membrane after a median of 14 scans.17

Basics

Probe and picture

  • Probe. A standard linear high-frequency probe (13 to 6 MHz) is enough for the front of the neck. A curved low-frequency probe (5 to 2 MHz) is used for the deeper submandibular views of the tongue and floor of the mouth.1
  • Position. Supine with the neck extended, the same position you would use for the airway itself.
  • Air stops the beam. A strong white line appears where the beam meets air. Everything deeper than that line is artefact.1

Technique 1

The cricothyroid membrane, transverse: TACA

Thyroid, Airline, Cricoid, Airline.

  1. T, thyroid. Probe transverse at the level of the thyroid cartilage: a bright triangle.
  2. A, airline. Slide towards the feet. The triangle gives way to a bright white line: the cricothyroid membrane.
  3. C, cricoid. Slide further. The cricoid appears as a black lying C with a white lining.
  4. A, airline. Slide back towards the head to the centre of the membrane.
  5. Mark the centre on the skin, across and along the neck.

Steps as described by You-Ten and colleagues.1

Faster

24 seconds

In obese women (BMI 39 to 44), 42 anaesthetists found the membrane in a mean of 24.0 s with the transverse technique and 37.6 s with the longitudinal. Each technique succeeded for 90%; every anaesthetist succeeded with at least one.3

Technique 2

The cricothyroid membrane, longitudinal: string of pearls

  1. Probe transverse just above the suprasternal notch. The trachea is a dark horseshoe with a bright line behind it.
  2. Slide the probe towards the patient’s right until its right edge lies over the midline of the trachea.
  3. Keep that edge on the midline and rotate the other end through 90° into the sagittal plane. The tracheal rings line up in front of the white line like a string of pearls.
  4. Slide towards the head. The cricoid is the larger, longer and more anterior pearl; the thyroid cartilage lies beyond it.
  5. Slide a needle between the probe and the skin. Its shadow should fall midway between the thyroid and the cricoid.
  6. Lift the probe. The needle marks the centre of the membrane; mark the skin.

Steps as described by You-Ten and colleagues.1 Learn both: the transverse view is quicker, the longitudinal view also shows the tracheal rings below, which matters when the membrane itself cannot be used.3

Why it matters

Marking the membrane before induction

Accuracy

Five times closer

Mean distance from the true midpoint on CT: 3.4 mm with ultrasound, 16.6 mm with palpation.4

Fewer injuries

25% against 74%

In cadavers with poorly defined necks, ultrasound marking cut laryngeal and tracheal injury from 74% to 25%. With difficult or impalpable landmarks, correct placement rose from 8.3% to 46.7%. Ultrasound took longer: 196 s against 111 s.5

Recommendation

Before, not during

The authors of the BJA review recommend identifying the membrane before induction in all patients: by palpation in most, by ultrasound when landmarks are unclear.2

Technique 3

Is the tube in the trachea?

  1. Linear probe transverse, just above the suprasternal notch, with light pressure.1
  2. Find the trachea: C-shaped rings with shadowing behind.1
  3. Watch during or just after intubation. Tracheal: a single air column, with a brief flurry of movement as the tube passes (the snowstorm sign). Oesophageal: a second bright structure with shadowing appears beside and behind the trachea: the double tract sign.1
  4. Pitfall: an oesophagus lying directly behind the trachea can hide in its shadow.1
  5. Then check both sides of the chest for lung sliding, which is an indirect sign that both lungs are being ventilated.22
Meta-analysis

98% and 98%

Eleven studies, 969 intubations: sensitivity 0.98 and specificity 0.98. In emergencies, 0.98 and 0.94.6

Ultrasound does not depend on pulmonary blood flow or ventilation, which is its advantage in cardiac arrest. The reviews position it as a preliminary test before capnography, or an adjunct when capnography is unavailable or unreliable. It does not replace capnography.67

Technique 4

Predicting the difficult airway

How to measure

  • Skin to epiglottis. Linear probe, transverse, through the thyrohyoid membrane between the hyoid and the thyroid cartilage. The epiglottis is a dark curved band with the bright pre-epiglottic space in front and the bright air line behind. Measure from skin to epiglottis.22
  • Skin to hyoid and skin to vocal cords. The same transverse sweep, at the hyoid (a bright inverted U with shadow) and at the level of the cords.22
  • Hyomental distance. Curved probe, midsagittal under the chin. Measure from the back of the mandibular symphysis to the front of the hyoid, with the head neutral and then extended. The ratio of the two is the hyomental distance ratio.22
  • Tongue thickness. The same submental midsagittal view: the greatest vertical thickness of the tongue.9
  • Keep the head position constant. The measurements change with head position, and published cut-offs were obtained in different positions.22
Meta-analysis, 2025

60 studies, 10 580 patients

Skin to vocal cords predicted difficult laryngoscopy with sensitivity 0.84 and specificity 0.81. Skin to epiglottis predicted difficult intubation with sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.86. Both rated high-certainty evidence.21

Strengths and weaknesses

What airway ultrasound does well, and where it fails

Advantages
  • Quick. About 24 s to find the membrane; about 13 s to confirm a tube.37
  • More accurate than fingers when the neck is difficult: obesity, labour, poorly defined landmarks.416
  • Independent of circulation and ventilation. Useful when capnography is unreliable.6
  • Learnable. Competence within about 20 scans after a short course.17
  • No radiation, at the bedside, with the same linear probe used for nerve blocks.
Disadvantages and pitfalls
  • Air hides everything behind it. Only the front wall of the airway is seen.1
  • Calcified cartilage casts shadows, and the vocal cords are seen in fewer than 40% of men aged 60 or over.1
  • A midline oesophagus can be missed behind the tracheal shadow; this position is reported in up to 16% of patients.122
  • Prediction indices are not standardised, and cut-offs differ between studies.8
  • It takes longer than palpation, and the machine must already be in the room. It is a tool for before induction; in a crisis its use depends on experience and immediate availability.35
  • No standardised training pathway exists yet.1

Also useful

Vocal cords, extubation and training

After extubation

Air column width

The width of the air column at the cords, with the cuff up and down, has been studied to predict post-extubation stridor. In 400 children a difference under 0.8 mm had a sensitivity of 93% and a specificity of 86%, better than the cuff leak test.22

Vocal cords

Seen through the thyroid cartilage

Probe transverse over the thyroid cartilage. For vocal cord palsy around thyroid surgery, pooled sensitivity was 78% before and 84% after surgery, with specificity of 98% and 96%.19

Tube confirmation

Two practice attempts

After a brief online tutorial, 91% of 66 emergency physicians read intubation clips correctly after one practice attempt and all after two.18
