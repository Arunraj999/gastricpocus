Adductor canal block

Local anaesthetic placed beside the femoral artery, deep to sartorius, in the canal that runs from the apex of the femoral triangle to the adductor hiatus. It targets the saphenous nerve and the sensory branches to the front and inside of the knee while largely leaving the quadriceps working. In volunteers it cut quadriceps strength by 8% from baseline, against 49% after a femoral nerve block.1 Where along the thigh the needle goes matters: the familiar mid-thigh injection lies, in most people, in the femoral triangle rather than the canal.2

Level of difficulty

Basic block

The femoral artery is an easy guide, the target is a few centimetres deep and there is no pleura or major nerve trunk in the path. In 20 patients the saphenous nerve was found beside the artery in every case, at a mean depth of 2.7 cm, and every block worked; the authors judged the technique simple and reliable.5 The skill lies in choosing the level. A scan that stops at the mid-thigh will often be in the femoral triangle, which behaves differently.2 Further down, below the canal, the saphenous nerve is small and loses its prominent arterial landmark, and it is harder to block reliably than the sciatic nerve in the popliteal fossa.10

Anatomy

Two spaces, one artery. The femoral triangle sits in the upper thigh. Its base is the inguinal ligament, its lateral side the medial border of sartorius and its medial side the medial border of adductor longus. Where those two muscle borders cross is the apex of the triangle, and this point is also the upper end of the adductor canal. The canal is a musculo-aponeurotic tunnel that runs from there to the adductor hiatus. Vastus medialis lies anterolateral to it, adductor longus and adductor magnus posteromedial, and the vastoadductor membrane forms its roof along its whole length. At the hiatus the femoral vessels pass backwards into the popliteal fossa.2

Names follow the place of injection. The ASRA–ESRA Delphi consensus of 2024 agreed a template that names each limb block by its target nerve and the anatomical place where the drug is given.11 On that logic an injection proximal to the apex of the femoral triangle is a femoral triangle block, and an injection between the apex and the adductor hiatus is an adductor canal block.2 The distinction is not pedantic. In 22 volunteers the mid-thigh point (halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the base of the patella), long used as the site for "adductor canal block", lay proximal to the start of the canal in every case, by a mean 4.6 cm (range 2.3 to 7.0 cm). The mean distance from the anterior superior iliac spine was 22.9 cm to the mid-thigh point and 27.4 cm to the top of the canal.2 Many trials labelled "adductor canal block" were therefore femoral triangle blocks, and their results need reading with that in mind.12

The nerves. In 20 cadaver limbs the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis were found every time, while branches of the anterior obturator nerve were present only inconsistently. The nerve to vastus medialis contributed substantially to the knee capsule through intramuscular, extramuscular and deep genicular branches; the saphenous nerve contributed more modestly, through its infrapatellar and posterior branches.13 The saphenous nerve runs in the canal itself. The nerve to vastus medialis is thought to travel in its own fascial sheath next to the canal, and the medial femoral cutaneous nerve joins the subsartorial plexus between sartorius and the vastoadductor membrane just distal to the apex.2

Level decides which nerves are reached. In four cadavers, 20 mL of dye in the distal femoral triangle stained both the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis in every thigh; the same volume in the distal adductor canal stained only the saphenous nerve.14 Lower down, 10 mL of dye in the distal canal reached the popliteal fossa and stained the popliteal plexus and the genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve in all 10 cadaver sides, whereas none of 3 femoral triangle injections spread to the popliteal fossa.15 The posterior capsule itself is supplied by branches of the posterior obturator and tibial nerves in every specimen, with contributions from the common fibular nerve in 53% and the sciatic nerve in 20%.16

Surface anatomy

The patient lies supine with the leg slightly abducted and turned outwards to open the medial thigh. The probe lies across the anteromedial thigh. In 20 foot and ankle patients the saphenous nerve was found in the canal a mean 12.7 cm above the knee joint.5 The mid-thigh point, halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the base of the patella, is a poor guide to the canal: it lay above the canal in all 22 volunteers studied.2 Start higher, follow the femoral artery down under sartorius, and mark the level where the medial borders of sartorius and adductor longus meet.

Indications

  • Analgesia after total knee arthroplasty. Against placebo, a catheter-based block reduced morphine use over the first 24 hours (40 against 56 mg) and patients completed the Timed Up and Go test faster at 24 hours (36 against 50 seconds).3 The ESRA PROSPECT recommendations for knee arthroplasty pair a single-shot adductor canal block with periarticular local infiltration, paracetamol, an anti-inflammatory drug and a single intraoperative dose of intravenous dexamethasone.4 The ICAROS group recommends peripheral nerve blocks for knee and hip arthroplasty unless contraindicated (strong recommendation, moderate evidence).17
  • Knee arthroscopy. In a meta-analysis of 10 trials, the block reduced resting pain after minor arthroscopic surgery, by 1.46 cm at 0 hours, and cut 24-hour oral morphine-equivalent use by 7.41 mg.18
  • Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, where a motor-sparing block is attractive for day surgery. One large trial found analgesia no worse than a femoral nerve block with much better quadriceps strength,19 but pooled trials showed no analgesic benefit over control after this operation.18
  • The saphenous territory in foot and ankle surgery, added to a popliteal sciatic block.520

Nerves blocked

The saphenous nerve is the constant target; it lies in the canal and supplies the skin of the medial leg down towards the medial malleolus, as well as infrapatellar and posterior branches to the knee.1310

The nerve to vastus medialis carries much of the sensory supply to the anteromedial knee capsule and is also a motor nerve.13 It is reached reliably from the distal femoral triangle but may be missed from the distal canal.14 The effect is volume-dependent: on electromyography, vastus medialis was affected in 35% of volunteers after 10 mL, 84% after 20 mL and 100% after 30 mL of lidocaine 1%, with no significant difference in quadriceps strength between the volumes.25

Branches of the obturator nerve. Anterior obturator branches are present in the canal only in some people.13 A distal canal injection can spread through the hiatus to the popliteal plexus and the genicular branch of the posterior obturator nerve.1526

The femoral nerve trunk, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and the sciatic nerve are outside the block. The skin over the front of the thigh and the lateral and posterior knee are therefore not covered.

What the block covers

The medial leg and much of the front and inside of the knee. The back and outer side of the knee need something else.

Landmark technique

Saphenous blocks by surface landmarks and nerve stimulation gave inconsistent results, which is why the ultrasound approach in the canal was developed.5 The best-known surface landmark, the mid-thigh point, lies above the canal in every volunteer studied, so a level chosen this way will usually produce a femoral triangle block.2 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Scan the anteromedial thigh in short axis with a linear probe. Find the femoral artery under sartorius, with vastus medialis lateral and adductor longus medial and deep.2
  2. Choose and name the level. Slide distally until the medial border of sartorius meets the medial border of adductor longus: this is the apex of the femoral triangle and the top of the canal. Above it you are doing a femoral triangle block; below it, an adductor canal block.211
  3. Identify the roof and the nerve. The vastoadductor membrane runs from vastus medialis to the adductors under sartorius.2 The saphenous nerve usually lies anteromedial to the artery.5 Colour Doppler confirms the artery and vein.
  4. Advance the needle in plane, usually from the lateral end of the probe, through or beneath sartorius towards the artery, aiming to place the tip beside it deep to sartorius.527
  5. Inject in small aliquots after negative aspiration and watch the fluid spread around the artery within the canal. Spread proximally into the femoral triangle is common.7
  6. Near the hiatus, the artery dives posteriorly. Injectate given here can reach the popliteal fossa.2615

RCT

Which level, for ligament reconstruction

In 108 patients having anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, a proximal injection (artery medial to sartorius) reduced 24-hour morphine use to 34.3 mg, against 64.0 and 65.7 mg at the mid and distal levels. Quadriceps strength fell by a similar amount at each level (16.7%, 13.4% and 15.3%), and nausea or vomiting was less common with the proximal injection (29.4% against 63.9% for distal).24 On the anatomical definition above, an artery still medial to sartorius lies in the femoral triangle, so the proximal level in this trial was probably a femoral triangle block.2

Meta-analysis

Femoral triangle or true canal

When 19 trials were reclassified by where the injection actually went, the femoral triangle group mobilised less well at 24 hours than the true adductor canal group.12 A second meta-analysis of six trials and 452 knee arthroplasty patients found no difference in rest or movement pain at 6, 12 and 24 hours.28

Cadaver

What the dye shows

Distal femoral triangle injection stained the saphenous nerve and nerve to vastus medialis in every thigh; distal canal injection stained only the saphenous nerve.14 Distal canal injection of 10 mL reached the popliteal plexus in 10 of 10 sides.15

RCT

Catheter level

A continuous proximal block was not inferior to a continuous distal one for opioid use in the first 24 hours after knee arthroplasty (difference −7.2 mg), with no difference in strength or walking.29 In another trial, 42% of patients with distal catheters needed intravenous morphine against 8% with proximal ones.30

Needle path and nerve stimulator

  1. Skin and subcutaneous tissue on the anteromedial thigh, then the fascia over sartorius.
  2. Through sartorius, or under its lateral edge, to the vastoadductor membrane.272
  3. Through the membrane into the canal, beside the femoral artery, where the saphenous nerve lies.5
  4. Stop short of the artery and vein. Inject and watch the spread around them.

RCT

Does a stimulator help?

In 80 foot and ankle patients, adding nerve stimulation to an ultrasound-guided subsartorial saphenous block did not significantly raise complete block at 30 minutes (55% against 45%). The saphenous nerve is purely sensory, so the sign sought is a tapping sensation down the medial leg; failure to elicit it within 5 minutes was associated with block failure.10

Case report

Not always motor-sparing

After 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% at the mid-thigh by a transsartorial approach, a 65-year-old day-case patient had no quadriceps power on discharge. The weakness lasted 20 hours. The authors blamed proximal spread or anatomical variation.27

Registry study

Ultrasound and toxicity

In an Australian and New Zealand registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, ultrasound guidance was associated with a lower risk of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.31

Volumes studied in the cited trials

  • 10 mL against 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.1% in volunteers: 2 subjects with each volume lost more than 25% of quadriceps strength.6
  • 5 to 20 mL of lidocaine 1%: proximal spread into the femoral triangle in 0 of 4 subjects at 5 mL, 58% at 10 mL, 50% at 15 mL and 50% at 20 mL, with no clear relation to volume.7
  • 15 mL of chloroprocaine 3% in volunteers.33
  • 15 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% with 2 mg perineural dexamethasone, in knee arthroplasty with an infiltration of the posterior capsule and periarticular injection.34
  • 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% with adrenaline, for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction.19
  • 30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% through a catheter, then 0.2% at 8 mL per hour for 24 hours, after knee arthroplasty.22
  • 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% for a subsartorial saphenous block in foot and ankle surgery.10

These are the volumes used in the studies, not a dosing recommendation. Whatever volume is chosen, the total dose of local anaesthetic must stay within the maximum safe dose for the drug, calculated on lean body weight. That matters here because this block is often given alongside a posterior capsule block and the surgeon's periarticular infiltration, all of which add to the total. In the Australasian registry, dose per kilogram, total dose and patient weight all predicted toxicity,31 and the ASRA advisory names small patient size and sarcopenia as added risks.35

Continuous techniques

The block began as a catheter technique: in the first report, a continuous block for 48 hours after knee arthroplasty gave low pain scores in eight patients, and in one patient 30 mL filled the canal down to its distal end on magnetic resonance imaging.36 Added to a single-shot femoral nerve block, a continuous infusion of ropivacaine 0.2% reduced 48-hour morphine use by 16.7 mg against a sham catheter, with better quadriceps strength and walking distance on the second day.37 Against continuous femoral nerve block in 100 patients, a canal catheter gave better walking and early functional recovery for the same pain scores and opioid use.38

Catheter or single shot? In 156 patients, a single injection, a 24-hour infusion and a 48-hour infusion gave similar opioid use and function; severe pain on the second day was reported by 21%, 14% and 12%.39 A meta-analysis of 11 trials (910 patients) found that catheters did not reduce opioid consumption, though they lowered pain scores at 48 hours slightly and improved quadriceps strength.40 A network meta-analysis of 27 trials found continuous blocks added to local infiltration to be statistically superior to infiltration alone, but questioned whether the gain was clinically meaningful.41 Most recently, in 94 patients who all had an infiltration of the posterior capsule, periarticular injection and multimodal analgesia, a 50-hour catheter infusion did not reduce opioid use between 24 and 48 hours compared with a single injection with perineural dexamethasone (30 against 38.8 mg); transient buckling, reported as catheter-associated, occurred in 7% of the catheter group and none of the single-injection group.34

Falls. In pooled trials of continuous femoral nerve catheters after hip and knee arthroplasty, 6 of 85 patients given ropivacaine fell, against none of 86 given saline.42 That evidence comes from femoral catheters, but it is a reason to assess leg strength before walking with any lower limb infusion.

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the injection site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Caution where early unaided walking is planned: quadriceps weakness can occur even with this block.27
  • Surgery outside the territory, such as the hip or the lateral and posterior knee, without other blocks.

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study.

Side effects and complications

Unwanted spread and motor effects are the main side effects. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

Motor weakness after a bolus and spread on imaging are not the same as a fall or a failed mobilisation. The studies used different definitions, so compare within a study and not across them.

  • Quadriceps weakness. Usually slight, but complete quadriceps paralysis lasting 20 hours has been reported after a mid-thigh injection.27 Pooled trials found better mobilisation than with femoral nerve block but no significant difference in falls.8
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. 0.87 per 1000 peripheral nerve blocks in the Australian and New Zealand registry, with ultrasound associated with lower risk.31 The ASRA advisory reports a trend towards delayed presentation, which may reflect ultrasound guidance, local infiltration techniques and continuous infusions.35 These figures cover all peripheral blocks, not this block alone. Keep the patient monitored and within reach of help after injection. If toxicity occurs, stop injecting, call for help, secure the airway, treat seizures promptly and give lipid emulsion early, following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline; the ASRA advisory sets out the same principles of treatment.35
  • Nerve injury. In more than 7000 peripheral nerve and plexus blocks, block-related nerve injury occurred at 0.4 per 1000; most neurological symptoms after surgery were unrelated to the block.43
  • Nausea and vomiting were more frequent with a distal than a proximal injection after ligament reconstruction (63.9% against 29.4%), reflecting greater opioid use.24
  • Sensory change in the sciatic territory. Injection at the adductor hiatus produced slightly reduced sensation in the sciatic distribution, without motor deficit.26 Bear this in mind before assessing the foot after knee surgery.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Name the level on the screen, not on the skin. The canal starts where the medial border of sartorius crosses the medial border of adductor longus.2
  • A mid-thigh injection is, in most people, a femoral triangle block; expect more analgesia and slightly more motor effect than a true canal block.212
  • "Motor-sparing" is relative. Check straight-leg raise and knee extension before the first walk.271
  • Pain itself weakens the quadriceps. In severe pain after knee arthroplasty, a good block can nearly double functional quadriceps strength.32
  • The block does not cover the back of the knee. Plan a posterior capsule block or accept posterior pain in some patients.916
  • More volume does not reliably mean more quadriceps weakness, but the vastus medialis effect rises with volume.625
  • For foot and ankle surgery, a saphenous block in the canal is not reliable enough on its own for surgical anaesthesia; test it before incision.20
  • With good multimodal analgesia, a catheter adds little for most patients; keep it for those expected to have difficult pain.3444

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Femoral triangle or adductor canal

The 2024 consensus names have made the distinction explicit.11 A 2025 meta-analysis of six trials (452 patients) found no difference in pain up to 24 hours; its abstract reports conflicting statements about which block was better at 48 hours, so treat that finding with caution.28 Trials comparing the two at clearly defined levels are still few.

Making a single shot last

In 90 patients aged 65 or over with combined canal and posterior capsule blocks, time to first rescue opioid was 4.5 hours with placebo, 13.9 hours with intravenous dexamethasone 4 mg and 18.5 hours with 4 mg given perineurally (2 mg per block), with no motor weakness or nerve injury.47 In another 2026 trial, a 50-hour catheter did not reduce opioid use between 24 and 48 hours compared with a single shot containing perineural dexamethasone.34 Perineural dexamethasone is an unlicensed use.

Going lower: the adductor hiatus

In 148 knee arthroplasty patients, programmed intermittent boluses at the adductor hiatus gave more knee flexion on days 1 to 3, a faster Timed Up and Go on days 1 and 2 and less posterior knee pain than continuous canal or hiatus infusions, with no difference in muscle strength.48 This is a single-centre trial.

Is the posterior capsule block worth adding?

A 2022 meta-analysis of 14 studies favoured adding it for pain, morphine use and length of stay.49 A 2026 meta-analysis of 19 trials (1702 patients) found no difference in pain or opioid use, though range of motion and walking distance improved.50 The network meta-analysis of 2023 also found no added analgesic value over infiltration for single-shot combinations.41
