1. Gastric ultrasound: how to scan

GASTRIC ULTRASOUND
  Right, let's talk about gastric ultrasound. It's a quick bedside scan, about two and a half minutes, and it answers one simple question.
  Is this stomach actually empty?

WHY SCAN
  We all know a fasting history isn't always reliable.
  In one study of five hundred and thirty-eight elective patients who'd all fasted, just over six per cent still had a full stomach on ultrasound.
  And in emergency cases, another study found a full stomach in more than half of them, fifty-six per cent.

SET UP
  For most adults, you'll want the curved, low-frequency probe, on an abdominal preset.
  In smaller children, under about forty kilos, the linear high-frequency probe usually gives you a nicer picture.
  Start with the patient lying flat, or sitting up a little.

PROBE POSITION
  Pop the probe in the epigastrium, just below the xiphisternum, in a sagittal plane.
  Keep it upright on the skin, with the marker pointing towards the head.
  Then sweep gently across from the left costal margin.

FIND THE ANTRUM
  Now, let's find the antrum.
  Right at the top of the screen, you'll see the lower edge of the left lobe of the liver.
  Tucked just underneath it is the antrum, which we're seeing in cross-section.
  Behind the antrum sits the pancreas.
  And deeper still, the aorta runs along in long axis, just in front of the spine.
  That's your standard view: the antrum, at the level of the aorta.

RIGHT LATERAL POSITION
  Next, roll the patient onto their right side.
  Gravity brings any fluid or food down into the antrum, so this is the view that really picks things up.
  So, don't call a stomach empty until you've had a look in this position.
  And if turning them isn't practical, sitting them up a bit is a reasonable alternative.

WHAT YOU SEE
  Empty first. The antrum is small and flat, with thick walls, and sometimes it looks a bit like a bull's eye.
  Clear fluid. Now it's round and thin-walled, and dark inside. Those little bright dots are swallowed air, which gives you the starry night look.
  Thicker fluids, like milk, look evenly bright.
  Solid food, soon after a meal, gives a frosted glass appearance, and it hides everything behind it.
  And a little later on, solid food looks more mixed and patchy, in a nice distended antrum.

GRADE CLEAR FLUID
  If what you're seeing is clear fluid, give it a grade.
  Grade zero: empty in both positions.
  Grade one: you only see fluid when they're on their right side. That fits with a small volume.
  Grade two: you can see fluid in both positions, which suggests it's probably one and a half mils per kilo, or more.

MEASURE THE VOLUME
  To measure the volume, freeze the image with the patient on their right side, between contractions, when the antrum is at its biggest.
  Trace right round the outer wall, the serosa, and take the average of three readings.
  In non-pregnant adults, the volume in mils is twenty-seven, plus fourteen point six times the area, minus one point two eight times the age.

FROM SCAN TO DECISION
  So, if it's empty, or there's clear fluid under one and a half mils per kilo, that's what you'd expect in a fasted patient.
  But clear fluid at or above that level, or any thick fluid or solid at all, and you treat it as a full stomach.
  The scan informs your plan. You still make the decision with the whole patient in mind.

LEARNING THE SCAN
  So, how many scans does it take to get good at this?
  In a study of anaesthetists, it took about thirty-three supervised scans to reach a ninety-five per cent success rate at telling empty from fluid from solid.
  So, get scanning, and start counting.
