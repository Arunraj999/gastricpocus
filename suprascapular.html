Suprascapular nerve block

Local anaesthetic placed beside the suprascapular nerve, either under omohyoid in the neck or on the floor of the supraspinous fossa.13 The nerve carries most of the sensory supply to the shoulder joint,6 and the block leaves the hand working.14 On its own it is a block for pain relief, not for surgery.8

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The suprascapular nerve block is not one of the basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.49 How hard it is depends on the approach. Under omohyoid the nerve is shallow and was identified in 81% of scans in volunteers, but it is small and lies beside the brachial plexus. In the supraspinous fossa the bony floor is easy to find, but the nerve itself, at a median depth of 35 mm, was identified in only 36%.1

Seeing the target is not the same as reaching it. In 27 patients whose needle had been placed for this block by ultrasound alone, the current then needed to produce a response was 0.8 mA or less in only five.39

Anatomy

The suprascapular nerve leaves the lateral side of the superior trunk. In 50 dissections its fibres came from C5 and C6 in 76%, from C4, C5 and C6 in 18%, and from C5 alone in 6%.5 It runs laterally and backwards across the posterior triangle beneath the inferior belly of omohyoid,1 reaches the upper border of the scapula and passes through the suprascapular notch, under the transverse scapular ligament, into the supraspinous fossa. There it lies on the bone beneath supraspinatus, supplies that muscle, then curves round the spinoglenoid notch to end in infraspinatus.

It carries most of the sensory supply to the shoulder joint.6 In 37 dissected shoulders it gave three sensory branches: two to the subacromial bursa, which also reach the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments, and one to the back of the joint capsule.9 The bursa, the acromioclavicular joint and the coracoclavicular ligaments had a second supply from the lateral pectoral nerve in every one of 25 shoulders in another series.7 The rest of the joint is supplied by the axillary, lateral pectoral and lower subscapular nerves.8

Two places to block it

Papers use several names for the same two sites. The first is the anterior, proximal, supraclavicular or subomohyoid approach.11316 The second is the posterior, distal or classical approach.212

Where the sensory branches leave matters for the posterior approach. A sensory branch was found in 87.1% of shoulders in one series, and in 48.2% of those it left the main nerve before the transverse scapular ligament.6 In another, the first sensory branch arose 2.5 cm from the notch, and the authors concluded that the injection has to reach the nerve proximal to the notch to include all three branches.11

The suprascapular artery travels with the nerve. In half of 12 dissected shoulders it passed through the notch under the ligament beside the nerve.10 The notch itself is reported to be absent in 8% of scapulae.3

Surface anatomy

Anterior approach. The probe lies in the posterior triangle above the clavicle, as for a superior trunk block. The trunk is found first and the nerve is followed laterally from it, under the inferior belly of omohyoid.13

Posterior approach. Feel the spine of the scapula, the acromion and the coracoid process. The probe lies over the supraspinous fossa, above the spine, in a coronal plane tilted slightly forwards.3

Indications

  • Analgesia after arthroscopic shoulder surgery. The PROSPECT recommendations for rotator cuff repair name interscalene block, or suprascapular nerve block with or without axillary nerve block.48 By the anterior approach it was non-inferior to interscalene block for pain in two large randomised trials, of 329 and 189 patients.1415
  • Patients in whom the diaphragm matters. Vital capacity was 90% of baseline after an anterior block against 67% after interscalene block.15 The posterior approach spares the diaphragm more reliably: with an axillary nerve block added, paralysis followed in 2% of patients against 40% after the anterior approach.18
  • Day surgery, when a working hand matters. Within the first 24 hours grip strength was unimpaired in 99% of patients after an anterior block and in 49% after interscalene block.14 In a second trial no patient had a numb hand, against 46% after interscalene block.19
  • Rescue analgesia in recovery. In patients with a pain score of 50 mm or more after arthroscopic shoulder surgery, 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% by the anterior approach cut the score by 50 mm at 30 minutes, against 27 mm with saline. Morphine use over the next six hours was 8.5 mg against 18.5 mg.21
  • Shoulder replacement, by catheter. After total shoulder arthroplasty a continuous suprascapular block preserved vital capacity better than a continuous interscalene block, with no significant difference in pain scores or opioid use.36
  • Chronic shoulder pain. The block has a long record in rheumatological, cancer and post-traumatic shoulder pain.2 In adhesive capsulitis the anterior and posterior approaches gave similar improvement at 12 weeks.38
  • Shoulder pain after thoracotomy. The evidence is mixed. One study of 36 patients found lower pain scores when the block was added to epidural analgesia;46 a trial of 135 patients found infiltration of the phrenic nerve by the surgeon more effective.47

Nerves blocked

One nerve: the suprascapular. Its motor fibres go to supraspinatus and infraspinatus, so a working block weakens the start of abduction and external rotation of the shoulder. Its sensory fibres go to the back of the joint capsule, the subacromial bursa, and the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments.9

By the anterior approach the block is often less selective than its name suggests. With 1 mL under omohyoid, 8 of 11 volunteers had a block of the suprascapular nerve alone.13 With 5 mL in cadavers, dye reached the superior trunk in 9 of 10 injections and the middle trunk in 8 of 10,16 and in a second study the C5 and C6 roots were stained in every specimen.17 A clinical volume placed here should be expected to reach part of the plexus as well.

Adding the axillary nerve: the shoulder block

The axillary nerve supplies much of the joint that the suprascapular nerve does not.8 Price described blocking the two together as the shoulder block in 2007.28 In a meta-analysis of nine trials the pair gave better pain relief than suprascapular block alone from 6 to 48 hours after surgery.34

For the axillary nerve, the technique tested in volunteers places the needle tip cranial to the posterior circumflex humeral artery, behind the humerus, in the space bordered by teres minor, deltoid, triceps and the shaft of the humerus. With 8 mL of lidocaine 2%, all 12 volunteers had a sensory block and 10 had a complete motor block. The nerve itself was hard to see; the artery and the muscles were the landmarks.29 In a small case series the same block was then used for pain after arthroscopy and after fixation of a proximal humeral fracture.50 The axillary nerve block will have its own page.

Landmark technique

Landmark and nerve stimulator techniques came first, and most use the posterior route.2 The classical technique walks the needle into the suprascapular notch, with a risk of pneumothorax and of injury to the nerve or vessels. Dangoisse and colleagues showed that the notch does not have to be found: the needle is introduced parallel to the blade of the scapula, away from the lung, and the solution is placed on the floor of the supraspinous fossa.40 Meier described a modified technique, also aimed at the supraspinous fossa, for single injections and catheters.41

In cadavers a landmark injection is accurate. An orthopaedic trainee placed 10 mL a median of 1.5 cm from the nerve and stained it proximal to its first sensory branch in all 15 shoulders,51 and in a direct comparison the needle tip ended as close to the nerve with landmarks as with ultrasound.11

The results in patients are more modest. A meta-analysis of ten trials (700 patients), all of landmark-guided posterior block against no block, found a saving of about 11 mg of oral morphine over 24 hours and judged the benefit not clinically important.27 A landmark block given by the surgeon matched an ultrasound-guided interscalene block for mean pain over 24 hours after rotator cuff repair, but with more pain and more morphine in the recovery room.35 For chronic shoulder pain, landmark and ultrasound-guided injections gave similar relief at four weeks in 50 patients.45

Posterior approach: supraspinous fossa

  1. Position. In the description followed here the patient lay prone with a pillow under the chest.3 Other studies have the patient sitting.39
  2. Place the probe. A linear probe over the supraspinous fossa, in a coronal plane tilted slightly forwards.3
  3. Name the layers. Trapezius, then supraspinatus, then the floor of the fossa as a bright concave line with shadow beneath it.3
  4. Find the fascia and the artery. The bright line just above the bone is the fascia of supraspinatus. Colour Doppler shows the suprascapular artery beneath it; the nerve lies beside the artery.3
  5. Bring the needle in from the medial end of the probe, in plane, until the tip is through the fascia and resting on the floor of the fossa.3
  6. Do not chase the notch. The picture that earlier reports took for the notch and its ligament was the floor of the fossa and the fascia of supraspinatus. The floor is the safer target: it does not depend on a notch that may be absent, and it carries less risk of pneumothorax.3

Anterior approach: under omohyoid

  1. Find the superior trunk, as for a superior trunk block.13
  2. Slide down the neck until a small dark circle leaves the lateral side of the trunk: the suprascapular nerve.13
  3. Follow it laterally under the inferior belly of omohyoid, away from the plexus.113 In one small series it lay a mean of 8 mm from the brachial plexus where it was blocked.52
  4. Place the needle in plane beside the nerve.13 The model below brings it from the lateral side so that the tip reaches the nerve before the plexus.
  5. Keep the volume small. The plexus and the phrenic nerve share this compartment.1617

Volunteer study

Which view shows the nerve

Under omohyoid the nerve was identified in 81% of scans, at a median depth of 8 mm. In the supraspinous fossa it was identified in 36%, at 35 mm. In cadavers, 95% of needles placed by the anterior approach were on target.1

RCT

How far the needle travels

In patients with adhesive capsulitis the needle went to a mean depth of 13.4 mm at 19.6 degrees for the anterior approach, and 30.6 mm at 38.7 degrees for the posterior.38

RCT

How much to inject

The trials against interscalene block used 10 mL of ropivacaine 1% or 15 mL of 0.5% by the anterior approach.1415 Smaller volumes have worked: 5 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% gave a longer time to first pain than the same dose at the interscalene level,20 and 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% worked as a rescue block.21

Cadaver studies

Volume and the phrenic nerve

Under omohyoid, 5 mL of dye reached the phrenic nerve in 20% of injections in one study and 41.7% in another.1617 With 10 mL the phrenic nerve was stained in 3 of 14 anterior injections and in none of 14 posterior ones.12

Observational

A reason to add the stimulator

In 27 patients the needle was placed for this block by ultrasound alone, and the current was then turned up until the patient felt it in the shoulder or the muscles twitched. It was 0.8 mA or less in five patients, 0.9 to 1.4 mA in 19, and 1.5 mA or more in three.39

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

These thresholds were measured at the trunks. During ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block in 39 patients, the median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it. A twitch at 0.2 mA or less was never seen outside the trunk. A twitch between 0.2 and 0.5 mA did not rule out an intraneural tip, and thresholds were higher in patients with diabetes.24

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.25

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root (mean peak 8.2 psi) and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.26

Continuous techniques

After total shoulder arthroplasty, 75 patients were randomised to a continuous interscalene, supraclavicular or suprascapular block, each run at 6 mL an hour of ropivacaine 0.2% with no starting bolus. At 24 hours vital capacity had fallen by a mean of 991 mL with the interscalene catheter and 464 mL with the suprascapular catheter. Pain scores and opioid use did not differ significantly between the groups.36

In 100 patients having arthroscopic shoulder surgery, a suprascapular catheter placed by the surgeon under arthroscopic vision was compared with a superior trunk catheter. Worst pain at 24 hours was higher by a mean of 0.9 points with the suprascapular catheter (95% CI 0.1 to 1.7), so non-inferiority was not shown, but hemidiaphragmatic paresis was significantly less frequent.37

For chronic shoulder pain, a catheter placed in the supraspinous fossa was reported in 30 patients, with one dislodged catheter and one local inflammation.41

Contraindications

  • Do not rely on it as the only anaesthetic. The axillary, lateral pectoral and lower subscapular nerves also supply the joint, and the skin is supplied by other nerves again.8
  • If any loss of diaphragm function would matter, avoid the anterior approach or keep the volume very low. Paralysis followed in 40% with 10 mL by the anterior approach and an axillary nerve block, against 2% by the posterior approach.18
  • If the patient needs the strongest pain relief in the first hours, this is not it. Pain in the recovery room was higher than after interscalene block in pooled analyses.2322

Side effects and complications

Hemidiaphragmatic paresis depends on the approach and the volume. Tap a bar for the detail.

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The trials used different drugs, definitions and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Why the anterior approach reaches the phrenic nerve. In cadavers, dye injected beside the nerve under omohyoid tracked back along the dorsal surface of the plexus to the C5 and C6 roots and stained the phrenic nerve in 41.7% of specimens.17
  • Horner syndrome and hoarseness were much less frequent than after interscalene block: relative risks of 0.17 and 0.24 in a meta-analysis of six trials.22
  • Hand numbness and weakness were rare: no numb hands against 46% after interscalene block,19 and grip unimpaired in 99% against 49%.14
  • Pneumothorax is the classical risk of the posterior landmark technique when the needle is advanced through the notch.40 Keeping the tip on the floor of the fossa is intended to avoid it.3
  • Intravascular injection. The suprascapular artery lies beside the nerve in the fossa and at the notch.310
  • More pain in the first hours than after interscalene block, by about 1.5 points in the recovery room in one meta-analysis and 0.4 in another.2322

Pearls of wisdom

  • Choose the approach for the patient. Anterior: easy to see, good analgesia, but close to the plexus and the phrenic nerve. Posterior: hard to see, kind to the diaphragm, more opioid afterwards.118
  • In the fossa, look for the artery, not the nerve. Colour Doppler finds it under the fascia of supraspinatus, and the nerve is beside it.3
  • The bright line above the bone is fascia. The fluid must spread beneath it, along the floor of the fossa.3
  • Under omohyoid, think small: 1 mL was enough for a selective block in most volunteers, and 5 mL already reached the trunk in cadavers.1316
  • Add the axillary nerve if the block has to carry the analgesia on its own.34
  • Tell the recovery staff to expect more pain early than after an interscalene block, and have a plan for it.23

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Which approach

The two pull in opposite directions. The anterior approach is easier to see and needed less morphine afterwards; the posterior approach protected the diaphragm.118 No trial has yet shown one to be better overall.

How little is enough under omohyoid

Trials have moved from 10 to 15 mL down to 5 mL.14152021 In cadavers even 5 mL reached the phrenic nerve in a fifth to two fifths of injections,1617 so the smallest effective volume is still an open question.

Sparing supraspinatus

Beyond the notch the nerve divides into a medial and a lateral trunk. In a cadaver model, a needle placed midway between the two notches reached the lateral trunk and its joint branches and stayed clear of the medial trunk. This was studied for radiofrequency treatment of chronic pain and needs clinical testing.42

Guidelines

The PROSPECT recommendations for rotator cuff repair, published in 2019, name suprascapular nerve block with or without axillary nerve block as the alternative to interscalene block.48 Several trials of the anterior approach have appeared since the evidence for that guideline was gathered.18192021
