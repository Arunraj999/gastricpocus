Distal nerve blocks of the upper limb: elbow, forearm and wrist

Single nerves blocked where they run alone: the radial nerve above the elbow, the median and ulnar nerves in the forearm, and the small sensory nerves at the elbow and wrist. They rescue an incomplete plexus block, give a hand that can still grip, and carry long-acting analgesia beyond a short plexus block. With distal blocks of the ulnar and median nerves, grip strength fell by a median of 21.4%; after a supraclavicular block it fell by 100%.1

Level of difficulty

Basic to intermediate

The nerves are superficial and each lies in a fixed place. In 11 volunteers the median, ulnar and radial nerves were found in the same position in every forearm but two, each with a vascular variant.5 In an emergency department, attending physicians, fellows and residents performed forearm blocks after a one-hour training session, with a median of 9 minutes per patient.8 The skill lies less in the needling than in choosing the right nerves and level for the operation, and in avoiding the vessels that run with them.

Anatomy

Below the axilla the terminal nerves part company. Each can then be blocked on its own, and the level chosen decides which branches are caught.

Radial nerve. It winds round the back of the humerus in the spiral groove with the profunda brachii vessels. It then pierces the lateral intermuscular septum and lies between brachioradialis and brachialis in the distal arm.4 It divides into the superficial branch and the deep branch (posterior interosseous nerve) close to the elbow: in 20 cadaver arms the division was never more than 2.1 cm from the lateral epicondyle.9 The posterior cutaneous nerve of the forearm leaves the radial nerve above the elbow.10

Superficial radial nerve. It runs under brachioradialis and becomes subcutaneous between the tendons of brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus, a mean of 9.0 cm above the radial styloid. It branches a mean of 5.1 cm above the styloid and most commonly supplies the thumb, index finger and the dorsoradial side of the middle finger.9 In 204 dissected limbs, 88% shared one branching type.11

Median nerve. At the elbow it lies medial to the brachial artery; in one of 22 forearms scanned it lay lateral to it.5 It passes between the heads of pronator teres and runs down the forearm between flexor digitorum superficialis and flexor digitorum profundus. Its palmar cutaneous branch leaves it a mean of 4.56 cm above the distal wrist crease.12

Ulnar nerve. It passes behind the medial epicondyle in the cubital tunnel and enters the forearm between the heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. In the forearm it lies under that muscle, joined by the ulnar artery on its radial side. In one forearm a superficial ulnar artery joined the nerve only at the wrist.5 The dorsal cutaneous branch leaves it a median of 6.8 cm above the tip of the ulnar styloid.13

Lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. The end of the musculocutaneous nerve. It emerges at the lateral border of the biceps tendon in every specimen studied and pierces the deep fascia just below the line between the epicondyles, close to the cephalic vein.1415

Variations that change the block

  • Martin-Gruber anastomosis: fibres cross from the median to the ulnar nerve in the forearm. Pooled prevalence 19.5% across 58 studies and 10,562 limbs, most often arising from the anterior interosseous nerve.16 A 2026 meta-analysis found about 21%, lower in cadaver studies.17
  • Riche-Cannieu anastomosis joins the median and ulnar nerves in the hand, with a pooled prevalence of 55.5%.16 Either connection can leave a muscle or a patch of skin working after one nerve has been blocked.
  • Superficial radial and lateral cutaneous nerves often overlap and communicate on the back of the thumb and wrist: primary communicating branches were found in 73.5% of 102 forearms in one study,18 and a connection in 9.3% of 204 limbs in another, using a different definition.11 Block both for surgery at the base of the thumb.

Surface anatomy

The arm rests supinated on a board. For the radial nerve the probe sits transversely on the lateral side of the distal third of the arm, about two thirds of the way from the midpoint between acromion and lateral epicondyle down to the epicondyle.4 For the median and ulnar nerves it sits transversely on the front of the mid-forearm.5 The lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm is found at the elbow crease lateral to the biceps tendon.14 The superficial radial nerve becomes subcutaneous about 9 cm above the radial styloid.9

Indications

  • Rescue of an incomplete plexus block. In 566 emergency patients given an axillary block, 5% had an incomplete block and were rescued with 2 to 3 mL of prilocaine 1% per missing nerve; all then had surgery without further analgesia.2 A 2024 meta-analysis concluded that additional peripheral nerve blocks are valuable rescue techniques.19
  • Ambulatory hand surgery with a hand that still works. Against a supraclavicular block with the same 15 mL of mepivacaine 1.5%, distal blocks of the median and ulnar nerves preserved grip and patients were more satisfied the next day.1 Selective blocks in the arm left less motor block at the elbow than an axillary block, with no difference in sensory duration.20
  • Prolonging analgesia after a short-acting plexus block. Long-acting distal blocks after a lidocaine or mepivacaine plexus block reduced moderate to severe pain in two randomised trials.321 A series of 125 patients reported an average analgesic duration of nearly 12 hours without prolonged motor block.22
  • Motor-sparing sensory blocks where the surgeon wants active movement, for example tendon surgery. Distal blocks are suited to this in principle; I have found no trial of distal blocks for intraoperative testing of a tendon repair. Wide-awake local anaesthesia without a tourniquet (WALANT) is the alternative studied for that purpose.2324
  • As the main anaesthetic for hand surgery, including carpal tunnel release.2526 In a retrospective comparison with proximal blocks, conversion to general anaesthesia did not differ.7
  • Emergency department procedures on the hand, in adults and children.827
  • When a plexus block is unwanted: the authors of one comparison point to no respiratory compromise and the possibility of bilateral blocks.7

Nerves blocked

Only the nerve or nerves injected. The level decides which branches are included.

  • Radial nerve in the distal arm: both the superficial and the deep branch, as the nerve has not yet divided.4 The posterior cutaneous nerve of the forearm leaves above the elbow and may already have gone.10
  • Superficial radial nerve in the forearm: skin only. Blocking it may spare radial motor function.5
  • Median nerve in the mid-forearm: the nerve and its palmar cutaneous branch, which leaves lower down.12 The branches to the forearm flexors have mostly left above this level.
  • Ulnar nerve in the mid-forearm: the nerve and its dorsal cutaneous branch when the block is above its origin.2913
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm: skin of the lateral forearm, and in many people the back of the thumb base through its overlap with the superficial radial nerve.18

The medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm, the intercostobrachial nerve and the skin of the upper arm are not blocked by any of these. One technique at the elbow added the medial and lateral cutaneous nerves of the forearm for carpal tunnel surgery.26

Landmark technique

The classic wrist block places local anaesthetic at the wrist crease: the median nerve between the tendons of palmaris longus and flexor carpi radialis, the ulnar nerve under the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris beside the ulnar artery, and a subcutaneous ring over the radial styloid for the superficial radial nerve. Elbow blocks by landmark target the median nerve medial to the brachial artery and the radial nerve lateral to the biceps tendon. These descriptions are standard and are not drawn from one study.

The wrist is low enough to miss branches that have already left: the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve (mean 4.56 cm above the crease) and the dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve (median 6.8 cm above the styloid).1213 Subcutaneous infiltration is needed to catch them.

RCT

Forearm ultrasound against the landmark wrist block

In 12 volunteers given 18 paired blocks with 3 mL of lidocaine 1%, 14 of 18 ultrasound-guided forearm blocks worked at 15 minutes, against 10 of 18 landmark wrist blocks: a difference of 22%.30

RCT

In small children

In 100 children aged 1 to 3 years having trigger thumb release, the median nerve block failed in 26% with a landmark block at the wrist and in none with ultrasound in the lower forearm. Additional sufentanil and propofol were needed less often.28

Radial nerve above the elbow

  1. Place the probe transversely on the lateral distal arm. In 50 volunteers the nerve was rarely visible high in the arm and became visible in 93% of arms by one third of the way down from the midpoint between acromion and epicondyle.4
  2. Find it in the spiral groove, close to the profunda brachii artery and vein, at a mean depth of 1.21 cm.4
  3. Slide distally to the point between brachioradialis and brachialis, two thirds of the way down. Here the nerve has passed through the lateral intermuscular septum, lies at a mean depth of 1.71 cm and is oval, with a cross-sectional area of 2.9 mm2. The authors judged this the better site because the vessels are further away.4
  4. Expect it deeper in larger arms: depth correlated with body mass index and arm circumference.4
  5. At the elbow the radial nerve is easy to see,5 but it divides within about 2 cm of the lateral epicondyle,9 so a block here should be placed before the division or include both branches.

Median nerve at the elbow and in the mid-forearm

  1. At the elbow, find the brachial artery in the antecubital fossa. The median nerve is the honeycomb structure medial to it, though it lay lateral in one of 22 forearms.5
  2. Follow it into the mid-forearm. It lies in the plane between flexor digitorum superficialis above and flexor digitorum profundus below. The mid-forearm combines a clear view, all the terminal branches and little risk to vessels.5
  3. Stay above the palmar cutaneous branch, which leaves a mean of 4.56 cm above the distal wrist crease.12
  4. Use colour Doppler to check for an artery running with the nerve before injecting.

Ulnar nerve in the mid-forearm, not at the elbow

  1. Find the ulnar artery on the medial side of the front of the forearm. The ulnar nerve lies on its ulnar side, under flexor carpi ulnaris.5
  2. Choose the level where nerve and artery are apart. The first description blocked the nerve about 15 cm above the ulnar styloid, and the block included the dorsal cutaneous branch.29
  3. Stay above the dorsal cutaneous branch, which leaves a median of 6.8 cm above the ulnar styloid.13
  4. Expect a small nerve: its mean cross-sectional area in the proximal forearm was 6.2 mm2.6
Why not the cubital tunnel

Behind the medial epicondyle the ulnar nerve lies in a tight fibro-osseous tunnel with nowhere for fluid to go, and this is a common site of ulnar neuropathy. Standard teaching is to avoid injecting there and to block the nerve in the arm or in the forearm instead, where it lies in muscle and can be surrounded. I found no trial of ulnar nerve block in the cubital tunnel. The ASRA practice advisory addresses blocks in patients with pre-existing neurological disease.31 Selective blocks of the ulnar nerve in the upper arm have been studied.20

Superficial radial nerve and the wrist

  1. Follow the radial artery on the lateral side of the forearm. In the mid-forearm the superficial radial nerve lies beside it under brachioradialis.
  2. Or block it where it becomes subcutaneous, a mean of 9.0 cm above the radial styloid, before it branches about 5 cm above the styloid.9
  3. Blocking the superficial branch may spare radial motor function.5
  4. At the wrist itself use the landmark block described above. Forearm blocks under ultrasound worked more often than landmark wrist blocks in volunteers.30

Lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm

  1. Find the biceps tendon at the elbow crease. The nerve emerges at its lateral border.1415
  2. Look beside the cephalic vein. At the line between the epicondyles the nerve ran within 1 cm medial to the vein in 78.7% of 96 limbs, and pierced the deep fascia below that line in 84.4%.14
  3. Expect one or two branches. A single trunk was found in 78.1% in one study;14 two branches at the level of the elbow joint in 82.1% in another, measured at a different level.15 A subcutaneous fan lateral to the tendon catches both.

Dose-finding

How little is enough

Mepivacaine 1%, ulnar nerve in the proximal forearm: the estimated dose for 95% of volunteers was 0.11 mL per mm2 of nerve cross-section, a mean of 0.7 mL.6 In the axilla, 2.3 to 3.4 mL of lidocaine 1.5% surrounded each terminal nerve.32

RCT, dose-finding

Ultrasound halves the median nerve volume

For median and ulnar blocks in the arm before carpal tunnel release, the volume of mepivacaine 1.5% that worked in half of patients was 2 mL for the median nerve with ultrasound against 4 mL with nerve stimulation. For the ulnar nerve it was 2 and 2.4 mL. Smaller volumes gave a shorter block but not a slower onset.33

RCT

Volumes in the trials

4 mL of mepivacaine 1.5% per nerve at the wrist;25 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.75% per nerve in the mid-forearm;3 5 mL of levobupivacaine 0.125% on the radial and median nerves at the elbow.21

RCT

Ultrasound or stimulator at the wrist

In 60 patients having endoscopic carpal tunnel release, ultrasound-guided median and ulnar blocks were quicker to perform (median 55 against 100 seconds for the median nerve) but slower in onset. Success was 93% in both groups.25

Needle path and nerve stimulator

Steer the needle yourself. The panel names each layer the tip crosses and warns when it nears an artery or enters the nerve. Switch on the nerve stimulator to see which muscles answer, then inject and watch where the fluid goes. Or press Play the block to watch the whole sequence first.

Radial nerve above the elbow

In plane from the lateral end of the probe, through brachioradialis to the plane where the nerve lies against brachialis. Stimulation brings wrist and finger extension. The aim is a ring of fluid round the nerve in the plane between the two muscles.

Median nerve in the mid-forearm

In plane through flexor digitorum superficialis to the plane beneath it. Stimulation brings thumb opposition and flexion of the index and middle fingers. Fluid should spread along the plane between the two flexors and lift the nerve.

Ulnar nerve in the mid-forearm

In plane from the ulnar end of the probe, so the tip reaches the nerve before the artery. Stimulation brings finger spreading and thumb adduction. Fluid should surround the nerve under flexor carpi ulnaris, not only the artery.

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.34

RCT

The stimulator speeds onset at the wrist

Complete sensory block of the median nerve took a median of 254 seconds after nerve stimulation and 370 seconds after ultrasound guidance, with the same success.25

Continuous techniques

In 22 hands after emergency surgery for severe trauma, catheters were placed beside the nerves in the distal forearm through a 2 to 3 cm incision and ropivacaine 0.2% was run at 2 mL per hour for 7 to 21 days. Pain scores stayed low, few rescue analgesics were needed and the extrinsic hand muscles kept working; one superficial infection settled when the catheter was removed.35 A single case report describes an ultrasound-guided median nerve catheter in the proximal forearm run for 11 days to allow hand therapy, with active finger flexion preserved.36

Contraindications

  • Surgery the distal blocks cannot cover on their own: an upper-arm tourniquet or the elbow. For bone surgery at the wrist they supplement a plexus block; pain control after wrist surgery was suboptimal in one series.22
  • Injection in the cubital tunnel, and particular care in a patient with an existing ulnar neuropathy.31
  • An artery that cannot be separated from the nerve on colour Doppler at the chosen level; move up or down the forearm.5

Side effects and complications

  • Nerve injury. No large series reports the risk for forearm blocks specifically. Across peripheral nerve blocks in general, neuropathy after axillary block was estimated at 1.48 per 100, and only one permanent neuropathy was reported among 16 studies.37 No complications were found at 3 months after forearm blocks in the emergency department,8 nor at one year in children.27
  • The ulnar nerve at the elbow. See the box under the ulnar nerve: inject in the arm or the forearm, not in the cubital tunnel.
  • Arterial puncture. The radial and ulnar arteries run beside the nerves, and a superficial ulnar artery can run apart from the nerve until the wrist.5 No vascular puncture was seen in a paediatric series.27
  • Incomplete block from overlap and anastomoses between nerves.1618 Pain control was suboptimal after some painful operations, including wrist surgery, trapeziometacarpal arthrodesis and finger amputation.22
  • Prolonged numbness of the arm was not reported after distal analgesic blocks in 125 patients.22

Pearls of wisdom

  • Map the operation first: incision, bone, joint and tourniquet. Block only what is needed.
  • Block the median and ulnar nerves in the mid-forearm, above their palmar and dorsal branches.1213
  • Block the radial nerve between brachialis and brachioradialis, before it divides.49
  • Never inject the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel.
  • Colour Doppler first: the radial and ulnar arteries are the structures to avoid.5
  • For surgery at the base of the thumb, add the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm.18
  • A short-acting plexus block for surgery plus long-acting distal blocks for analgesia gave less pain after surgery without prolonged motor block.32122

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Distal blocks as the whole anaesthetic

The motor-sparing advantage is clear,120 but the trials are small and a 2024 meta-analysis found no randomised comparison with axillary block.19 A large trial of surgical success is still needed.

Short-acting plexus, long-acting distal

Two trials and a large series support it for analgesia.32122 The best drug, concentration and nerves for each operation are not settled, and the most painful wrist operations were less well controlled.22

Distal blocks or WALANT

WALANT has matched axillary block for carpal tunnel release and is studied for tendon repair.3823 No trial has compared it with distal nerve blocks, which also leave the hand moving.

How small a volume

Under 1 mL blocked the ulnar nerve in volunteers,6 but smaller volumes shorten the block.33 For analgesia, volume and duration have to be balanced.
