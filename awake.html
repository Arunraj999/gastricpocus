Awake surgery under regional anaesthesia

Operating on a patient who is awake asks more of the block, the team and the conversation beforehand. This page sets out the caveats and the points worth remembering.

Caveats

What the evidence asks you to plan for

Plan B

The planned technique is not always delivered

In a trial of 1,600 patients having hip fracture surgery, 83.8% of those assigned to spinal anaesthesia received it. Agree the alternative before you start.1

Outcome

Awake is a choice, not a guarantee

In the same trial spinal anaesthesia was not superior to general anaesthesia for survival and walking at 60 days, and delirium was similar. Offer awake surgery for the patient's preference and specific risks.1

Conversion

Some patients will need more

In a comparison of 150 hand surgery patients, sedation was given to 12 of 50 with an infraclavicular block and 5 of 50 with wide-awake local anaesthesia; 6 and 4 were converted to general anaesthesia.2

Breathing

The awake patient feels the phrenic nerve

Landmark interscalene block caused hemidiaphragmatic paresis in every patient studied. Consider this before awake shoulder surgery in anyone with limited respiratory reserve.3

Pearls to remember

Practice points

Faculty practice points. They reflect experience and are not drawn from trials.

Before

The conversation

  • Describe what the patient will feel: pressure, pulling and movement, without pain.
  • Agree in advance what happens if the block is not enough.
  • Ask about lying flat, claustrophobia, cough and restless legs.
Block

Anaesthesia, not analgesia

  • Map the incision, the deep structures and the tourniquet to the nerves that supply them.
  • Allow enough time for onset, and test in the surgical field before draping.
  • Carry out Prep, Stop, Block immediately before the needle.
During

The awake theatre

  • Tell the whole team at the brief that the patient is awake.
  • Position for comfort first; pad pressure points on the side that is not blocked.
  • Offer music or headphones, and keep talking to the patient.
  • Keep sedation light enough to hold a conversation.
Tourniquet

Plan for the cuff

  • Know the expected tourniquet time before choosing the block.
  • Check that the skin under the cuff is covered.
Rescue

If the patient feels pain

  • Stop the surgery and find out where and what.
  • Options in order: surgical infiltration, a supplementary block, then general anaesthesia.
  • Sedation does not rescue a failing block.
After

Follow-up

  • Give written advice on protecting the numb limb and starting painkillers early.
  • Ask about the experience; it is the outcome that matters to the patient.
