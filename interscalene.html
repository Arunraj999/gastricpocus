Interscalene brachial plexus block

Local anaesthetic placed around the C5 and C6 roots between the anterior and middle scalene muscles. The reference block for shoulder surgery, and the one most likely to reach the phrenic nerve.111

Level of difficulty

Core block

The interscalene block is one of the small set of basic blocks proposed as core skills for every anaesthetist.13 It is still an invasive procedure with potentially serious complications, and should be performed by practitioners with appropriate experience.15

Anatomy

Winnie described the interscalene approach to the brachial plexus in 1970.3 The C5, C6 and C7 roots lie stacked between the anterior and middle scalene muscles, beneath the lateral edge of sternocleidomastoid. The phrenic nerve runs on the anterior scalene close to the C5 root, which is the anatomical reason the diaphragm is so often affected.12 The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves leave the upper roots and pass through the middle scalene.8

Surface anatomy

The interscalene groove is felt behind the lateral border of sternocleidomastoid at the level of the cricoid cartilage.3

Indications

  • Analgesia for shoulder surgery. Recommended for rotator cuff repair alongside paracetamol, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug and dexamethasone, with opioids for rescue.6
  • Surgical anaesthesia. In a randomised trial of 160 patients having trauma surgery to the upper arm, surgical anaesthesia was achieved in 99% with ultrasound guidance and 91% with nerve stimulation.7
  • Arthroscopic shoulder surgery under block alone has been reported with as little as 5 mL of ropivacaine 0.75%.9

Nerves blocked

The interscalene approach blocks the caudad part of the cervical plexus and the cephalad part of the brachial plexus preferentially, so the choice of approach should follow the site of surgery.16 Blocks adequate for shoulder surgery produce sensory anaesthesia of C3 to C5, which is why the phrenic nerve is involved.1

Caveats

  • Phrenic nerve. Hemidiaphragmatic paresis is the characteristic side effect; consider it before blocking anyone with limited respiratory reserve.1
  • Not a block for the forearm or hand. The lower roots are the least reliably covered.16
  • Nerves in the needle path. The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves pass through the middle scalene.8

Landmark technique

The original description relied on palpation of the groove and paraesthesia.3 With that technique and 34 to 52 mL of mepivacaine, every patient studied developed hemidiaphragmatic paresis.1 With a nerve stimulator set to 0.5 mA, 2 Hz and 0.1 ms and 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.75%, surgical anaesthesia was achieved in 91%.7

Descriptive

Nerves in the needle path

A nerve was visible in or on the middle scalene in 90% of 50 patients, at about the depth of the C6 root (1.1 cm from skin) and 0.7 cm posterior to the plexus. Stimulation identified it as the dorsal scapular nerve in 77% and the long thoracic nerve in 23%.8

RCT

Where to place the injectate

20 mL placed 4 mm lateral to the sheath gave hemidiaphragmatic paresis in 21%, against 90% when placed between C5 and C6, with similar analgesia.10

RCT

How much to inject

5 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% gave the same pain scores, sleep quality and 24-hour morphine use as 20 mL, with fewer respiratory effects.2

RCT

Guidance

Ultrasound guidance achieved surgical anaesthesia in 99%, against 91% with nerve stimulation alone.7

Observational

What 0.2 mA tells you

During ultrasound-guided supraclavicular block in 39 patients, the median lowest current that produced a twitch was 0.60 mA with the needle outside the trunk and 0.30 mA inside it. A twitch at 0.2 mA or less was never seen outside the trunk. A twitch between 0.2 and 0.5 mA did not rule out an intraneural tip, and thresholds were higher in patients with diabetes.22

Observational

A silent stimulator is not proof of distance

With needle-to-nerve contact confirmed on ultrasound during axillary block in 102 patients, a motor response at 0.5 mA or less was present in 74.5% and paraesthesia in 38.2%.23

Observational

Injection pressure as a second monitor

At the interscalene roots, opening injection pressure stayed below 15 psi with the tip 1 mm from the root (mean peak 8.2 psi) and reached 15 psi or more in 35 of 36 injections with the needle touching the root.24

Consensus

Structures to identify

For the block view, an international Delphi consensus strongly recommends identifying the anterior scalene, the middle scalene and the C5 and C6 roots, with a weaker recommendation for sternocleidomastoid, the C7 root and the upper trunk. Orientation scanning adds the upper trunk as a core structure.25

Continuous techniques

In a randomised, double-blind trial, patients sent home with an interscalene catheter and a portable pump delivering ropivacaine 0.2% had a median rest pain score of 0 on the first postoperative day, against 4.8 with saline. Opioid use, opioid side effects and sleep disturbance were all lower.14

A critical review concluded that a continuous interscalene block with a basal infusion and patient-controlled boluses is the most effective analgesic technique after both major and minor shoulder surgery, and that single-injection blocks are limited by their duration.15

Contraindications

  • Phrenic nerve block limits the use of this block in patients with reduced respiratory reserve, and it may be contraindicated in those with pulmonary disease.211
  • Choose the approach for the individual patient, taking account of comorbidity and the type of surgery.12

The trials used different drugs, definitions of paresis and assessment times, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Hoarseness was less frequent after superior trunk block than after interscalene block.5
  • Dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves lie in the path of a posterior in-plane needle in most patients.8

Pearls of wisdom

  • Scan the middle scalene for small nerves before you choose the needle path, and pass above or below any you find.8
  • A small volume does the same analgesic work as a large one.29
  • Deposit the local anaesthetic lateral to the sheath and away from the anterior scalene.1012
  • For a patient with limited respiratory reserve, consider a superior trunk or suprascapular block before modifying the interscalene block.45

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Moving the injection away from the roots

A 2017 review found that no single modification of the interscalene block had brought phrenic nerve block below 20%.11 Two years later the superior trunk block reported 4.8% in a randomised trial.5

Still to be confirmed

Posterolateral supraclavicular injection, C7 root block and combined infraclavicular and suprascapular blocks were proposed as diaphragm-sparing options that needed confirmatory trials.11
