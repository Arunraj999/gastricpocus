Shoulder pericapsular nerve group (SPENG) block

Local anaesthetic placed at the front of the shoulder joint: in the plane between deltoid and the subscapularis tendon over the humeral head, and in some versions also deep to subscapularis on the joint capsule.12 The aim is the articular branches that supply the anterior capsule, mainly from the axillary and subscapular nerves, rather than the brachial plexus itself.3 Small trials suggest it spares the diaphragm.45 Whether it relieves pain as well as an interscalene block is not settled: of four small trials, two found pain similar, one found it better and one found it clearly worse.67

Main use
Analgesia after shoulder arthroscopy; manipulation of the frozen shoulder48
Target
Deltoid–subscapularis plane, with or without the pericapsular space deep to subscapularis12

Level of difficulty

Intermediate block

The view is a standard anterior shoulder scan of the coracoid and the subscapularis tendon, and its authors judged it quick to learn.2 In a trial against the suprascapular nerve block, it took less time to perform and hurt less during the procedure.9 In an emergency department it was performed by an intern under supervision.10 Two things make it harder than it looks. The subscapularis tendon is tough, and one group found the needle could not be pushed through it.1 And the published versions differ in where the local anaesthetic goes, so you need to know which one you are doing and why.21

What it is called. The literature uses several names for overlapping techniques: shoulder PENG block, pericapsular nerve block, and shoulder anterior capsular (SHAC) block.452 The 2024 ASRA–ESRA consensus on limb block names recommends naming a block by the nerve and the site where it is blocked. It did not name any shoulder pericapsular block: its PENG block is the hip block, between the psoas tendon and the pubic ramus, and a generic “shoulder block” was excluded in the first round.11 This page uses SPENG for the anterior technique. Be aware that a 2026 paper used the same abbreviation for a different, posterior pericapsular injection aimed at the suprascapular and axillary nerves.12

Anatomy

Who supplies the glenohumeral joint. In 15 dissected shoulders the pattern was constant. The suprascapular nerve supplied the posterosuperior quadrant, the posterior division of the axillary nerve the posteroinferior quadrant, the superior nerve to subscapularis the anterosuperior quadrant, and the main trunk of the axillary nerve the anteroinferior quadrant. The lateral pectoral nerve and the posterior cord contributed less often.3 A review of 30 anatomical papers named the suprascapular, lateral pectoral, axillary and lower subscapular nerves as the parent nerves. It found the densest nociceptors in the subacromial bursa.13 In 25 cadavers the subacromial bursa, the acromioclavicular joint and the coracoclavicular ligaments had a dual supply from the suprascapular and lateral pectoral nerves in every specimen.14 On histology the upper and anterior halves of the capsule were more richly innervated than the lower and posterior halves.15

What this means for an anterior injection. The front of the capsule, supplied by the axillary and subscapular branches, is the target. The suprascapular nerve supplies the back and top of the joint and the subacromial region.313 An anterior injection was not designed to reach it, and some authors add a suprascapular block when that territory matters.2

Two planes, two versions. The superficial plane lies between the deep layer of the deltoid fascia and the superficial layer of the subscapularis fascia, in front of the myotendinous junction of subscapularis.2 Cadaver dye placed from the front along the first part of the axillary nerve stained the axillary nerve well and reached the musculocutaneous nerve as well.16 Interfascial injection alone stains the axillary nerve but does not reach the capsule, according to the authors of one trial, who cite this cadaver work.7 The deep plane lies under subscapularis, against the capsule. The shoulder version was first evaluated in a cadaveric radiological study of this deep injection.174 As later summarised, 10 mL of dye was placed deep to subscapularis in 10 cadavers and reached the capsule on fluoroscopy and dissection,18 with spread over the whole capsule and partly into the axilla.7 In 11 injected shoulders and 35 more dissected, an injection under subscapularis spread over the anterior, inferior and posterodorsal walls of the capsule, the subacromial bursa and the bicipital groove. In some it entered the joint. More than 40% of specimens had at least one opening (the foramen of Weitbrecht or Rouvière) between the subscapular bursa and the joint. Lower volumes made accidental intra-articular injection less likely.19

The SHAC block uses both planes, the superficial one first, then the needle crosses subscapularis to the pericapsular space.2 Several reports labelled “PENG” injected only in the superficial plane.145 A correspondence has argued that the shoulder PENG may not be a true pericapsular block.20

Surface anatomy

Most reports place the patient supine with the arm abducted to about 45 degrees and externally rotated.175 The SHAC authors used a beach-chair position; external rotation and abduction move coracobrachialis and biceps out of the way.2 A neutral arm position has also been described, for manipulation of the shoulder.21 A high-frequency linear probe sits on the front of the shoulder, along the line between the coracoid process and the humeral head.118 The needle enters in plane through deltoid; one group advanced it from cranial to caudal.18

Indications

  • Analgesia after shoulder arthroscopy, under general anaesthesia and as part of multimodal analgesia, particularly when sparing the diaphragm matters.4522
  • Manipulation under anaesthesia and early rehabilitation of the frozen shoulder.818
  • Emergency department analgesia for shoulder dislocation and proximal humeral fracture. Evidence is limited to five patients in two case series.2310
  • Acute shoulder pain outside surgery, for example a vaso-occlusive crisis in sickle cell disease (single case).24
  • Part of an anaesthetic for proximal humeral fixation when the interscalene block is undesirable, accepting that it was not enough on its own.1

Nerves blocked

The intended targets are the articular branches to the anterior capsule: the main trunk of the axillary nerve (anteroinferior quadrant) and the superior nerve to subscapularis (anterosuperior quadrant), with a variable contribution from the lateral pectoral nerve.3 Deep infiltration towards subscapularis was proposed to cover the axillary and subscapular branches.1 In cadavers, an anterior injection along the axillary nerve stained that nerve and reached the musculocutaneous nerve.16

Not targeted: the suprascapular nerve, which supplies the posterosuperior capsule and, with the lateral pectoral nerve, the subacromial bursa and acromioclavicular joint.314 Deep cadaver injections did spread to the posterodorsal capsule and the subacromial bursa,19 but no clinical study has shown that the suprascapular territory is reliably covered. The skin over the shoulder is not covered either; one trial added a superficial cervical plexus block,5 and in a case of open fixation the patient felt the incision.1

What the block covers

The front of the joint capsule. The back of the joint and the skin need other blocks.

Landmark technique

There is no landmark version. Every published description uses ultrasound to find the plane between deltoid and the subscapularis tendon.12 This page does not describe a landmark technique.

Ultrasound technique

  1. Position the arm: abducted about 45 degrees and externally rotated.17
  2. Place a linear probe along the line between the coracoid process and the humeral head.15
  3. Name the layers: deltoid on top, the subscapularis tendon over the round humeral head, and the coracoid at the medial end. The myotendinous junction of subscapularis has a mixed, dotted echo pattern.2
  4. Run colour Doppler over the planned path before needling.
  5. Advance the needle in plane through deltoid until the tip lies between deltoid and the subscapularis tendon. Confirm the plane with a small volume of saline.1824
  6. Superficial version: inject in this plane and watch it open.14 SHAC version: after the superficial injection, pass through subscapularis to the pericapsular space beside the humeral head and inject a smaller volume.27

Anatomical relationships follow standard descriptions. Statements drawn from a specific study carry a reference number.

RCT

Which plane did the trials use?

Superficial plane only: 15 mL against an interscalene block,4 and 10 mL with a superficial cervical plexus block against an interscalene block.5 Both planes (SHAC): 15 mL superficial plus 5 mL deep, and its authors thought the 5 mL may have been too little for the capsule.7 The other trials read for this page do not state the plane in the parts available.9228

Cadaver

Deep injection spreads widely

Injectate under subscapularis reached the anterior, inferior and posterodorsal capsule, the subacromial bursa and the bicipital groove, and sometimes the joint. More than 40% of shoulders had a communication between the subscapular bursa and the joint.19

Cadaver

An anterior approach to the axillary nerve

Dye injected from the front along the first part of the axillary nerve spread well on the nerve and reached the musculocutaneous nerve. The nerve kept a constant relation to subscapularis.16

Debate

Is it really pericapsular?

A correspondence argued that the shoulder PENG may not be a true pericapsular block.20 A 2026 cadaver letter examined a 10 mL injection in the neighbouring plane between coracobrachialis and subscapularis.26 Clinical evidence comparing the planes is lacking.

Needle path and nerve stimulator

The needle passes through skin, subcutaneous tissue and deltoid to the plane on the subscapularis tendon; for the SHAC version it continues through subscapularis towards the humeral head.2 There is no nerve to find, so stimulation has no defined end point. None of the full-text clinical reports read for this page used a nerve stimulator.

Case report

A hard tendon

When the needle touched the subscapularis tendon it felt like bone and would not advance further. The injection was made between deltoid and the tendon.1

RCT

Paraesthesia in the arm

Reported in 3 of 25 patients (12%) after the shoulder PENG against 9 of 25 (36%) after interscalene block.4

Case report

Spread to the plexus

After a related injection, the subscapularis plane block, one patient could not flex the index and middle fingers and had palm numbness, which resolved within two hours.25

Cadaver

Into the joint

A deep injection can enter the joint through gaps between the glenohumeral ligaments; lower volumes made this less likely.19

Volumes studied. These are the volumes used in the cited studies, not a recommendation: 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.25%;5 15 mL of bupivacaine 0.25%;4 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.25%;24 10 to 15 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% with dexamethasone 8 mg;2 15 mL plus 5 mL of bupivacaine 0.5%;7 15 to 20 mL of bupivacaine 0.5%;1 10 mL of bupivacaine 0.5% with 10 mL of lidocaine 2%;8 and 20 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% with betamethasone 6 mg.18

Dose safety. Calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic from lean body weight, and count every other dose given, including wound or port infiltration and any second block. Dose per weight and patient weight both predicted toxicity in a registry of 25,336 blocks.27 If toxicity is suspected, stop injecting, call for help, maintain the airway and give 100% oxygen, treat seizures, and give 20% lipid emulsion following the Association of Anaesthetists guideline or the ASRA advisory.28

Continuous techniques

No study of a catheter for an anterior pericapsular shoulder block was found for this page. A posterior interfascial approach between infraspinatus and teres minor has been used with a catheter in a single case.29

Contraindications

  • Patient refusal, infection at the site, or allergy to local anaesthetic.
  • Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs: judge the depth and compressibility of the site; the ESAIC/ESRA guideline asks for set intervals for deep, non-compressible blocks and for bleeding risk to be considered for every block.30
  • Open surgery with the block as the only anaesthetic: it was not enough on its own for open fixation of the proximal humerus.1
  • An unexamined axillary nerve after dislocation or fracture: document deltoid sensation and power first. The emergency department authors chose this block partly because it preserved post-reduction assessment of the axillary nerve.10
  • Severe respiratory disease: not a contraindication, but diaphragm sparing rests on two small trials, one assessed only clinically.45

These are standard clinical considerations rather than findings of a specific study, unless referenced.

Side effects and complications

Tap a bar to see the study detail.

The two trials used different interscalene volumes and different methods of diagnosis, so compare within a trial and not across them.

  • Few events, few patients. No complications were recorded over 24 hours in either group of a frozen shoulder trial of 127 patients, 64 of whom received the block,8 and no adverse event or motor block in a series of 75.2 These numbers are far too small to estimate rare harms.
  • Spread to the brachial plexus. Arm paraesthesia followed 12% of blocks in one trial,4 and a transient median nerve palsy followed a related subscapularis plane block.25
  • Intra-articular injection is possible with the deep injection, through gaps between the glenohumeral ligaments.19
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. In an Australasian registry of 25,336 peripheral nerve blocks, the incidence was 0.87 per 1000, and the site of injection was one of the predictors.27 This figure covers all peripheral blocks, not this one. Management follows the ASRA advisory and the Association of Anaesthetists guideline, including 20% lipid emulsion.28
  • Bleeding. No haematoma was reported in either group of a trial of 127 patients (64 received the block).8 Use colour Doppler before needling.

Pearls of wisdom

  • Decide which version you are doing. Most trials called “PENG” injected only between deltoid and the subscapularis tendon; the SHAC block adds a deep injection on the capsule.42
  • The back of the joint belongs to the suprascapular nerve. If that territory matters, add a suprascapular block.32
  • The skin is not covered. Add a superficial cervical plexus block or ask the surgeon to infiltrate the ports or incision.51
  • Do not rely on it alone for open surgery; it gave neither full anaesthesia nor muscle relaxation.1
  • For the deep injection, keep the volume low to limit entry into the joint.19
  • “Diaphragm-sparing” is promising, not proven. If it matters, scan the diaphragm before and after.45
  • After a dislocation or fracture, examine and record axillary nerve function before you block.10
  • Expect firm resistance at the subscapularis tendon; do not force the needle.1

The PROSPECT guideline for rotator cuff repair recommends an interscalene block or a suprascapular block with or without an axillary nerve block; it predates the shoulder pericapsular blocks.40 Reviews of diaphragm-sparing options set the bar these blocks must clear: no single change to the interscalene block brings phrenic block below 20%,41 and limiting volume and moving away from the C5–C6 roots matter most.42

Evolving evidence

Watch this space Where practice is moving, and what has not yet been settled.

Does it match an interscalene block?

Four small trials disagree. Two found equivalent pain,46 one found it better when combined with a cervical plexus block against a low-volume interscalene block,5 and one found it clearly worse after rotator cuff repair.7 No meta-analysis exists. The answer may depend on the plane, the volume and the operation.

Proof of diaphragm sparing

The only ultrasound evidence comes from a trial that added a cervical plexus block,5 and the other trial assessed the diaphragm clinically.4 Pericapsular blocks were not among the techniques in the 2025 meta-analysis of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis.35 A trial against the superior trunk block with ultrasound assessment is the obvious next step.

Frozen shoulder and the emergency department

The largest trial is in frozen shoulder manipulation (127 patients),8 supported by a cohort study of rehabilitation.18 Emergency use rests on five patients so far.2310

New planes, same name

A deep plane to subscapularis approach was described in 2019,43 a coracobrachialis–subscapularis plane was studied in cadavers in 2026,26 and a posterior pericapsular technique was published in 2026 under the name SPENG.12 Check which technique a paper means before comparing results.
